Winter is the season when dust control matters most, and also the season when the equipment delivering it is least reliable. Cold air holds very little moisture, the wind is stronger, and in heating season the surfaces that were damp all summer are dry. The visible plume at a transfer point travels further and stays airborne longer, so the months with the highest dust load are exactly the months when a machine that stands idle overnight is most likely to be out of service.

Water is the working medium of a fog cannon, and water is what freezes. That combination makes winter the real test of a dust suppression programme — not the specification sheet, not the maximum throw distance, but what happens on a night when the machine stops at 18:00 and the temperature keeps falling until 06:00.

## Freezing Is a Pressure Event, Not a Slow Process

The common mental model is that water gradually thickens, then freezes, and that the damage is caused by something enormous. In reality the damage is caused by a small force applied with nowhere to go.

Water expands by about nine per cent in volume when it turns to ice. Nine per cent does not sound like much until it is confined. Inside a closed valve body, a solenoid seat, a gauge tapping or a pump volute, the expansion has no free surface to grow into, and the pressure that results is high enough to split cast iron and swell copper tube. Nothing about the process is gradual at that point; the component simply fails.

This is why the damage from a cold night is so often discovered weeks later. A hairline crack in a pump casing or a stress fracture at a manifold weld does not announce itself in the dark. It appears on the first warm morning when the machine is pressurised again, as a drip, a mist, or a spray pattern that never returns to what it was.

The second thing to understand is when the failure happens. It almost never happens while the machine is spraying. Moving water has energy in it and resists nucleation; water that has stopped is what freezes. A fog cannon therefore has two completely different risk profiles — one while it is running, one while it stands — and the second is the dangerous one.

## The Risk Window Opens Well Above Zero

The temperature on the yard thermometer is not the temperature of the machine. On a clear, calm night, exposed metal and plastic surfaces radiate heat to the sky and settle several degrees below the air temperature, which is why frost forms on car roofs and steel structures while the forecast still reads plus three. Wind makes it worse on the windward side, and thin-walled parts — a nozzle ring, an exposed manifold, a filter bowl — lose heat fastest because they have little thermal mass to give back.

The practical consequence is that the freeze risk window opens well before the thermometer reaches zero, and the first genuine warning of the season is the first frost, not the first hard freeze. Winterising a fog cannon in response to a hard freeze warning means winterising it after the first damage has already been done in a marginal cavity somewhere.

What matters more than the minimum temperature is idle time. A machine that sprays continuously through a shift and stands full for eighteen hours is in a different situation from one that runs around the clock, or one that is properly emptied at the end of each shift. Two identical cannons on the same site, in the same weather, will have entirely different winters depending on that single operational decision.

## Decide Which Winter You Are Running

There are only two honest options for a water-based machine in winter, and most sites drift between them, which is how damage happens.

**Keep it moving.** If the site genuinely needs dust suppression through the cold months, continuous circulation is the strategy: the pump runs, the water keeps moving, and no line is allowed to stand full. This is a real operating commitment, not a switch. Every stop longer than a shift becomes a drain-down event, and the nozzle orifices are the first place a thin film will freeze, which shows up as a distorted pattern before it shows up as a fault. Sites that run this way also have to think about ice on the ground: overspray that lands on haul roads, corners and walkways in sub-zero temperatures creates a slip and vehicle hazard that is entirely of your own making, so spray timing and direction have to account for traffic routes, not just dust sources.

**Drain it and keep it dry.** For intermittent use — which is most sites — the correct strategy is drain-down storage: the machine should not be holding water when it is not spraying. This is not a compromise; for a once-through spray circuit it is the only method that removes the failure mode rather than managing it.

The decisive rule of the whole subject is short enough to write on a shift board: **a cannon that sprays for six hours and stands for eighteen is a wet machine, not a winterised one.** The question is never how cold it gets. It is how long the machine stands full.

## The Shutdown Routine That Actually Solves It

A drain-down that is done half-heartedly is worse than none, because it leaves the operator with a false sense of security. The routine that works has a fixed order.

First, stop and empty under gravity. Open the lowest drain point and let the bulk of the water out before touching anything else. If the machine has no drain at its true low point, then the ice will use that low point as its drain — which is the design lesson worth taking away for the next purchase.

Second, open every branch. Nozzle ring, manifold ends, gauge lines, filter bowl, strainer housing, hose reel, any spare outlet. Water hides in the branches that nobody uses, and an unused branch with a closed valve is a sealed cavity waiting for the first cold night.

Third, blow out. Dry compressed air at moderate pressure through the highest point, with all outlets open, until nothing but air comes out of the lowest one. This is the step that turns a "drained" machine into a dry one, and it is the step that gets skipped when the shift is running late.

Fourth, leave the valves mid-open, not shut. A closed valve is a pressure vessel; a mid-open valve is not.

Fifth, cap the suction and dry the strainer bowl. Sediment holds water even when the pipe above it looks empty.

## Where a Frozen Cannon Breaks First

Freeze damage is not random. It follows the water path, and it concentrates wherever water is trapped in a small volume, in a closed body, or in a section with no drain.

![Water path diagram ranking freeze vulnerability from pump casing and nozzle orifices through valves, dead legs and the reservoir](/images/fog-cannon-winter-damage-priority-map.svg)

Two mechanical rules set the order. The first is that the smallest trapped volume freezes first: a nozzle orifice, a gauge tapping or a closed solenoid seat holds a few millilitres, there is no room for expansion, and the pressure arrives almost instantly. The second is that anything that can be isolated will hold water: closing a valve turns an ordinary pipe run into a pressure vessel, so the more isolation points a circuit has, the more places water can be locked into when the pump stops.

There is a maintenance consequence that is easy to miss. A frost-damaged cannon rarely presents as "the cannon is broken". It presents as one section of the spray ring running dry, a pressure gauge that behaves oddly, or a pattern that no longer covers the face of the pile. Those are dust control failures before they are maintenance items, and they are usually discovered by a complaint or an inspection rather than by the operator.

## Anti-Freeze Measures: What Earns Its Place

**Heat, applied deliberately.** Self-regulating trace heating cable on exposed lines and in the pump and control cabinet, controlled by a thermostat, is the one active measure that reliably protects water that cannot be drained. Insulation on its own only delays cooling and gives a false sense of protection — it slows the freeze but does not prevent it. A cabinet heater with its own thermostat is cheap insurance for the control panel and the pump enclosure, and it must be checked after every power interruption, because a tripped breaker in an outdoor cabinet is invisible until someone opens the door.

**Lubricants and fluids matched to temperature.** Grease that stiffens and hydraulic oil that thickens will stall an actuator or overload a motor long before anything freezes. If the machine is on a diesel-driven trailer or truck, the winter items are their own list: battery capacity falls sharply with temperature, summer-grade diesel begins to wax near zero, and DEF/urea freezes around minus eleven degrees and expands if the tank was filled to the brim. Coolant should be checked for concentration, not just level.

**Protection that acts on information.** A frozen line announces itself in the data before it announces itself as a leak: pressure at the pump rises while flow at the manifold falls. Dry-run protection and a pressure or flow alarm are the cheapest protection available, because they stop the pump before the pump destroys itself. On a machine that runs unattended or on a remote site, that is worth more than any mechanical accessory.

**Antifreeze, used with discipline.** Glycol belongs in closed circuits — hydraulic cooling, engine coolant, a sealed recirculation loop. It does not belong in a once-through spray circuit, where it leaves the machine and lands on the product, the coal pile or the yard. It contaminates stock, it changes how the dust wets, it costs money, and it only works at the correct concentration. A diluted loop provides no protection at all, which is worse than knowing you have none. Equally, a drain valve that is buried behind a panel and needs a spanner is not a winter measure; it is a good intention.

## The Morning After a Frost

Restarting a machine that has been through a cold night is where more damage is done than during the night itself, usually by people trying to help it.

Never hammer ice off a manifold, a valve or a fitting, and never use an open flame on any part of a spray circuit. Through the freeze, you can always thaw by bringing the machine back to ambient temperature — a warm air blower, a heated enclosure, or warm water applied to the outside of the machine only. Pushing warm water into a frozen blind cavity simply gives it more water to freeze again.

Then check in a fixed order. The nozzle ring first, because a cracked or split orifice is visible and it is the cheapest part to replace. The strainer and filter bowl next. Then the valves, then the pump. Bring pressure up slowly and watch the gauge on the way up, because a hairline crack will show as a mist or a drip under pressure and stay hidden at low pressure. A mechanical seal that has been pushed out of its seat shows as a drip at the weep hole.

Expect at least one fitting to fail in the first week after an unusually cold night. Knowing that in advance is what makes it a maintenance event rather than a crisis.

## A Winter Calendar That Survives a Busy Month

**Before the first frost.** Do the full drain-down while the weather is still warm and the mistake is cheap: find the true low points, clear or fit drains, test the blow-out, check that insulation is intact and heat trace actually powers up, verify the thermostat setpoint, and stock one spare nozzle set, a pump seal and a solenoid. This is the single most valuable afternoon of the year for a spray system.

**Every shift, below about plus five degrees.** Either confirm circulation or complete the drain-down. Look at the spray pattern, not just the pressure.

**Weekly.** Strainer and filter bowl, insulation integrity, and confirmation that the cabinet heater is still live. Inspect the nozzle ring for blockage and wear.

**Monthly.** Trend the pressure and flow readings, check for pump seal weeping, and look at the manifold for any deformation that appeared without a mechanical cause.

**At the end of winter.** Pressure test the whole circuit before the season starts in earnest, replace the nozzle set if the pattern is uneven, and write down what froze and where. The low point that cracked this year is the low point that needs a drain before next year.

## What a Winter Failure Actually Costs

A dust control failure in December or January does not arrive at a convenient time. It arrives in the season with the highest dust load, when the site cannot simply wait two weeks for a part, and when an unusual plume is most likely to attract attention.

The direct costs are unglamorous: a cracked pump casing or a deformed manifold is not a hardware store item, and it comes with a lead time, a lifting operation and downtime. On a truck-mounted unit, the chassis goes out of service with the cannon. There is also a safety cost that people forget until it happens — ice on haul roads and turning circles created by the machine's own overspray.

The soft cost is worse. A cannon that sprays unevenly is still counted as working. It runs, it makes noise, it looks right, and the yard stays dusty. Nobody notices until someone measures.

The sites that get through winter without drama are rarely the ones with the most equipment. They are the ones where the drain-down at the end of the shift is somebody's job, done in a fixed order, and checked. That is a procedural difference, not a technical one, and it is available to any operator with any machine.
