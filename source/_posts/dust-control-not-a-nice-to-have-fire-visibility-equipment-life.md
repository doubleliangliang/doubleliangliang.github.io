Ask a plant manager whether dust control is worth the money and you will usually get a reasonable answer: it depends on the permit, the budget, and whether anyone has complained recently. That framing treats dust as a compliance line item — something you buy when a regulator or a neighbour forces the issue, and defer when the capital committee is looking for savings.

The framing is wrong, and it is expensive. Compliance measures one thing — what crosses the boundary — and that is the smallest part of what dust costs. The three bills that decide the number are billed elsewhere, and none of them arrives with an invoice: an exposure that accumulates inside the people who work in the material and a fuel load that accumulates above them, a sight distance that shortens without anyone reporting it, and a maintenance bill that shows up as unplanned downtime, premature bearing failure, and control cabinets that have to be replaced years early.

This article works through those three bills in detail — **worker health and process safety, visibility, and equipment life** — and then sets out the four functions that actually pay them down: area suppression with fog cannons, point suppression with dry fog, carry-out control at the wheel wash, and the control layer that decides when the other three run. Dust bills an operation for more than these three, and the ones that already sit inside somebody else's budget are named at the end of this section. Everything else in a plant is covered only where it marks a boundary.

## The Three Bills Dust Sends You Every Month

It helps to separate what dust does from what dust control is usually sold as.

Dust control is sold as an environmental measure. It is costed against a permit limit, it competes for capital against production equipment, and its business case is written in the language of compliance. That is why it gets deferred: a compliance item that is not currently being enforced looks optional.

What dust actually does is operational. The same settled layer that shows up in an environmental inspection is a fuel load sitting on a beam above a hot bearing. The same airborne cloud that shows up on a boundary monitor is the reason a loader operator cannot see a pedestrian at thirty metres. The same abrasive particulate that leaves the plant through the gate is grinding its way through the plant's own conveyor idlers, gearboxes, and switchgear.

Three bills, all paid by the same operation, and none of them filed under dust.

Environmental compliance is the only bill that arrives with a number attached and a reference to quote, which is why it gets managed — and also why it is mistaken for the whole bill rather than the smallest part of it. The rest are absorbed into other budgets under other names: a lost-time injury, a collision nobody can prove was caused by dust, a maintenance variance, an unbudgeted motor replacement. That is precisely why they stay invisible in the decision that matters.

The rest of this section takes each bill in turn, because they are not equivalent, and they do not all point to the same piece of equipment. Three bills is not the whole list, either. The others are just as real, but they already have a budget line with somebody else's name on it, and that is exactly why nobody reads them as a dust problem.

## Bill One: The First Bill Is Paid by People, Not by Buildings

Ask a plant what dust costs it and the answer usually starts with the explosion. The order is backwards. The bill that is paid first, paid every working day, and never appears in an incident report is the one paid by the people who work in the material.

**Respirable dust does not accumulate in buildings. It accumulates in people.** Where the material contains crystalline silica, regulatory exposure limits are typically in the range of 0.05 mg/m³ as an eight-hour time-weighted average, and some jurisdictions apply a precautionary level around 0.02 mg/m³. OSHA estimates that roughly 2.3 million workers in the United States are exposed to silica at work. The jurisdictions that tightened these limits most recently did not do so because of mining — they did it because silicosis came back, in stone fabrication shops working engineered stone, where California health authorities reported a rising count of confirmed cases, deaths and lung transplants, and Cal/OSHA responded with a permanent standard covering high-exposure tasks. The reason wet methods are used so heavily in cutting, crushing and screening is that suppression at the point of generation cuts respirable exposure by something approaching an order of magnitude compared with dry methods. It is the largest single lever available on a process that already exists.

A settled layer is a fire load. A suspended cloud is a dose. They are not the same problem, and they do not resolve on the same timescale.

The other half of this bill is the half that makes the news.

The public image of a dust incident is a single violent event. Operationally, it is a sequence, and the first stages are a fire, not an explosion.

A layer of combustible dust settles on a horizontal surface — a beam, a cable tray, the top of a motor, the inside of a duct. Heat arrives from something routine: a bearing running hot, a welding job two metres away, a failed lamp, friction in a seized idler. The layer does not need a spark to become dangerous; depending on the material and the layer depth, it can begin to smoulder, producing little smoke, no visible flame, and no signal on most detectors a plant owns.

That smoulder is the dangerous stage, because it turns a loose deposit into an ignition source. When the settled material is later disturbed — by cleaning, by a passing vehicle, by a thermal updraught — a cloud forms in an enclosed or semi-enclosed space, and a secondary explosion follows. Historically, that second event causes most of the structural damage and most of the casualties, because it consumes the dust that had accumulated across the whole building rather than the dust present at the original point of failure.

The scale is not marginal, and it is well documented.

The United States Occupational Safety and Health Administration reports that since 1980, more than 130 workers have been killed and more than 780 injured in combustible dust explosions. The industry's annual incident reporting — the Combustible Dust Incident Report series — recorded an average of roughly 33 dust explosions a year in the United States across 2016 to 2019, with approximately 30 injuries and 3 fatalities a year, and found that in 2019, 87% of global fatalities in the dataset came from explosions rather than fires. The National Fire Protection Association, working from its own data, describes an average of about 31 explosions a year at United States manufacturing facilities, alongside more than four times that number of dust fires.

Fires matter on their own account. Data drawn from the National Fire Incident Reporting System shows an annual average of 69 structure fires in industrial properties between 2020 and 2024 in which the item first ignited was dust, fibre or lint, causing around $3 million a year in property damage. That is roughly 2% of the 3,100 or so industrial structure fires reported annually — a small share of incident reports, and a much larger share of the consequences, because these events are preventable with housekeeping and control.

Regulatory attention has followed. OSHA has revised its Combustible Dust National Emphasis Programme to target inspections using enforcement history and incident reports, and NFPA consolidated six separate combustible dust standards — including NFPA 652, 654, 61, 484 and 664 — into a single standard, NFPA 660, published in 2025. That consolidation is useful because it states the control philosophy in three words that apply to any material: **contain, capture, clean**.

Where does suppression fit in that triad?

Suppression is not a substitute for design. It does not replace explosion venting, isolation, or a proper dust hazard analysis, and it should never be sold as if it did. What suppression does is operate on the same mechanism in the opposite direction: it stops fine particulate from becoming airborne in the first place, which reduces both the cloud that could be ignited and the layer that could later be disturbed into one.

That has two consequences worth stating plainly. First, **the settled layer is the target as much as the plume is** — a system that knocks a visible plume to the ground without anyone removing it has moved the fuel load, not eliminated it. Second, the environments where this matters most are also the environments where the electrical question has to be answered deliberately: where combustible dust is present, Ex configurations can be provided to suit the customer's application, and the specific protection type and certificate are supplied with the project.

## Bill Two: Dust Blinds the People Who Operate the Plant

The second bill rarely appears in an environmental report, because it is not measured in concentration. It is measured in sight distance.

Airborne dust does more than make a site look untidy. It scatters light, which reduces contrast; it compresses depth perception, which is what an operator uses to judge a bucket position, a clearance, or a reversing distance; and it masks edges, openings, and moving plant. In an area where loaders, tippers, haul trucks, and pedestrians share a working surface, that is not an aesthetic problem. It is the mechanism by which collisions happen.

Three practical observations follow, and they are summarised from what operators and safety literature consistently report.

**Vision degrades before anyone notices.** A dusted windscreen, a scratched visor, and a lens film each take a small bite out of clarity, and the operator adapts to the degraded view rather than reporting it. By the time the reduction is obvious, the working conditions have already been unsafe for some time.

**Backscatter hides where the problem is.** In low light, airborne dust in a headlight or a work light looks the same as fog. That matters twice: it makes a dust problem difficult to judge visually, and it means that lighting design can either reveal where suppression is failing or conceal it. A beam that shows a persistent haze is one of the cheapest diagnostic tools on site.

**Visibility is a control in its own right.** Separating pedestrian and vehicle routes, marking route edges, cleaning lenses and windows as part of the housekeeping round, and specifying high-visibility clothing appropriate to a dusty environment are all sensible. None of them address the source. Suppression does, and it is the only one of the measures listed that improves the operator's view without requiring the operator to compensate for anything.

There is a regulatory reason to take visibility seriously beyond safety, and it is worth understanding because it changes what you should buy.

In the United States, many industrial permits for particulate matter operate on a visible-emission limit — commonly no more than 20% opacity averaged over any consecutive six-minute period, with compliance determined by EPA Reference Method 9, in which a trained observer certifies the reading. A visible-emission limit converts an aesthetic complaint into an enforceable legal criterion. Assessment by human observer also explains the shape of the instrument you end up buying: a plant under an opacity limit does not primarily need a device that reports a concentration number, it needs a device that can detect and document a visible dust-escape event, because that is the criterion it will be judged against.

One qualification on the same point matters for anyone specifying automated monitoring. The opacity rules explicitly allow an exceedance where uncombined water — steam, in practice — is demonstrated to be the only cause. Any system that claims to detect visible dust automatically therefore has to distinguish a dust plume from water vapour. This is the single most common source of nuisance alarms in plants where hot material is present, and it is a design requirement, not a tuning detail.

## Bill Three: Dust Is Buying You New Bearings, Motors and Control Cabinets

The third bill is the one that is easiest to prove after the fact and hardest to see in advance, because it is absorbed into maintenance and capex rather than filed under environment.

Dust damages equipment through a small number of well-understood routes. They compound, and that is why the effect is often underestimated.

**Abrasive wear.** Hard mineral particles that settle into a bearing housing, a linear guide, a gearbox breather, or a conveyor idler seal do not simply sit there. Mixed with grease and oil they form a lapping paste, and the assembly stops wearing like a lubricated machine and starts wearing like a grinding machine. Belt systems, screw conveyors and bucket elevators, where the product itself is abrasive, are the clearest case.

**Lubrication failure.** Contaminated lubricant loses its film strength. A machine can keep running, so nothing looks wrong, but the component reaches end of life early and the failure is then recorded as a lubrication problem or a parts-quality problem rather than a dust problem.

**Blocked cooling paths.** Dust accumulates on heat sinks, motor fins, radiator cores, and ventilation filters. The result is a slow temperature increase that does not trip anything. In electronics this has a well-established consequence: as a rule of thumb, each 10 °C rise in operating temperature roughly halves the service life of an electrolytic capacitor. A drive cabinet whose filter is not maintained is therefore not merely dirtier than a clean one; it is ageing faster in a way nobody books.

**Electrical and control contamination.** Conductive dust — metal fines, carbon, some process residues — settles on terminal blocks, busbars and connectors, bridging clearances and producing leakage currents, nuisance trips and intermittent communication faults. Non-conductive dust causes the thermal problem instead. Both degrade the diagnostic quality of the control system at the same time, because a fouled sensor, encoder, or photoelectric switch reports the wrong thing. Frustrating intermittent faults are, very often, contamination.

**Hydraulic and pneumatic degradation.** Seals and valve spools are intolerant of particulate. Pressure loss, slower actuation, and sticking valves are commonly the first visible symptom of an environment problem rather than a component problem.

![Five pathways through which settled and airborne dust shorten the life of industrial equipment](/images/dust-control-dust-ages-equipment.svg)

The point is not that any one of these is catastrophic. It is that they act at the same time, on different subsystems, and their symptoms get treated separately: an electrician attends the intermittent fault, a fitter replaces the bearing, a contractor changes the filters. The underlying cause — dust loading in the operating environment — remains untouched, so the countdown restarts.

The financial case is not about the price of a bearing. It is about downtime, and the published benchmarks for downtime are stark. A 2025 survey of more than 600 manufacturing decision-makers across the United States, the United Kingdom and Germany found that 61% of manufacturers had experienced unplanned downtime in the preceding year, that 45% of incidents lasted up to 12 hours and 15% stretched to 72 hours, and that at an average cost of $1.7 million per hour the sector was losing up to $852 million a week. A separate 2025 survey of 3,600 industrial decision-makers found that 44% experienced equipment-related interruptions at least monthly and 14% weekly, with most respondents putting the cost between $10,000 and $500,000 per hour. Cross-industry benchmarks compiled from reliability studies place steel and heavy metals at an average of roughly $300,000 per hour of unplanned downtime, with worst-case critical-machine events running into eight figures, and put the unplanned downtime cost across large manufacturers at around 11% of total revenue.

Those figures are not a prediction for any single plant. They are the reason the third bill deserves to be treated as an availability programme rather than a cleaning expense.

## The Bills That Have Someone Else's Name on Them

The three bills above are the ones that arrive without an invoice. They are not the whole list. Dust also bills the operation in ways that already sit inside a budget — which is exactly why they are rarely read as dust problems.

**Product that leaves as dust is product you already paid for.** At a conveyor transfer point, published bulk handling benchmarks put fugitive material loss somewhere between under 0.1% at a well-maintained installation and 3% at a poorly maintained one, with around 1% a commonly used engineering working figure. At 200 tonnes an hour, one percent is two tonnes an hour of material that has been bought, handled, and then handed to the wind. It does not come back.

**Occupational disease is billed decades after the exposure.** Explosions concentrate harm into minutes; occupational exposure spreads it across a working life, which is why an exposure that is going wrong today never presents as an incident this year. In the United Kingdom, an estimated 12,000 deaths a year are linked to past work-related lung disease — a figure dominated by conditions with decades of latency, so it mainly reflects exposures that were controlled, or were not, a long time ago. That lag is exactly what makes the line easy to discount now, and it is why the exposure sits at the front of this article rather than in an appendix.

**Quality is the bill your customer sees.** A particle that lands on a wafer, a coating, a food-contact surface or a pharmaceutical powder is a defect the process cannot remove afterwards. Plants that count contamination in particles per cubic metre do not treat dust as housekeeping, because in those plants dust is scrap.

**Relationships outside the fence do not run on compliance.** A permit is not a defence to a private nuisance claim, and public benefit is not one either. In the United Kingdom, a 2025 High Court decision — *Andrews and others v Kronospan Ltd* — arose from 159 residents suing over six years of dust, odour and noise from a wood panel plant. The operator ultimately succeeded, but only after a four-week trial, and the outcome turned on the quality of its own monitoring data. The lesson is not that such claims win. It is that the defence is assembled out of measurement records — cheap to accumulate, impossible to backfill.

**Housekeeping labour, insurance and the cost of capital** close the list. Cleaning is a recurring cost that scales with how much material settles, and in a combustible dust zone it has to be done with different equipment and from a smaller available labour pool. Environmental performance increasingly feeds insurance underwriting and lending terms, where a documented control regime and an undocumented one are two different risk profiles.

None of this needs a second system. Every line above improves when less dust leaves the source in the first place. But it is worth naming, because a plant that budgets against three bills is managing exposure, fire risk, sight distance and machine life — and leaving material, customers, neighbours and finance to whoever happens to own those numbers this week.

## Why "Nice-to-Have" Is the Most Expensive Word in the Budget Meeting

Put the three bills side by side and the reason dust control gets deferred becomes obvious. Two of them never appear in the accounting system at all, and the third only becomes urgent when a regulator or a neighbour raises it.

There is also a structural reason. A dust programme is usually presented as an equipment purchase with an environmental justification, which places it in competition with production capital and gives it the weakest possible business case. Presented instead as what it is — a measure that protects continuity of operation, reduces a fire load, and returns the operator's sight distance — the same proposal competes differently, and often wins.

The honest version of the argument has a boundary as well, and stating it is what separates engineering from sales. **Not every dust problem is a water problem.** Some are solved by enclosure, by extraction, by filtration, or by changing how material is handled. Pretending otherwise produces installations that are switched off within a season, which is how a plant ends up concluding that dust control does not work.

So the practical question is not "should we buy a dust suppression system". It is "for which dust sources is suppression the right answer, and what kind of suppression does each source need".

## What the Regulatory Frameworks Already Ask For

Before answering that, it is worth noting that the obligations most plants are trying to meet already describe the same four functions. The vocabulary differs between jurisdictions; the engineering converges.

In the **European Union**, the controlling instrument is the Industrial Emissions Directive (2010/75/EU), supported by the BAT conclusions for iron and steel production (Commission Implementing Decision 2012/135/EU). Those conclusions address diffuse dust directly, and they are more specific than most people expect. They require an associated diffuse dust action plan within the plant's environmental management system, including the use of sufficient PM10 monitors together with wind direction and wind strength data to identify which sources are actually driving ambient readings. The listed techniques include orienting long stockpiles relative to the prevailing wind, wind barriers, controlling the moisture content of delivered material, containment on conveyors and in hoppers, dust-suppressing water sprays, housekeeping with cleaning and damping of roads, dust extraction and bag filtration, and emissions-reduced sweeping machines. On road transport the document is more direct still: hard-surfaced roads, road cleaning, sheeting of vehicles, restriction of vehicles to designated routes — and the employment of **wheel-cleaning equipment to prevent the carryover of mud and dust onto public roads**.

In the **United States**, the framework runs through the National Emission Standards for Hazardous Air Pollutants for integrated iron and steel manufacturing, National Ambient Air Quality Standards for PM10 and PM2.5, and permit conditions including the visible-emission limits discussed above. Permits also impose a general duty to take reasonable precautions to prevent fugitive particulate emissions, and the usual list of precautions reads like a specification: water or chemical dust control during grading and road work; application of water or suitable chemicals to unpaved roads and material stockpiles; full or partial enclosure of stockpiles where wetting is not sufficient or appropriate; good housekeeping with prompt cleanup of spilled or accumulated material; and hoods, fans and fabric filters to enclose and vent dusty material handling.

In **emerging markets**, project-financed capacity frequently has to satisfy the IFC Performance Standards and the World Bank Group environmental, health and safety guidelines from the first day of operation, because the financing requires it.

**China's** industrial sectors have gone through a national ultra-low emission retrofit programme — a China-specific policy construct, not a term used in European or North American permitting — which for fugitive sources relies on a visual criterion rather than a concentration limit: no visible dust escape at dust-generating points or from production equipment, and a clean yard with no accumulated dust. The supporting technical guidance is detailed, covering enclosed storage, enclosed conveying, wheel and body washing equipment at yard exits, and monitoring networks covering boundary air quality, key dust areas and the run status of treatment equipment.

Four vocabularies, one engineering reality. Every one of these frameworks asks for the same four things: bring dust down across open areas, lock it down at enclosed points, stop it leaving the site on wheels, and be able to prove that all of it happened.

## Four Functions, Not One Product

The most common and most expensive conceptual mistake in this field is to treat the available equipment as a single product family ranked by size, and to buy the largest unit the budget allows.

It is not a family. It is four functions, and they solve different problems in different physics.

![The four functions of a dust control system: area suppression, point suppression, carry-out control and the control layer](/images/dust-control-four-functions.svg)

| Function | Job it performs | Typical device | Where it belongs | Precondition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Area suppression | Brings down an airborne plume over open ground | Long-range fog cannon | Stockyards, crushing platforms, tipping areas, slag yards, haul roads | Wind-aware layout; mechanical sweeping on the same route |
| Point suppression | Captures fine dust at a fixed source | Dry fog system | Belt transfer points, crushers, screens, chutes, bucket elevators, bunker charging | An enclosure or containment volume to work inside |
| Carry-out control | Stops material leaving the site on vehicles | Wheel wash station | Site exits used by haulage | Water capture and treatment; a drainage or drying stage |
| Control layer | Decides when the other three run, and produces the record | Monitoring, controllers, presets, dashboards | Across all of the above | Per-zone thresholds; commissioning and tuning as scope |

Fog cannons are not large dry fog systems, and dry fog systems are not small cannons. They atomise differently, act on particulate by different mechanisms, and belong at opposite ends of the same material handling chain. Asking which one "works better" has no answer, in the same way that asking whether a screwdriver is better than a hammer does not.

## Fog Cannons: Covering the Ground Where Dust Starts to Move

A fog cannon addresses the area problem: open ground covered in coarse material, where dust is lifted by wind, by falling material, and by vehicle wheels, and where the plume is visible enough that neighbours can see it.

The physics imposes the design decisions.

**Droplet size has to match the dust.** Capture efficiency for a given particle is highest when the droplet is of a comparable scale, because impaction and interception do the work. A coarse spray knocks down the visible plume above a stockpile but does very little to the fine fraction, which is the part that travels and the part that matters for exposures. The fine mist band — broadly 10 to 50 micrometres — is the right regime for PM10 and PM2.5, and it can only be delivered close to the source, because fine droplets lose momentum quickly and evaporate over a long throw. This is exactly why area and point suppression are complements rather than competitors.

**Throw distance is a still-air number.** Published ranges assume calm air and no obstruction. On a working site a crosswind visibly shortens effective reach and pushes the plume off target. That is not a defect in the machine; it is a property of the medium. The design decisions that actually determine performance are mounting height, overlap between adjacent units under the site's prevailing wind, and whether the control system can compensate for wind direction — not the number in the brochure.

**Water brings dust down; it does not remove it.** Particles that settle onto a haul road are re-suspended by the next truck. Without a sweeper working the same route on a defined schedule, a cannon programme builds a machine that moves dust from the air to the ground and back again. Cannon plus sweeping is one measure, not two.

## Dry Fog: Locking a Point Source in Place

Dry fog works on the opposite problem: a fixed, enclosed, fine-dust source, at a distance of metres.

A twin-fluid nozzle atomises compressed air and water together, producing droplets in the 1 to 10 micrometre range. At that scale, capture is dominated by diffusion and by agglomeration — droplets and particles collide, grow, and settle. The moisture added to the material is measured in fractions of a percent, which is what makes the technique usable on a belt or a chute where a coarse spray would wet the product, cause build-up, or create a handling problem.

It fits a specific set of situations: belt transfer points and loading skirts, crusher feed and discharge, screening equipment, chute loading, bucket elevators, and bunker charging. Fine dust, fixed source, enclosed volume.

And it comes with one precondition that outweighs every other consideration: **dry fog needs containment**.

Used in the open, the fog disperses on the wind and the dust does not move. The equipment runs, consumes air and water, and produces nothing that can be measured. This is not a rare mistake, and it is never the customer's fault — it is the fault of a proposal that quoted nozzles without asking what would contain them. If a dry fog proposal does not begin with the enclosure, the skirting, and the extraction, that proposal is not finished.

## Wheel Wash: The Device That Stops Dust Leaving on Wheels

The least glamorous item in this article is the one with the clearest regulatory standing.

The mechanism is simple and entirely physical. Dust deposited on a haul road adheres to tyres and undercarriage. A truck leaves the site and sheds that material onto the public road. It dries, traffic lifts it, and the resulting particulate is attributed to your plant regardless of how well the stockyard itself is managed.

That is why the European BAT conclusions name wheel-cleaning equipment specifically rather than leaving it as an optional accessory, and why several jurisdictions treat a wheel and body wash at yard exits as a required installation. Where an inspector is looking for evidence of a site's materials containment, this is the evidence.

The engineering is larger than the name suggests. Four things decide whether it works.

**Coverage.** Tyres alone are not enough. The undercarriage, mudflaps and lower body carry a substantial share of the load carried onto public roads.

**Water capture and sludge handling.** A wheel wash that discharges onto the ground a few metres from the gate has relocated the problem rather than solved it. The station needs collection and treatment — settlement, recycling, or discharge to a facility — plus a routine for the sludge, because that sludge is the material you were trying to contain. Closed-loop recycling is the usual answer, and it also reduces the water demand of the installation.

**Free drainage.** A vehicle that leaves the gate still shedding water leaves a wet trail on the public road. In summer that is a nuisance; when the temperature falls it becomes a safety hazard. A drainage or drying stage belongs in the specification, not in the variation order.

**Traffic actually driving through it.** A station only works if vehicles use it. On a large site, one exit is frequently not enough, and a station positioned on the wrong side of a weighbridge will be bypassed.

A wheel wash is not an air quality device. It is a materials containment device, and it works best when it is designed together with the road cleaning schedule, the surface hardening programme, and the traffic plan.

## Smart Control: Turning a Daily Opinion Into a Record

Most installations that underperform have the same architecture underneath: a timer and a contactor. The yard sprays for a fixed period on a fixed cycle regardless of what is happening. It is cheap, and on paper it is defensible.

It fails in three directions at once. Water and power are spent on an idle yard. Material is over-wetted in one season and pipes freeze in another. And when a review arrives, there is no record of anything at all.

What replaces the timer is a loop rather than a schedule: monitor, identify, grade, trigger, verify, record.

![The dust control loop: monitoring and detection feeding identification, triggering, verification and reporting](/images/dust-control-control-loop.svg)

Two details matter more than the architecture diagram.

**The system must separate dust from steam.** This is the most common false-alarm source wherever hot material is handled — the same problem the visible-emission rules acknowledge by exempting uncombined water. If a detection model cannot tell a dust plume from water vapour, the operators will mute it within a month, and they will be right to. Camera-based and laser-based detection both work; what matters is whether the model was trained on your dust, your steam, and your lighting.

**Thresholds must be per zone.** An alarm level that is appropriate at a stockyard is meaningless at a transfer tower, and a site-wide threshold will either over-spray the quiet areas or under-serve the busy ones. Related to this: a commissioning period on site is not evidence that the product is unfinished. It is how the thresholds get set to your plant, and it should be budgeted as scope — typically a matter of weeks rather than days.

The measurable benefit of the loop is resource efficiency. Vendor-reported figures for sensor-driven, demand-based misting systems show reductions in water consumption in the tens of percent, with the highest claims approaching 80% against continuous spraying. Those numbers depend entirely on the site, and they should be treated as a hypothesis to be verified against your own metered baseline — which the same system will give you, per zone, for the first time.

The KPI worth putting on the wall is the most direct one available: **visible dust-escape events per week, per zone**. It is a number an operations manager can act on, a number a community liaison officer can report, and — where an opacity or visible-emission limit applies — a number that maps straight onto the regulatory criterion. It is also the number a review team will ask for first.

## Where Water Is Still the Wrong Answer

Stating the boundary is part of the argument, so here it is in brief.

Suppression is not the right tool for channelled emissions, which belong to extraction, cyclones, bag filters and electrostatic precipitation. It is not the right tool where moisture is a process parameter rather than an environmental one — finishing stages in cement and lime production, product drying, and high-temperature gas streams are the standard examples — nor where adding water to the material creates a handling or reaction problem. And in some combustible dust environments the wetting agent itself needs to be considered carefully in the design rather than assumed benign.

Those cases exist in every plant, and the useful output of a dust survey is not only the list of places where spray belongs. It is the list of places where it does not.

## Choosing a System Without Writing a Tender

Once the sources have been grouped, the four functions assigned, and the exclusions written down, the remaining decision is about suppliers — and this is where most of the risk sits.

The three questions that separate proposals most reliably are not technical specifications. They are whether the proposal was designed as one system or assembled from a catalogue; how long the supplier has been solving this problem in your industry specifically; and what happens after handover, in terms of spares and response.

Behind those questions sit a handful of things that are genuinely difficult to evaluate from a datasheet.

Droplet size distribution cannot be measured on site, and it changes in service as wear, scaling and filtration affect the nozzle and the water. It has to be guaranteed by design and configuration. Throw distance is usually a theoretical or design figure, and there is currently no mandatory test method that would let two quoted figures be compared — which is the practical reason to refer to the industry technical standard for air-assisted spray dust suppression equipment (JB/T 13562-2018) when discussing range claims. Noise and vibration are barely noticeable at installation and become significant under continuous operation. Power draw for the same nominal throw is a cost and a noise penalty rather than evidence of a better configuration. "Automatic" means different things in different quotations, from a manual remote through to interlocks with the material handling sequence. And durability is not about whether a failure is inside the warranty period; it is about whether the unit keeps running without intervention.

One point on service life deserves to be separated from the warranty discussion, because the two get confused. A properly specified installation, with consumable parts replaced on schedule, water quality within the agreed range, and winter protection carried out to procedure, is designed for more than ten years of normal operation. That is a statement about the design, not about the warranty term, and it is worth asking a supplier to make it in writing.
