Every right that is written down forces somebody to decide what it obliges. Clean air now belongs on that list. The United Nations recognised the right to a clean, healthy and sustainable environment at the Human Rights Council in 2021 and at the General Assembly in 2022, and more than 150 states already carried some version of it in their own constitutions, statutes or case law before either vote happened. The recognition is no longer the contested part.

Enforcement is. The pollution that is easiest to photograph is not the pollution that does the most damage, and the pollution that does the most damage is the pollution that never passes through a chimney.

Stack emissions come with a duct, a monitoring port, a number and an inspector. Fugitive emissions — diffuse emissions, in the language of European regulation — come off open stockpiles, conveyor transfer points, haul roads, crushing decks and loading bays. There is nothing to sample, the source moves with the traffic, and the output changes with wind speed, moisture and season. That is the part of the right to clean air that is hardest to deliver, and it is where the world's regulatory systems are converging fastest.

This article sets out four layers of response that the major jurisdictions now share — recognition, regulation, measurement and control — and what each layer actually demands of an industrial site. It draws on United Nations and WHO material, European Union and United States regulation, and China's decade of air quality work.

## Why a Right to Clean Air Had to Be Written Down

The Human Rights Council resolution of 8 October 2021 was the first time the right to a clean, healthy and sustainable environment was recognised at the global level. Resolution 48/13 was proposed by Costa Rica, the Maldives, Morocco, Slovenia and Switzerland, and it passed with 43 votes in favour and four abstentions — an unusually wide margin for an environmental text, and the outcome of more than four decades of work by civil society, national human rights institutions and indigenous peoples' organisations. The UN General Assembly followed on 28 July 2022 with resolution 76/300, adopted with 161 votes in favour, none against and eight abstentions.

What made those votes necessary rather than decorative was the arithmetic behind them. In the report accompanying the Human Rights Council process, the Special Rapporteur recorded that pollution and hazardous substances cause at least 9 million premature deaths a year — roughly one in six deaths worldwide, and several times the annual toll of AIDS, malaria and tuberculosis combined. The World Health Organization puts approximately 24 per cent of all global deaths, about 13.7 million a year, down to environmental causes including air pollution.

The Special Rapporteur's 2026 report on clean air, prepared for the Human Rights Council's sixty-first session, states the position without hedging: "Clean air is an element of the human right to a clean, healthy and sustainable environment, and is essential for the enjoyment of all human rights, including to life and health." It puts air pollution at approximately 8 million premature deaths a year, and notes that 83 per cent of the premature deaths associated with air pollution come from chronic disease — cardiovascular disease, stroke, chronic respiratory disease and cancer. These are not acute poisoning events. They are the long-run consequence of breathing a baseline that nobody chose.

The same report identifies the distributional pattern that turns a health statistic into a rights problem. Exposure is highest among people who contributed least to it, and the extreme cases have a name in the UN literature: sacrifice zones, such as the Quintero-Puchuncaví industrial area in Chile, where communities carry a disproportionate share of the health consequences of contamination they had no part in creating.

## What the Right Says, and What It Leaves Open

The General Assembly text does not create a new mechanism. It recognises the right as one related to other rights and to existing international law, and several states recorded in their explanations of vote that political recognition by itself did not carry legal effect. That caveat is worth understanding rather than dismissing, because it explains where the right becomes operational.

It becomes operational when a court accepts that a degraded environment damages an individual's protected interests. The clearest example to date is the European Court of Human Rights judgment of 9 April 2024 in Verein KlimaSeniorinnen Schweiz and Others v. Switzerland. A Swiss association of some 2,400 women over the age of 64 argued that repeated heatwaves, made more severe by inadequate national mitigation, harmed their health. The Grand Chamber held that Switzerland had failed its positive obligations under Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights — the right to respect for private and family life, which the Court read as encompassing health and well-being — and found critical gaps in the domestic regulatory framework. It was the first climate case decided by an international human rights court, and its reasoning applies across the 46 member states of the Council of Europe. It builds on a line of national litigation running back through the Dutch Supreme Court's Urgenda judgment.

The structural shift is what matters for industry. Once a court accepts that an environment degraded below a threshold breaches an individual right, the question stops being whether the site holds a permit and becomes whether the permit is achieving what it is for. European law has moved in the same direction: the revised Industrial Emissions Directive gives, for the first time, a route for people to seek compensation for damage to health caused by illegal pollution.

## The Pollution That Never Leaves a Chimney

Consider how differently the two halves of an industrial site's emissions are governed.

A stack has one exit, one cross-section, one flow rate. Its emissions can be sampled at a known point, converted to a concentration in milligrams per normal cubic metre, and compared against a number in a permit. The measurement is traceable, repeatable and contestable in a way that satisfies both the operator and the regulator.

A stockpile has none of that. It sheds dust across its whole surface, more when the wind picks up and less after rain. A conveyor transfer point releases dust only when material is moving. A haul road releases dust only when a truck is on it, and the truck carries the dust out of the gate on its tyres and in the air behind it. In the steel sector, fugitive emissions have been estimated at more than half of the industry's total particulate emissions — which is to say that the half of the problem with no duct is also the larger half.

That asymmetry has a health consequence. The World Health Organization's 2021 global air quality guidelines tightened the recommended annual average for PM2.5 from 10 to 5 micrograms per cubic metre, with a 24-hour guideline of 15. On current measurement, more than 99 per cent of the world's population breathes air that exceeds the annual figure. In the Global Burden of Disease assessment for 2021, particulate matter ranked as the leading risk factor for the global disease burden, accounting for 8.0 per cent of total disability-adjusted life years — ahead of high systolic blood pressure and tobacco.

Air pollution also crosses boundaries. Dust raised in one jurisdiction settles in another, which is one reason the instruments that address it cannot be purely domestic.

## The Four Layers of Response

Across very different legal traditions, the same four layers keep appearing. Each answers a different question.

**Recognition** answers who can complain, and on what basis. **Regulation** answers what the site is required to achieve, and under what permit. **Measurement** answers how a source without a duct is made accountable. **Control** answers what is physically installed to bring the dust down.

A jurisdiction can be strong at one layer and weak at another, and the weakest layer sets the outcome.

## Layer One — Recognition: Rights, Constitutions and Standing

More than 150 states recognise the right to a healthy environment through their constitutions, national legislation, judicial decisions or participation in regional treaties. The Inter-American Court of Human Rights has read it into the American Convention, and UN treaty bodies have taken the same position: the Committee on the Rights of the Child, in General Comment No. 26, affirmed that children have the right to a clean, healthy and sustainable environment and treated it as implicit in the Convention on the Rights of the Child.

The practical content of the right is procedural as much as substantive. The General Assembly resolution states that the exercise of the rights to seek, receive and impart information, to participate effectively in the conduct of public affairs, and to an effective remedy is vital to the protection of a clean, healthy and sustainable environment. Information and participation are the mechanisms through which a rights claim becomes a specific obligation on a specific installation.

For an operating site the consequence is concrete. A complaint from a neighbouring community is no longer only a nuisance claim to be handled with a public relations response. In jurisdictions where environmental associations can obtain standing, and where a court is willing to examine whether measures were adequate rather than whether paperwork was complete, a complaint is the entry point to an argument about whether the site is meeting the standard the right implies.

## Layer Two — Regulation: Permits, BAT and the Opacity Number

This is the layer where the right becomes a number, and the numbers differ by jurisdiction more than the principles do.

**European Union.** The Industrial Emissions Directive, 2010/75/EU, is the main instrument. It was amended by Directive (EU) 2024/1785, which entered into force on 4 August 2024, with member states required to transpose it by 1 July 2026. It applies to roughly 37,000 industrial installations. Its working method is sector-by-sector: technical working groups under the European Commission's Joint Research Centre produce BAT reference documents, which become BAT Conclusions, which form the basis of permit conditions written by national authorities. Four changes in the revised directive matter for dust. First, emission limit values must now be applied at the point where emissions leave the installation. Second, competent authorities must set limits close to the best performance level technically achievable for that installation, rather than accepting the lenient upper end of the range associated with best available techniques. Third, the definition of pollution has been widened to include noise and odour. Fourth, environmental inspections are required at least every one to three years depending on risk, with data flowing into a new Industrial Emissions Portal from 2028.

Fugitive emissions are addressed explicitly within that architecture. Operators must identify and characterise all non-channelled sources — open surfaces, material handling, operational activities — evaluate the conditions that produce dispersion and the associated risk, and document the whole picture in an environmental management plan with traceability and self-monitoring obligations. National transpositions make the requirement concrete; the Italian decree implementing the directive, for example, ties BAT application, periodic permit review and continuous or periodic recording of emissions data into the authorisation itself.

Sectoral BAT conclusions go further still. For iron and steel production, the 2012 BAT conclusions require operators to establish a diffuse dust action plan inside the environmental management system, using PM10 monitoring together with wind direction and wind speed data to triangulate the main dust sources. The same document, in its road transport section, names wheel-cleaning equipment among the required measures — a useful reminder that at this level of regulation a wheel wash is not an accessory but an obligation.

**United States.** Fugitive emissions are controlled through the Clean Air Act and its New Source Performance Standards. Subpart OOO of 40 CFR Part 60, covering nonmetallic mineral processing plants, sets visible emission limits of less than 10 per cent opacity for non-crusher affected sources and less than 12 per cent for crusher-affected sources, with less than 7 per cent opacity plus a particulate concentration below 5 grams per dry standard cubic metre where the affected facilities sit inside a building. Compliance is demonstrated against the average of five six-minute averages. A 20 per cent opacity limit over a six-minute period is a widely used permit condition elsewhere.

Enforcement is where the American system makes its point. The Clean Air Act sets a statutory civil penalty of up to 25,000 US dollars per day of violation, which after inflation adjustments now exceeds 124,000 dollars per day per violation. A field citation programme covers minor violations at up to 5,000 dollars per day. Knowing violations carry criminal exposure of up to five years for a first offence. Alongside the penalties sits an administrative layer that is easy to underestimate: a dust control plan with daily logs recording weather conditions, wind speed, the times and locations of water or suppressant applications, complaints received and visible emissions observed. Site conditions change, and a plan that has not been updated is treated the same as no plan at all.

**China.** China's programme is the largest single air quality effort of the last decade, and it was built around structural change rather than end-of-pipe equipment alone. The 2013 air pollution action plan was followed by the sustained campaign known as the Blue Sky Defense War. Between 2013 and 2023, the national annual average PM2.5 concentration fell from 68 to 30 micrograms per cubic metre, a reduction of more than half, and the number of days with heavy pollution or worse fell by more than 80 per cent. In Beijing, PM2.5 fell from 89.5 to 32 micrograms per cubic metre and heavy pollution days from 58 to 8. The United Nations Environment Programme described the result as the "Beijing Miracle".

What makes the Chinese case instructive is that the improvement happened alongside growth. Over the same decade GDP rose by 78 per cent and energy consumption by 37 per cent. Coal's share of primary energy consumption fell from 67.4 to 55.3 per cent. The central government invests more than 30 billion yuan a year in air pollution prevention and control, with cumulative investment exceeding 200 billion yuan and total mobilised investment reaching around 1 trillion yuan. A comparison from the University of Chicago found that China reduced air pollution over seven years by roughly as much as the United States did over thirty.

**Emerging markets and international finance.** Where domestic enforcement is thinner, lender requirements often do the work. The IFC Performance Standards and the World Bank Group's environmental, health and safety guidelines set out expectations for fugitive dust at industrial and construction sites, and project finance covenants increasingly make those expectations contractual. Soft infrastructure matters too: the United Nations designated 7 September as the International Day of Clean Air for Blue Skies, first observed in 2020, to keep public attention on a problem that has no single dramatic image.

## Layer Three — Measurement: Making the Invisible Accountable

A source with no duct cannot be measured the way a stack is measured. Jurisdictions have therefore settled on three instruments, usually in combination.

The first is **visible emission observation**. A trained and certified observer reads the opacity of a plume at 15-second intervals and averages the readings over six minutes, using the procedure set out in EPA Method 9; the United States rules then average five such six-minute averages before comparing against the limit. The method is inexpensive, legally robust and accepted in court — and it is episodic, because it depends on a person being present with the right sun angle at the moment the dust is moving.

The second is **ambient monitoring**. Particulate monitors placed at the fence line or in the receiving community measure what actually reaches people rather than what leaves a process. The more useful deployments are segmented: a road corridor divided into sections, each with its own monitor for PM10 and PM2.5 together with local wind data, so that suppression on that section can be triggered by that section's own numbers. This turns a compliance report into a control input.

The third is **visual surveillance**. Cameras with automated haze and particle recognition aimed at stockyards, transfer towers and plant exits, usually linked to an alerting rule and often to the suppression system itself.

All three share one technical problem, and it is worth stating plainly because it is the most common source of operational failure. Industrial dust is frequently accompanied by water vapour, and regulators know it: United States opacity rules explicitly exempt uncombined water, meaning steam that has not combined with particulate. Any automated system that cannot distinguish a steam plume from a dust plume will report the plant's own morning mist as a violation. False positives, not misses, are the reason camera-based systems get switched off. The requirement that follows is not more sensitivity but better discrimination — a system that pairs visual recognition with a moisture or particulate discriminator, and that is tuned against the site's actual daily behaviour rather than a laboratory scene.

There is a second, less technical problem. Measurement changes an outcome only if something acts on the result. A monitor that produces a graph nobody reads is an exhibit, not a control.

## Layer Four — Control: What Actually Comes Down on Site

Suppression does not destroy dust. It moves it from suspension to the surface. Two consequences follow, and both are frequently missed.

The first is that suppression has to be applied where the dust is generated, not where it is noticed. Spraying the visible cloud downwind of a transfer point is treating the symptom. The second is that material brought down has to be removed. A settled layer on a haul road is available for the next truck and the next gust of wind; without mechanical sweeping to clear it, the same dust goes back into the air the same afternoon. Suppression and cleaning are one operation, not two.

Within that constraint the equipment splits along a clear line. **Point suppression** uses dry fog at enclosed transfer points, crusher discharge chutes, bucket elevators and storage silo tops, where droplets in the 1 to 10 micrometre range add moisture in the parts-per-thousand range — enough to make fine particles cohere and settle, without wetting the material enough to cause sticking or handling problems. **Area suppression** uses long-throw fog cannons on open stockyards, crushing platforms, haul roads and material yards, where the particles are coarser, the area is exposed and wind is the transport mechanism. The rule of thumb is short: fog covers the point, cannons cover the area. They are the two ends of one process chain, not a basic version and an upgraded version of the same product.

**Carry-out control** handles what would otherwise leave the site. Wheel-cleaning equipment at the gate, hard-surfaced transport roads and sheeting of loads address the dust that vehicles take with them — the same measures the European BAT conclusions name for road transport in the iron and steel sector.

The fourth function decides whether the other three are worth their capital cost: the **control layer** that links suppression to dust monitors, wind sensors and the operating status of the equipment being served, so that spraying starts when material is moving and stops when it is not. Unlinked systems run on a timer, which means they run when nothing is happening and stay idle when the dust is worst.

Where the difference between suppliers shows up is not on the specification sheet. Any two suppliers can write "complies" against every line of a parameter table. What separates systems is whether the pump, the spray ring, the nozzles, the fan and the control logic were designed as one machine, and whether the structure protects itself against the material it works in. A configuration that is coherent and matched across its parts is worth more than any one component being excellent, and this is a design question rather than a price question.

That is stated separately from the commercial terms — a one-year warranty with lifetime after-sales support.

Three questions are more useful in a procurement conversation than any threshold on a datasheet. Was this designed as one system, or assembled from components? How long has the supplier been doing this in an industry similar to yours? And what happens on spares and response the year after commissioning?

## Where the Frameworks Converge, and Where They Still Disagree

The convergence is real. No major jurisdiction now treats fugitive dust as unregulated. All of them have moved to the same four-layer structure, and all of them are shifting weight from paperwork toward measurement, and from measurement toward enforceable rights. That direction is unlikely to reverse; the health evidence, and the court decisions that follow it, point one way.

The disagreement is in the numbers, and it is large. The WHO recommends 5 micrograms per cubic metre as an annual average for PM2.5; national standards are looser, and the gap between them is a factor rather than a rounding error. Opacity thresholds range from 7 per cent for an enclosed building to 20 per cent as a widely used permit condition. Enforcement intensity varies more widely than the rules themselves — the same nominal limit is a negotiated condition in one jurisdiction and a per-day penalty in another.

The revised European requirement to set limits near the best achievable performance, rather than at the lenient end of the range, is the most significant recent move, and it has not yet been mirrored everywhere. Whether it becomes the model or remains a regional standard is the open question of the next few years.

## What This Means If You Run a Plant

Strip the legal language away and the right to clean air asks an operator four questions. Can you produce the plan, and is it current? Can you show what you actually did, day by day, with the weather attached? Can you demonstrate that you can tell dust from steam, and that your monitoring does it rather than assumes it? And does your suppression equipment run when the material moves and stop when it does not?

Those four questions have a compliance answer and an operational answer, and the operational answer is the one worth acting on. Compliance measures one thing — what crosses the boundary — and that is the smallest part of what dust costs a plant. The same settled layer that draws a complaint is a fuel load sitting above a hot bearing, a sight distance that shortens without anyone reporting it, and an abrasive film working through conveyor idlers, control cabinets and electrical terminals across the whole site.

A right that has been recognised is a right that will be measured. The operations that treat that as an engineering problem, rather than a paperwork problem, will be the ones that are not surprised.
