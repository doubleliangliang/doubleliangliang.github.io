Search for `fog cannon` in Europe and you will find one machine. Search for `anti-smog gun` in Delhi, Jakarta or Karachi and you will find the same machine, described in a different language for a different argument — a ring of nozzles inside a duct, an axial fan behind it, and a jet of atomised water thrown tens to hundreds of metres downwind.

The naming difference matters for procurement, and I will come back to it. The more useful question is what that jet of water can actually do about dust and about haze, because those are two different problems with two different particle populations — and most of the disappointment in this category comes from expecting one machine to solve both.

## Dust and Haze Are Not the Same Problem

Dust — fugitive dust, whatever it is called in the local market — is mechanically generated. Traffic lifting a road surface, a haul truck on a dry ramp, a loader dropping material into a hopper, wind crossing an unstabilised stockpile, a demolition hammer bringing down a wall. It is dominated by coarse particles, and it lives and dies close to where it is made. PM10 is the relevant number.

Haze is a different assembly. It is what you see when fine particulate scatters light in humid air, often hundreds of kilometres from anything. A large share of it is **secondary**: sulphate and nitrate formed in the atmosphere from SO₂ and NOₓ, and secondary organic aerosol from VOCs. Those are gases when they leave the stack or the tailpipe. They become particles later, in the sky, as a result of chemistry.

A fog cannon does not touch that chemistry. It has no mechanism for removing SO₂, NOₓ or VOCs, and no mechanism for chasing a particle that has not been formed yet. Anyone selling an anti-smog gun as an air-quality solution at city scale is overselling, and buyers in South Asia have learned to be sceptical for exactly this reason.

What a fog cannon does have is real, and it deals with the other half of the problem — the half that starts on the ground. It is worth putting a number on how much that half is. In the steel sector, which has been studied harder than almost any other industry, fugitive and unorganised emissions account for **more than 50% of total particulate emissions**, according to the 2019 joint document from China's five ministries on ultra-low emission in the steel industry. Half of the problem, in other words, is made of dust that never went through a stack at all. That dust is mechanical, coarse, and generated at a known point. It is exactly the target a cannon was designed for.

## What the Water Actually Does

Three things happen when the plume reaches a dust source. They are worth separating, because they need different droplet sizes and different application rates.

**Attachment and settling.** A droplet and a particle that collide become one heavier object. The physics of collision is unglamorous: a large particle is hit head-on by a droplet (impaction); a mid-sized one is caught by the edge of a droplet as the air curves around it (interception); and the smallest ones, which follow the airflow most obediently, are gathered when they drift into a droplet by random molecular motion (diffusion). In all three cases the combined particle grows in mass and settles faster. Airborne dust becomes deposited dust.

**Humidification and agglomeration.** Water vapour raises local relative humidity, and hygroscopic particles take on water and grow. Grown particles collide more readily and clump, and a clump of fine particles settles like a coarse one. This is the only route a fog cannon has to influence the fine end of the spectrum — and it is a real one, though it works through growth, not through chemistry.

**Surface conditioning.** The least discussed and, on most yards, the largest single effect in the numbers. Water that reaches material, a road surface or a working face changes it from something wind and wheels can lift into something they cannot. This is the mechanism that stops the next tonne of dust from ever becoming airborne, and it is invisible on a specification sheet.

Which of the three dominates depends on the site. On an open stockyard, surface conditioning carries most of the benefit. At a demolition face, it is airborne capture. At a conveyor transfer point, neither applies — the correct tool is a dry fog system, not a cannon, and its droplet band is measured in single microns.

## The One Number That Decides Whether It Works

Droplet size is the number that separates a working installation from an expensive water sprinkler, and it is the number most often missing from a product page.

The governing principle: **capture efficiency is best when droplets and particles are of comparable size.** A droplet two or three times the diameter of the particle it is aimed at collides with it efficiently. A droplet an order of magnitude larger does something unhelpful — it pushes the air in front of it as it falls, and fine particles ride that displaced air around the droplet instead of into it. A 1 mm droplet is an excellent way to wet a road surface and a poor way to capture PM10.

| What you are aiming at | Typical particle size | Droplet band that works | Correct tool |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Visible dust plume, coarse yard dust | largely above 50 µm | 100–200 µm | Cannon, high flow, low pressure |
| PM10 from traffic, demolition, material handling | 10–100 µm | 10–150 µm | Standard cannon, spectrum centred lower |
| Fine primary particulate and odour-carrying aerosols | under 2.5 µm | 1–10 µm | Dry fog system at the generation point |
| Secondary PM2.5: sulphate, nitrate, organics | under 1 µm, formed in the atmosphere | — | Not a capture target. Source control, not spraying |

Read the table from the bottom. The last row is the honest boundary of the technology, and stating it plainly is more persuasive to a technical buyer than any claim.

There is a second droplet fact that catches out even experienced operators: **the droplet you make is not always the droplet that arrives.** In hot, dry air a small droplet evaporates within seconds, and the far end of the plume is dry air and nothing else. Effective throw in an arid inland basin can be less than half the figure quoted for a humid coastal site. That is not a defect — it is the physics, and it is why a throw distance is meaningless without a stated wind speed, humidity and elevation angle.

## Six Applications Where the Money Is Well Spent

### City roads and the dust that comes back every morning

Road dust is a reservoir, not a source. It is deposited overnight by traffic, tyres, brakes, construction spills and wind, and re-entrained the next day. The effect of a single pass of a fog cannon truck is temporary, and honest operators say so. What the machine does well is knock the load down before the morning peak and wet the surface so that less of it lifts.

One thing has to be said plainly, because it is the most common way a municipal road programme wastes its water: **a cannon moves dust from the air to the ground, and only cleaning moves it off the ground.** Spray without sweeping puts the same material back into the air by mid-afternoon, and the morning's water has bought a few hours of quiet and nothing else. Road suppression therefore has to be run as one operation with the sweeper fleet — the cannon or the misting line wets the surface and brings the airborne fraction down, and a wet-type street sweeper follows on the same shift to collect and remove what has just landed. Run the front half only and you have moved the dust from one place to another. The same logic applies at a construction gate: suppress the plume but let trucks track mud onto the public road and you have solved half a problem.

### Provincial highways and haul roads: you cannot keep running trucks up and down

On a long, heavily trafficked road that carries construction materials, dust is not an event, it is a continuous condition. A water truck or a cannon truck has to refill, has to be scheduled, and occupies the carriageway while it works; by the time the second pass arrives, the dust from the first has already lifted again — and the diesel, water and labour do not scale with the length of the road. The machine is not the problem. A cyclic operation simply cannot match a continuous source.

The answer is to put the spraying into the road itself. **Pole-mounted misting lines** mount high on the street-lighting columns or on dedicated poles, at 25–50 m spacing in a continuous run, and hold a mist curtain along the carriageway. **Section-level air quality monitors** then give each stretch its own PM10, PM2.5 and wind readings, and the misting on that stretch is triggered, dosed and timed from its own measured data — so the dust is intercepted above the road surface as it starts to move, instead of being chased after it has already spread. Compared with a mobile cannon, the mist is deliberately finer: this configuration wins by being continuously present rather than by throwing water a long way. In a recent municipal deployment in Longhua, Shenzhen, that meant 70 pole-mounted units across six sub-districts at 25 m or 50 m spacing, with six particulate monitoring points and 24 video channels linked to automatic start and stop. Nobody stands next to it, the water demand is a fraction of a cyclic truck operation, and the output is continuous, demand-driven and stable over the long run.

The form matters here. A mobile cannon on a municipal chassis covers a moving problem; a pole-mounted misting line covers a street or an arterial stretch continuously and unattended. Several Chinese cities run both — mobile units on the main corridors, fixed misting lines along the stretches where resuspension is worst, the fixed line for coverage and the mobile units for the gaps. What does not work is a plain water truck at high flow: it produces slurry, the slurry dries into a crust, and the crust grinds back into the air within a day.

### Demolition and construction sites

This is the application with the clearest regulatory pressure and the clearest visible result. A demolition face releases dust in bursts, controlled by wind direction, and the operator can see whether the plume is being suppressed. The variables that decide success are placement and timing rather than machine size — a smaller cannon positioned on the downwind side and started before the wall comes down beats a larger one started afterwards. Site hoarding, wetting of debris before movement and a wheel-wash at the gate belong to the same system; a cannon that suppresses the plume but lets trucks track dust onto the public road has solved half the problem.

### Open storage yards: coal, ore, aggregate, sand

The largest single market for this equipment, and the one where surface conditioning dominates. A fixed cannon with oscillation covers a defined area on a duty cycle, and the real engineering question is coverage geometry against stockpile shape, not peak throw. Yard dust is also where an anti-smog gun configured for area duty is genuinely the right tool — and where the payback is measured in material not lost to the wind as much as in compliance.

### Waste transfer stations and landfill sites

Dust here comes with odour, and the same machine handles both. Water alone suppresses the dust; dosing a permitted agent into the spray line adds odour control. Tower-mounted cannons are the common configuration, because the sources sit below grade and the plume has to reach down into them. Atomisation quality matters more than reach here — you are treating an enclosed working face, not a landscape.

### Industrial plant boundaries and fugitive emissions

Steel works, foundries, cement, power stations, port stockyards. The sources are many and scattered, and the cost of manning them individually is what pushes the market toward automation. The current generation of installations uses video to identify dust and vehicle movement and to trigger the cannons covering the affected area, which turns a set of individually controlled machines into one closed loop. Where a plant boundary abuts housing, noise becomes a design constraint alongside throw — an urban installation is judged at 20 m, not at 200 m.

### The adjacent benefit that is real but different: heat

Mist reduces local air temperature, and roof- and mast-mounted units are widely used over public squares, transit interchanges and plant walkways. It is a genuine effect and a genuine selling point. It is also a different benefit from dust suppression and should be argued separately: a buyer choosing a machine for cooling has different priorities from one choosing for PM10, and bundling the two claims weakens both.

## Where a Fog Cannon Will Not Help

This section is more useful than the previous one.

**Gaseous precursors.** NOₓ, SO₂, VOCs. No water-based device removes them. If the local air-quality problem is driven by vehicle and industrial emissions, a cannon is not the answer.

**Secondary PM2.5.** It forms in the atmosphere, downwind, over hours. There is nothing at street level to capture.

**City-scale air quality index claims.** This is the crux of the anti-smog gun debate in India, and it deserves a straight answer. A cannon reduces particulate concentrations **in and immediately downwind of its plume**, and above all on the ground surface it treats. It does not lower a city-wide average, and a fleet of them does not substitute for controlling emissions. Used honestly — on identified hotspots, on days and in wind conditions where it will work, against coarse dust — it delivers a measurable local improvement. Sold as a general remedy for a metropolitan AQI problem, it will be judged a failure within a season.

**Stack plumes.** A cannon sprays a jet with an upward component, not a buoyant rising plume. Hot, high, buoyant release points are for collection and filtration.

**Overspray.** Water is not free of consequences. Too much of it on a haul road produces slurry that hides potholes and loses traction; on a public road it creates ice in winter and slip risk at any temperature; on adjacent steel structures it accelerates corrosion; near electrical equipment it is a hazard; and in a region with a constrained water supply it is a cost that has to be justified per cubic metre. Every one of those is a design input, not a footnote.

## From a Purchase to a Working System

The difference between installations that work and installations that disappoint is rarely the machine. It is the sequence of decisions made before it was ordered.

**Map the sources before choosing the equipment.** Every source on site, its geometry, whether it is airborne or on a surface, and what drives it — wind, wheels or a drop height. This list is the specification. Almost every undersized or oversized installation I have seen was a machine chosen before that list existed.

**Decide the mechanism.** Airborne capture or surface conditioning? Airborne means droplets matched to particle size, positioned downwind, triggered with the source. Surface conditioning means coverage geometry, duty cycle and application rate per square metre. They are different machines, with different nozzle arrangements and different control logic.

**Then size for throw, under conditions.** Ask for throw at a stated wind speed and elevation angle, and ask what fraction of the plume is still liquid at the far end. A throw figure without conditions is a marketing figure.

**Then solve water.** Flow rate, supply pressure available on site, water quality and filtration — how many filtration stages, how easily the elements are changed, how often the nozzles have to be cleaned — and whether the site intends to run on recycled water. Filtration stages and automatic backwash are the difference between a machine that runs for years on site water with a stable droplet spectrum, and one that is cleaned weekly by hand while its nozzles drift further out of specification.

**Then solve control.** Remote control is not automation. Real automation decides *when* to spray and how much, using dust monitoring, weather data or machine vision. On a plant with many sources and no operator, this is what makes a fleet viable — and it is what converts a comparison against a competitor's unit price into a comparison against a different class of system.

**Then solve the two seasonal risks.** Freezing in winter and evaporation in summer. Both are design decisions taken at order stage: trace heating, insulation and drain points for one; droplet spectrum, machine count and placement for the other.

## Six Questions That Expose an Inflated Specification

1. **Throw at what wind speed, elevation angle and supply pressure — and what is the residual droplet density at the far end?** Change the test conditions on one machine and the range moves by more than 30%.
2. **What is the Dv50**, not the minimum droplet size? The median diameter governs capture efficiency, and the width of the distribution matters almost as much.
3. **Coverage area — projected or effective capture — and at what wind speed?** Then: **how many litres per square metre** does that imply? That last figure is the operating cost.
4. **Is "automatic" remote control, or a closed loop on dust monitoring?** The gap between the two is the entire labour cost of the installation.
5. **What is the sound pressure level at 20 m?** For any installation near housing this is a permitting question, not a comfort question.
6. **How often do the nozzles need cleaning, how hard is that to do, and does it mean shutting the machine down? How many stages of filtration are there, and how easy are the elements to change?** Site water usually carries sand and silt, and nozzle wear and blockage are the commonest cause of an installation silently degrading between year one and year three. A long cleaning interval, an awkward strip-down or too few filtration stages — and two years in, the spray leaving the nozzles is no longer the spray the machine was specified with.

Keep these two statements **separate**, because tender documents constantly merge them: "one-year warranty with lifetime after-sales support" is a commercial commitment; "design service life of more than 10 years under proper maintenance" is an engineering statement that holds only if wear parts are replaced on schedule, water quality stays within the agreed range, and winter anti-freeze procedures are followed.

## Two Words, and Why the Local One Wins the Enquiry

Back to the naming. `Fog cannon` is the globally recognised term and the one most buyers search for. `Anti-smog gun` is what the same machine is called in India and much of South Asia, where regulation and public debate treat it as a device against smog rather than against dust — and where search volume is far higher. `Dust suppression cannon` and `dust control cannon` are the words that appear in UK, EU and North American engineering documents and rental fleets.

For an exporter the practical rule is simple: **use the word the buyer's market uses, and keep the engineering content identical.** Three terms to avoid, all of which cause commercial confusion rather than merely sounding wrong — `water cannon`, which in English means a fire monitor or a riot-control vehicle; `sprayer`, which is agricultural and matches a different customs description; and `fog machine`, which is a stage-effect device.

One more detail worth knowing, because it settles arguments about whether "fog" is serious engineering language: the customs nomenclature under which these machines are declared — HS 8424.8999.90 — describes them as *projecting or fog spraying mechanical appliances*. Even the tariff schedule says fog. And the machinery industry standard for this product family, **JB/T 13562-2018**, carries the formal English title *Air-assisted spraying device for dust suppression* — the name to use in drawings, technical volumes and tender submissions.
