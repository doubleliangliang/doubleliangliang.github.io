Cement plant fugitive dust is normally treated as an engineering problem. It is not. It is a compliance problem that has an engineering answer, and the compliance question comes first, because it decides which answer is the right one.

The reason is simple and regularly underestimated. The same plume — leaving a transfer tower, rising off a stockpile, trailing behind a haul truck — is described, judged and evidenced in completely different ways depending on where the project is built and who is paying for it. A control measure that satisfies one framework can be invisible to another. A technical proposal written in the vocabulary of one system can be read as non-responsive in a second without a single engineering fault being found, and a supplier can lose a tender for reasons that never appear in the technical evaluation.

This article is for the people who have to answer for a cement project's dust: the plant engineer preparing a permit application, the project team assembling a tender, the supplier whose proposal has to survive three different reviewers. It works through four rulebooks, the four questions they all ask underneath their different language, and the four tools that answer them. The kiln line, the cement mill and the coal system appear only where they mark a boundary — and in a cement plant that boundary matters more than it does anywhere else in heavy industry, because cement is the one bulk product that hardens when you add water to it.

## Four Rulebooks, Four Ways to Be Judged

Four frameworks cover most of the ground, and they do not agree on how to describe the problem even where they agree on the physics.

**The European Union.** The controlling instrument is the Industrial Emissions Directive (2010/75/EU). For cement, lime and magnesium oxide, the BAT conclusions are set out in Commission Implementing Decision 2013/163/EU, and they address diffuse dust directly rather than in passing. BAT 14 covers diffuse dust from dusty operations and lists the accepted techniques: a simple and linear site layout; good housekeeping of buildings and roads with proper and complete maintenance; watering of raw material piles; enclosure or encapsulation of dusty operations such as grinding and screening; covered conveyors and elevators built as closed systems; storage silos of adequate capacity fitted with filters for the air displaced during filling; a circulation process in preference to a once-through arrangement for pneumatic conveying; reduction of air leakage and spillage points; automatic devices and control systems; and continuous, trouble-free operation. BAT 15 extends the same logic to bulk storage. Alongside those, the conclusions call for paved lorry areas, wetting of roads especially in dry weather, and vacuum cleaning rather than dry sweeping where dust has accumulated.

Two features of that list are easy to miss and highly consequential. **Automatic devices and control systems are themselves a listed technique.** Not an accessory bolted onto a water system, but a recognised means of preventing diffuse dust — which means that in a European permit an instrumented control layer is not an upgrade to be traded away in a value-engineering round. It is part of the answer. And the list is written as prevention and containment first, with watering appearing as one item among many rather than as the default.

**The United States.** For crushing, screening, conveying, bagging and loading the framework is the New Source Performance Standards for nonmetallic mineral processing plants, 40 CFR Part 60 Subpart OOO. It sets fugitive emission limits by opacity: for sources built on or after 22 April 2008, 7 percent opacity generally and 12 percent for a crusher without a capture system, with buildings enclosing affected facilities held to 7 percent opacity and 5 g/dscm. Compliance is determined visually, by a trained observer using EPA Method 9.

Two consequences follow. A visible criterion becomes legally enforceable — which is why the same document requires periodic inspection of water sprays, and grants a testing exemption to facilities controlled by water carryover from those inspected sprays. That is an explicit acknowledgement in a federal standard that water suppression is the recognised technique for fugitive points. And rules of this kind routinely exempt *uncombined water* from the opacity reading, which is the direct regulatory reason why any automatic monitoring has to be able to separate dust from steam and water vapour, rather than simply measuring whiteness.

**Lenders and international projects.** Project-financed capacity frequently has to meet the IFC Performance Standards and the World Bank Group EHS Guidelines for Cement and Lime Manufacturing from the first day of operation, because the lender requires it. Those guidelines set emission levels for the kiln and for other point sources including clinker cooling and cement grinding, and they single out exposure to fine particulates and to crystalline silica as a principal occupational hazard of the sector. The practical difference from a national permit is timing and documentation: the obligation attaches before commissioning, and it is evidenced through a management system rather than a stack test.

**China.** The cement sector there is run under a nationwide emissions programme with a visible criterion for fugitive sources — no visible dust or fume escaping at dust-generating points, and a tidy plant with no accumulated dust — supported by technical guidance on enclosed storage and conveying and on wheel and body washing at yard exits. This is the framework most likely to appear in a supplier's reference list, and the one least transferable by default to a project elsewhere, because it is built around a national programme rather than a permit-by-permit process.

Four vocabularies. One engineering reality. That is the reason for reading them together rather than one at a time.

## Reading the Four Frameworks Side by Side

Set the four against each other and the differences turn out to be mostly about how the obligation is written, not about what actually reduces dust.

| Framework | The instrument | How the obligation is judged | What it implies for equipment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| European Union | IED 2010/75/EU, with BAT conclusions 2013/163/EU | Against a list of accepted techniques, plus a general duty to prevent and contain | Techniques rather than numbers — and automatic control is itself one of the techniques |
| United States | 40 CFR Part 60 Subpart OOO | Visually, by opacity, using Method 9 | Water sprays are named as a control technique and carry an inspection requirement |
| Lenders (IFC / World Bank) | Performance Standards and sector EHS Guidelines | Against a management system, from before commissioning | Documentation and evidence carry as much weight as the hardware |
| China | National emissions programme for the sector | Against a visible criterion: no visible dust escaping, no accumulated dust | Enclosed storage and conveying, plus wheel and body wash at the exits |

The table is more useful read downward than across. Every one of the four ends up asking the same four things: bring the dust down across open ground, lock it down at enclosed points, stop it leaving the site on wheels, and show that all of it happened. What differs is which of the four gets the attention. A European reviewer looks for containment and control first. An American inspector stands at the fence line with a Method 9 form. A lender asks for the evidence trail. A Chinese programme looks for the visible result.

A proposal that answers only the first of the four has answered a quarter of the question, and it will read as incomplete in three of the four jurisdictions.

## Four Obligations, Not Ninety Sources

Three properties make this problem resistant to the usual approach of buying a machine and pointing it at the dust.

It is **distributed** — a cement works may hold dozens of genuine emission points across several square kilometres. It is **intermittent** — a truck tips for forty seconds, a loader works a pile for three minutes, and the visible plume is gone before anyone reaches for a control. And it is **unmeasured** — a stack has a monitor attached, while most open sources have nothing at all, so without a baseline there is no way to demonstrate that anything improved.

The remedy is not a larger machine. It is to stop counting sources and start grouping the obligations they create. Four obligations cover a cement plant, and each one maps to a class of tool rather than to a product.

| The obligation | Where it arises | What settles it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bring the dust down across open ground | Quarry benches, the crushing apron, raw material and additive yards, clinker piles, haul and plant roads | Area suppression — a long-range fog cannon |
| Lock the dust down at the point it is generated | Enclosed transfers and loading skirts, crusher hoods and screens, the raw mill system, silo and hopper charging points | Point suppression — a dry fog system |
| Stop the dust leaving the site on wheels | The site exit, and the public road immediately beyond it | Carry-out control — a wheel wash station |
| Demonstrate that all of the above happened | Every zone, continuously, for the period the permit requires | The control layer |

Three boundary cases belong in the same conversation, because stating them is part of the answer. The kiln line and cooler are a channelled source with a stack, already handled by bag filtration and continuous monitoring. Everything downstream of the cement mill is handled by enclosure, inward negative pressure and extraction rather than by water. And the coal system is a protection question before it is a suppression question. A proposal that claims all four obligations can be settled everywhere on the site has not understood the site.

## The One Property That Makes Cement Different

Everything above would also be true of a coal terminal or a steel stockyard. Cement adds one property that redraws the map, and it has nothing to do with the dust itself. It is about the product.

Ordinary Portland cement is a hydraulic binder: it begins to hydrate on contact with water and will set in air or under water. Finished cement is therefore specified with a tight moisture ceiling — typically required to leave the mill below roughly 0.5 percent. Anything above it appears as caking on silo walls, blocked discharge chutes, poor flow in air slides, slipping belts, and a product that no longer meets its declared strength class. The industry term is **pre-hydration**, and it is a quality problem, not a dust problem.

Upstream of the cement mill that ceiling is never in play. Quarry benches, the crushing apron, the raw material and additive yards, clinker storage and the road network all handle coarse material that will shortly be ground and burned at more than 1,400 °C. Water added there is evaporated or driven off in the kiln, and a fog cannon or a road-watering programme is a legitimate tool. Downstream of the mill the position reverses completely: the mill itself, the silos, the packers, the bulk and bag loading heads and the air slides all handle the finished product, and dust there is fine, sticky and already saleable. Adding water does not suppress it; it downgrades it.

The direction of travel inside the process points the same way. Water injection into the mill and spraying of the mill shell were once used to control temperature; where cement moisture matters more than the temperature benefit, the industry now prefers better mill ventilation, reduced over-grinding and burnishing the grinding charge. Even inside the mill, the movement is away from water.

## Area Suppression: Bringing a Plume Down Across Open Ground

A fog cannon pressurises water through a ring of nozzles and accelerates the spray with an axial fan. Capture is dominated by inertial collision and gravitational settling, effective distance runs from tens of metres to a few hundred, and the unit can be mounted on a tower so that no operator has to stand in the dust to run it.

Its place in the rulebooks is unusually well established. Watering of raw material piles is a named technique in the European BAT list. In the United States, the inspection requirement attached to water sprays under Subpart OOO amounts to a federal standard recognising water as the control for fugitive points. The measure itself is not controversial. What is contested is whether it works at a particular site — and three characteristics decide that, none of which appear on a specification sheet.

**Reach is a still-air figure.** Published throw distances are measured in calm conditions, without obstruction, at one droplet size. In a working yard a crosswind shortens effective reach and pushes the plume off target. Mounting height, overlap between adjacent units and wind-aware presets matter more than the headline number, and a layout in which any single unit can fail without leaving a gap is more robust than one that depends on a hero machine in the corner of the yard.

**Droplet size has to match the dust, and the match is not universal.** Coarse spray above roughly 200 microns brings a visible plume down over a stockpile effectively but does very little to respirable particles. Fine mist in the 10 to 50 micron band is the right regime for PM10 and PM2.5, and it only works when the nozzle is close to the source, because fine droplets lose momentum and evaporate over a long throw. That is precisely why a long-range cannon and a dry fog system are complements rather than substitutes for one another.

**Water is a budget item, not a free resource.** Every litre applied to a raw material yard either evaporates, drains or enters the material, and in a cement plant the last of those has to be agreed with the people running the raw mill and the kiln rather than decided by the environmental department alone. Fix flow rates after the acceptable moisture increment is agreed, and treat the winter case as a design condition: a system that has to be bypassed in the coldest month has an availability problem, not a winter problem.

One rule on the roads is worth repeating because it is the step most often skipped. **Water brings dust down; it does not remove it.** Particles that settle on a haul road are re-suspended by the next vehicle, so without a mechanical sweeper working the same route on a defined schedule, the suppression system is moving dust from the air to the ground and back again. Cannon plus sweeper is one measure, not two.

## Point Suppression: Locking a Source Down Where It Starts

A dry fog system atomises compressed air and low-pressure water together inside the nozzle, producing droplets typically in the 1 to 10 micron band. Capture is dominated by Brownian diffusion and agglomeration rather than gravity, effective distance is a few metres because the nozzle sits at the emission point, and moisture added to the material is measured in fractions of a percent.

Its regulatory position is the mirror image of the cannon's. Where the cannon answers the watering clause in the BAT list, dry fog answers the clause next to it — enclosure or encapsulation of dusty operations — by making a contained source behave. The locations it fits share three properties: the dust is fine, the source is fixed and confined, and the belt or the product cannot tolerate added water. Belt transfer points and loading skirts, crusher feed and discharge openings, vibrating screens, chute loading points, bucket elevators and silo charging points all qualify.

There is one non-negotiable precondition, and it is the one most often missing from a proposal. **Dry fog requires containment.** Used in the open, the fog disperses and the dust does not move; the equipment runs, consumes air and water, and achieves nothing measurable. That is why dry fog design starts with the enclosure, the skirting and the extraction, and only then considers nozzles. When a supplier proposes a dry fog system for an unenclosed transfer or an open apron, the correct next question is what will contain it.

The strategic value of the two measures together is coverage. A cannon aimed at a transfer tower sprays water into the open air beside the tower, not into the chute. Dry fog inside the enclosure, plus a cannon on the open yard and the apron, covers the whole material handling route — and each one is doing the job the other cannot.

## Carry-Out Control: The Obligation at the Gate

Wheel wash is usually the last measure considered and the first cited in a compliance review, because its absence is visible from outside the fence. Material deposited on a haul road adheres to tyres and undercarriage; a vehicle leaving the site transfers it to the public road; the deposit dries, traffic re-suspends it, and the resulting particulate is attributed to the plant no matter how well the stockyard itself is managed.

It is also the obligation with the clearest paper trail. The European BAT conclusions for steel name *wheel-cleaning equipment* explicitly, alongside hard-surfaced transport roads, road cleaning and sheeting of loads. The cement conclusions approach the same problem through paved lorry areas, road wetting and housekeeping. National quarry and materials-handling permits in several jurisdictions go further, treating the wheel and body wash as a required installation at yard exits rather than an optional accessory, often specifying that it be sited immediately at the exit and that the wash water be captured or treated rather than allowed to run off.

The engineering content is larger than the name suggests, and four things decide whether a station works.

**Coverage.** Cleaning the tyres alone leaves the undercarriage and lower body, which carry a substantial share of the load. A useful installation addresses the full footprint of the vehicle — sills, chassis, mudflap areas and the underside, where deposits accumulate and are hardest to reach.

**Water capture and reuse.** A wheel wash that discharges onto the ground at the gate has relocated the problem by a few metres. The station needs collection and treatment — settlement stages, recycling, or discharge to a treatment facility — and it needs a routine for the sludge, because that sludge is the material that was being prevented from leaving. A closed arrangement in which wash water passes through settlement and returns to a clean-water tank turns the station from a consumable into a recycling loop.

**Free drainage.** A vehicle that leaves the gate still shedding water deposits a wet trail on the public road, which is a nuisance in dry weather and a safety hazard when it freezes. A drainage or forced-drying stage after the wash, and heat tracing of the pipework in cold climates, are part of the specification rather than additions to it.

**Wrong-way traffic.** The station only works if vehicles actually pass through it. Physical arrangement, signage and site discipline matter as much as the nozzle layout, and on larger sites a single exit is often not enough.

Wheel wash is not an air quality device. It is a materials containment device, and it is most effective when it is designed together with the road cleaning schedule, the surface hardening programme and the site traffic plan.

## The Control Layer: The Obligation to Produce Evidence

Every framework above eventually asks a version of the same question: how do you know, and what can you show? That is the obligation the control layer discharges, and it is the reason a control layer is not a luxury item in three of the four jurisdictions.

The European BAT list settles the point directly by naming automatic devices and control systems as a technique. American compliance is visual, which makes a continuous camera record the most natural evidence there is. A lender's management system asks for a documented, auditable process. Only the cheapest arrangement — a timer and a contactor, spraying the yard for five minutes every half hour whether or not anything is moving — has nothing to offer in any of the three: it spends water and power on an empty yard, it is too wet in one season and frozen in another, and when a review asks what happened during a particular shift last month, it has no answer.

What replaces it is not a bigger timer. It is an event-driven loop, and the functions it has to perform are the ones a plant already performs manually.

It has to **watch the sources** rather than the skyline — cameras at the actual emission points, particulate monitors at hot spots and at the boundary, and a compact weather station supplying wind speed, wind direction, temperature and humidity. The wind data is not decoration: it determines whether a spray programme lands on the source or drifts onto a road, a workshop or a neighbouring property, and the BAT framework for bulk materials explicitly relies on combining monitor data with wind direction to work out which sources are driving the ambient readings.

It has to **recognise what it is seeing**. Vision models must detect plumes and — critically — separate them from steam and water vapour, the single most common false-alarm source in a cement plant, since clinker cooling, hot material and wet processes all produce white plumes that are not dust. A second class of models recognises the operation itself: a truck tipping, a loader moving, a belt running. Recognising the operation allows suppression to begin before the plume is visible rather than after it has dispersed.

It has to **size its response**. A binary alarm produces either nuisance triggering or missed events. A graded response — a short local burst at the lowest level, the full preset for the zone at the next, and escalation to the control room with the image attached at the highest — is what makes the system tolerable to live with, and it is also what stops a stuck valve from running a pump for an entire shift.

It has to **act on the right zone and nothing else**, on stored presets for azimuth, pitch, flow, droplet size and duration, through a gateway that speaks the plant's own protocol — dry contact, Modbus or MQTT — with a proper handshake to the control system, so that operators keep authority over their area rather than losing it to a black box.

It has to **check whether it worked**, using the same sensors that raised the alarm, and escalate rather than spray indefinitely when a plume does not clear in the expected window.

And it has to **leave a record**: zone status, alarm lists, equipment availability, paired images, water and power consumption, and the compliance records a reviewer will ask for, exported on a schedule and retained for the period the permit requires.

Two rules separate a system that survives from one that is muted within a month. Thresholds must be set **per zone**, because an alarm level that makes sense at a raw material yard is meaningless inside a crushing station; and a commissioning period on site is a normal part of delivery, not evidence that the product is unfinished. Two to four weeks of tuning per plant should be costed as scope.

The most useful measure is also the most direct: **visible dust-escape events per week, per zone.** It is a number an operations manager can act on, a number a community liaison officer can report, and — where an opacity or visible-emission limit applies — a number that maps straight onto the regulatory criterion.

## Where Water Is the Wrong Answer

A credible programme states its limits as clearly as its applications, because equipment specified into the wrong scenario is not merely ineffective: it gets switched off, and the conclusion drawn is that dust control does not work.

**Channelled sources belong to capture and filtration, not to spraying.** The kiln, the preheater and the clinker cooler have a duct and a stack, and they already have filters. Spraying water into that gas stream would be pointless at best.

**The finished-cement section is closed to water**, as set out above, and the measure that serves it is enclosure, inward negative pressure and bag filtration. Where a loading head needs dust knocked down right at the spout, the small quantity of mist involved has to evaporate before it lands, be interlocked with the loading action, apply only when a vehicle is actually being loaded, and be agreed with the people who own the product specification — because at that point the water quantity is a process parameter, not an environmental setting.

**The coal system is a protection problem.** A suspended coal dust cloud is itself the hazard. Housekeeping practice matters as much as equipment: blowing accumulated dust off with compressed air is precisely the action that creates the cloud an explosion needs, so wet or vacuum methods are used instead. Ex configurations can be provided to suit the customer's application; the specific protection type and certificate are supplied with the project. Layer control, temperature and carbon monoxide monitoring, and correct electrical protection belong in the same package. Explosion protection and mining approvals are separate regimes and should be verified separately.

Stating these limits is not a disclaimer. It is the reason the rest of the proposal can be trusted.

## Reading a Proposal Against the Rulebook

Most sourcing in this sector is still decided on three columns — throw distance, flow rate, price — none of which predicts what the equipment will cost to own, and none of which answers any of the four rulebooks.

Read a proposal the way each reviewer will. A European reviewer is looking for the BAT technique list, and will want to see containment and control addressed rather than watering alone. An American reviewer is working to a visible criterion, which puts camera coverage, plume detection and the ability to separate dust from steam near the top of the list. A lender is looking for the management system: who owns each zone, how the thresholds were set, what the records look like. All three will ask what happens after commissioning, because spare-part availability, response arrangements and a documented winter procedure decide the annual availability figure.

Three things separate suppliers, whatever the jurisdiction: whether the machine was designed as one system or assembled from parts; how long the supplier has actually built for cement, mining and bulk material handling, and what those installations look like after several years of abrasive dust and continuous duty; and what happens after commissioning, since a settled product platform with a long supply commitment lets a plant build a maintenance routine while a design revised every year does not. Experience shows up in details — whether a manifold can be drained, whether a nozzle can be changed without dismantling the spray ring, whether a control cabinet sits somewhere it will not flood.

Part of the picture cannot be verified on site however the questions are framed. Droplet size cannot be measured in a working plant and drifts as nozzles wear, scale and filters load. Noise and vibration differences are not audible on a new machine and only accumulate under continuous duty. Throw distance is a design figure measured under defined conditions, and no mandatory test regime applies to it — which is precisely why an industry technical standard, such as JB/T 13562-2018 *Technical specification for air-assisted spray dust suppression devices*, is more useful at tender stage than a brochure number. Asking under what conditions a figure was obtained is more informative than comparing the figure itself.

The cost of getting this wrong is not theoretical. A unit that falls short in effective reach or spray distribution does not fix itself with a settings change; the remedy is additional units, new foundations and a second round of shutdown coordination. An unreliable unit produces repeated repairs in an area that is already dusty and has poor visibility, which is a safety question before it is anything else. What becomes visible, after three years, is which supplier's equipment is still running and which has become a maintenance item.

## Where This Leaves a Cement Project

The four rulebooks differ in language, in the evidence they accept and in where they place the emphasis. They converge on the same four obligations, and those obligations map onto four tools that do genuinely different jobs: one covers an area, one locks a point, one closes the gate, and one makes the whole thing provable.

The useful question for a project is therefore not which of the four matters most — all four are asked — but which side of the hydration boundary each proposed measure falls on, and which obligation it is actually answering. A proposal that can answer that question zone by zone will survive a European permit review, an American visible-emission inspection and a lender's due diligence without having to be rewritten for each.
