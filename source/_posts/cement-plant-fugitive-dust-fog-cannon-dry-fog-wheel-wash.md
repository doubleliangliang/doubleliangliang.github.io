Cement plants have a stack, and in most of them the stack is no longer the problem. Fabric filters and electrostatic precipitators on the kiln and the clinker cooler, continuous monitoring at the main outlet, maintenance regimes that have been running for two decades — the channelled emission is mature engineering, delivered by vendors with reference lists and defended with data that stands up in a review.

The dust that still draws complaints, and that still moves a boundary monitor, rarely passes a sampling port at all. It leaves a quarry bench, a tipping apron, a stockpile, a transfer point or a haul road. European regulators call it **diffuse dust**; in North America the usual term is **fugitive dust**; both describe the same thing — particulate released from an operation without passing through a controlled point of discharge.

This article deals with one question and four answers. **What actually reduces fugitive dust in a cement plant, and where does each tool belong?** The tools are long-range fog cannons, dry fog systems, wheel wash stations and the control layer that decides when the other three run. The kiln line, the cement mill and the coal system appear only where they mark a boundary — and in a cement plant that boundary matters more than it does anywhere else in heavy industry, because cement is the one bulk product that hardens when you add water to it.

## Why the Stack Is Not the Whole Story

Three structural properties explain why fugitive dust outlives the stack problem, and why the usual approach — buy a machine, point it at the dust — underperforms.

**It is distributed.** A cement works may hold dozens of genuine emission points across several square kilometres: a limestone quarry, a crushing and screening station, raw material and additive yards, the raw mill and its silos, the kiln line and cooler, clinker storage, the cement mill and dispatch area, a coal route, and the road network that connects all of them. No single device stands in front of that.

**It is intermittent.** A truck tips for forty seconds. A loader works a pile for three minutes. A gust crosses an open stockpile and the visible plume is gone before anyone reaches for a control. Average-based thinking fails here, because the emission is concentrated into short events.

**It is unmeasured.** This is the most consequential of the three. A stack has a monitor attached; most open sources have nothing at all. Without a baseline there is no way to demonstrate improvement, and without proof a dust programme becomes a matter of opinion.

The result is a familiar pattern: capital is spent, equipment is installed, and at the next review nobody can say what changed. The remedy is not a larger machine. It is to treat fugitive dust as a measurable operating problem rather than an equipment purchase.

## The Language the Regulator Actually Uses

International projects are regularly tripped up by vocabulary. Different jurisdictions frame the same obligation in different words, and a proposal written in the idiom of one system can read as non-responsive in another. Four frameworks cover most of the ground.

In the **European Union**, the controlling instrument is the Industrial Emissions Directive (2010/75/EU). For cement, lime and magnesium oxide the relevant BAT conclusions are Commission Implementing Decision 2013/163/EU, and they address diffuse dust directly rather than in passing. BAT 14 covers diffuse dust from dusty operations and lists the accepted techniques: a simple and linear site layout; good housekeeping of buildings and roads with proper and complete maintenance; watering of raw material piles; enclosure or encapsulation of dusty operations such as grinding and screening; covered conveyors and elevators built as closed systems; storage silos of adequate capacity fitted with filters for the air displaced during filling; a circulation process in preference to a once-through arrangement for pneumatic conveying; reduction of air leakage and spillage points; automatic devices and control systems; and continuous, trouble-free operation. BAT 15 extends the same logic to bulk storage. Alongside them the conclusions call for paved lorry areas, wetting of roads especially in dry weather, and vacuum cleaning rather than dry sweeping where dust has accumulated.

Two features of that list are easy to miss and consequential. First, **automatic devices and control systems are themselves a listed technique** — not an accessory bolted onto a water system, but a recognised means of preventing diffuse dust. Second, the techniques are written as *prevention and containment first*, with watering appearing as one item among many.

In the **United States**, the framework for crushing, screening, conveying, bagging and loading is the New Source Performance Standards for nonmetallic mineral processing plants, 40 CFR Part 60 Subpart OOO. It sets fugitive emission limits by opacity: for sources built on or after 22 April 2008, 7 percent opacity generally and 12 percent for a crusher without a capture system, with buildings enclosing affected facilities held to 7 percent opacity and 5 g/dscm. Compliance is determined visually, by a trained observer using EPA Method 9. Two consequences follow. A visible criterion becomes legally enforceable, which is why the same document requires periodic inspection of water sprays and grants a testing exemption to facilities controlled by water carryover from inspected sprays — an explicit acknowledgement that **water suppression is the recognised technique for fugitive points**. And rules of this kind routinely exempt *uncombined water* from the opacity reading, which is the direct regulatory reason why any automatic monitoring has to be able to separate dust from steam and water vapour.

In **emerging markets**, project-financed capacity frequently has to meet the IFC Performance Standards and the World Bank Group EHS Guidelines for Cement and Lime Manufacturing from the first day of operation, because the lender requires it. Those guidelines set air emission levels for the kiln and for other point sources including clinker cooling and cement grinding, and they single out exposure to fine particulates and to crystalline silica as a principal occupational hazard of the sector.

**China's** cement sector uses a different construction again, built around a nationwide emissions programme with a visible criterion for fugitive sources — no visible dust or fume escaping at dust-generating points — supported by technical guidance on enclosed storage and conveying and on wheel and body washing at yard exits.

Four vocabularies. One engineering reality. Every framework asks the same four things: bring dust down across open areas, lock it down at enclosed points, stop it leaving the site on wheels, and demonstrate that all of the above happened. A proposal that answers only the first has answered a quarter of the question.

## The One Property That Makes Cement Different

Cement's dust is not like the dust in a steel stockyard or a coal terminal, and the difference is not about the dust at all. It is about the product.

Ordinary Portland cement is a hydraulic binder: it begins to hydrate on contact with water and will set in air or under water. Finished cement is therefore specified with a tight moisture ceiling — typically required to leave the mill below roughly 0.5 percent. Anything above that appears as caking on silo walls, blocked discharge chutes, poor flow in air slides, slipping belts, and cement that no longer meets its declared strength class. The industry term is **pre-hydration**, and it is a quality problem, not a dust problem.

This single property redraws the map of where water-based suppression is allowed.

![Where water-based suppression is permitted along a cement plant process chain, and where added water becomes a quality defect](/images/cement-water-boundary-process-chain.svg)

Upstream of the cement mill, the boundary is generous. Quarry benches, the crushing apron, raw material and additive yards, clinker storage and the road network all handle coarse material that will shortly be ground and burned at more than 1,400 °C. Water added there is either evaporated or driven off in the kiln, and a fog cannon or a road-watering programme is a legitimate tool.

Downstream of the cement mill, the position reverses. The cement mill itself, the cement silos, the packers, the bulk and bag loading heads and the air slides all handle the finished product, and the dust there is fine, sticky and already saleable. Adding water to it does not suppress dust; it downgrades material. That part of the plant is served by enclosure, inward negative pressure and bag filtration, with the same principle applied two steps further back in the coal system, where protection is matched to the application rather than to a generic specification.

One more detail is worth stating because it surprises people outside the industry. The sector does not solve the problem by adding water inside the process either. Water injection into the mill and spraying the mill shell were once used to control temperature, and where lowering cement moisture matters more than the temperature benefit, the industry now prefers better mill ventilation, reduced over-grinding and burnishing the grinding charge. Even inside the mill, the direction of travel is away from water.

That is the frame for everything that follows: a cement plant has a line on it, and the useful question about any dust control proposal is which side of the line each measure falls on.

## Nine Scenarios, Not Ninety Sources

The first practical step in any programme is to stop counting sources and start grouping them. A dust inventory that lists every transfer point, hopper lip and loader pass produces a spreadsheet nobody can act on. Group by **the place and the operations that happen there**, and a cement works collapses into nine scenarios.

![Nine fugitive dust scenarios in a cement plant and the measure that matches each](/images/cement-fugitive-dust-scenarios.svg)

| Scenario | What happens there | Matching measure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quarry | Drilling, blasting, loading, haul roads at the face | Long-range fog cannon |
| Crushing station | Open tipping apron in front; hoods, screens and belt transfers behind | Fog cannon on the open apron; dry fog inside the enclosure |
| Raw material and additive yards | Stacking, reclaiming, hopper discharge, truck traffic, yard roads | Long-range fog cannon |
| Raw meal preparation | Mill feed and discharge, separator, silo top, filter discharge | Dry fog |
| Kiln line and cooler | Preheater, rotary kiln, clinker cooler — a channelled source | Enclosure and bag filtration |
| Clinker storage and handling | Silo discharge, stockpiles, belt transfers | Fog cannon at discharge and stockpiles; dry fog at enclosed transfers |
| Cement milling and dispatch | Cement mill, cement silos, packers, loading heads | Not a spray task — enclosure, negative pressure, bag filtration |
| Coal preparation | Coal yard, coal mill, coal silo, coal transport | Fog cannon at the yard; protection matched to the application in the coal system |
| Plant and haul roads | Re-entrained dust under repeated vehicle traffic | Fog cannon plus mechanical sweeping |

The distribution is the point. **Five scenarios take a fog cannon** — the quarry, the crushing station's open apron, the raw material and additive yards, clinker storage, and the road network. What they share is that they are open or semi-open, the material is coarse, and the dust is driven by wind and vehicles rather than sitting in a duct.

**Four scenarios take dry fog** — the enclosed parts of crushing and screening, transfer points and bucket elevators, the raw mill system and raw meal silo tops, and enclosed batching and weighing. What they share is a confined space and dust that is extremely fine and sticky.

**Three scenarios take neither, and saying so is part of the answer.** The kiln line is a channelled source with a stack, already handled by bag filtration. Everything downstream of the cement mill is handled by enclosure and extraction. Coal preparation is a protection question before it is a suppression question.

Note also the order of work inside the crushing station. The chute, the receiving pit and the hood come first. A cannon aimed at a crusher that has not been enclosed is being asked to compensate for missing steelwork, and it will not succeed.

## Four Tools, Four Different Jobs

The mistake that costs the most money in this field is treating the available equipment as one product family, ranked by size. It is not a family. It is four functions, and only one of them involves a decision about how much water goes into the air.

![Four distinct roles in cement plant fugitive dust control: area suppression, point suppression, carry-out control and the control layer](/images/cement-four-tools-roles.svg)

**Area suppression** is the long-range fog cannon. It projects a large volume of atomised water across open ground and brings a visible plume down over an area. It belongs on stockyards, the open tipping apron at a crushing station, clinker piles and haul roads.

**Point suppression** is the dry fog system. It atomises compressed air and water together at the nozzle, producing droplets in the micron range that cause fine particles to agglomerate and settle without wetting the product or the belt. It operates inside an enclosure, at a fixed source, at distances measured in metres.

**Carry-out control** is the wheel wash station. It has nothing to do with air. Its job is to stop the dust already deposited on a vehicle and its tyres from being carried out of the gate, where it dries, is re-suspended by traffic, and is attributed to the plant.

**The control layer** decides when the other three run, and produces the evidence that they did. It is the difference between owning equipment and operating a system.

A cement works usually needs all four. The proportions differ by site, and the sequence in which they are applied matters more than the quantity of any single one.

## Fog Cannons: Bringing a Plume Down Across Open Ground

A fog cannon pressurises water through a ring of nozzles and accelerates the resulting spray with an axial fan. Depending on nozzle selection and operating pressure, droplets range from tens to a few hundred microns. Capture is dominated by inertial collision and gravitational settling, effective distance runs from tens of metres to a few hundred, and the unit can be mounted on a tower so that no operator has to stand in the dust to run it.

Three characteristics decide whether a cannon performs in a working plant rather than on a test pad.

**Reach is a still-air figure.** Published throw distances are measured in calm conditions, without obstruction, at one droplet size. In a working yard a crosswind noticeably shortens effective reach and pushes the plume off target. Mounting height, overlap between adjacent units and wind-aware presets matter more than the headline number. A layout in which any single unit can fail without leaving a gap is more robust than one that depends on a hero machine at the corner of the yard.

**Droplet size has to match the dust, and the match is not universal.** Coarse spray above roughly 200 microns brings a visible plume down over a stockpile effectively but does very little to respirable particles. Fine mist in the 10 to 50 micron band is the correct regime for PM10 and PM2.5, and it only works when the nozzle is close to the source, because fine droplets lose momentum and evaporate over a long throw. This is precisely why long-range cannons and dry fog systems are complements rather than substitutes.

**Water is a budget item, not a free resource.** Every litre applied to a raw material yard either evaporates, drains or enters the material. In a cement plant the last of those has to be agreed with the people running the raw mill and the kiln, not decided by the environmental department alone. Fix flow rates after the acceptable moisture increment is agreed, and treat the winter case as a design condition rather than a seasonal inconvenience: a system that has to be bypassed in the coldest month has an availability problem, not a winter problem.

On the roads there is one further rule worth repeating, because it is the step most often skipped: **water brings dust down, but it does not remove it.** Particles that settle on a haul road are re-suspended by the next vehicle. Without a mechanical sweeper working the same route on a defined schedule, the suppression system is moving dust from the air to the ground and back again. Cannon plus sweeper is one measure, not two.

## Dry Fog: Locking a Point Source Where It Starts

A dry fog system atomises compressed air and low-pressure water together inside the nozzle, producing a fog with droplets typically in the 1 to 10 micron band. Capture is dominated by Brownian diffusion and agglomeration rather than gravity, effective distance is a few metres because the nozzle sits at the emission point, and moisture added to the material is measured in fractions of a percent.

Those characteristics fit a specific set of problems: belt transfer points and loading skirts, where material falls and displaced air carries fines; crusher feed and discharge openings; vibrating screens; chute loading points; bucket elevators; silo and hopper charging points. These locations share three properties — the dust is fine, the source is fixed and confined, and the belt or the product cannot tolerate added water.

There is one non-negotiable precondition. **Dry fog requires containment.** Used in the open, the fog disperses and the dust does not move; the equipment runs, consumes air and water, and achieves nothing measurable. This is why dry fog design starts with the enclosure, the skirting and the extraction, and only then considers nozzles. When a supplier proposes a dry fog system for an unenclosed transfer or an open apron, the correct next question is what will contain it.

The strategic value of dry fog in a cement plant is that it reaches the sources a cannon cannot serve. A cannon aimed at a transfer tower sprays water into the open air beside the tower, not into the chute. Pairing the two gives coverage of the whole material handling route: dry fog inside the enclosure, cannon on the open yard and the apron.

## Wheel Wash: The Materials Device at the Gate

Wheel wash is usually the last measure to be considered and the first to be cited in a compliance review, because its absence is visible from outside the fence. Material deposited on a haul road adheres to tyres and undercarriage; a vehicle leaving the site transfers it onto the public road; the deposit dries, traffic re-suspends it, and the resulting particulate is recorded as the plant's contribution to ambient dust no matter how well the stockyard itself is managed.

The European BAT conclusions for steel name *wheel-cleaning equipment* explicitly, alongside hard-surfaced transport roads, road cleaning and sheeting of loads. The cement BAT conclusions approach the same problem through paved lorry areas, road wetting and housekeeping. The mechanism is the same in both cases, and the device belongs at the gate. National quarry and materials-handling permits in several jurisdictions go further, treating the wheel and body wash as a required installation at yard exits rather than an optional accessory, often specifying that it be sited immediately at the exit and that the wash water be captured or treated rather than allowed to run off.

The engineering content of a wheel wash station is larger than the name suggests, and four things decide whether it works.

**Coverage.** Cleaning the tyres alone leaves the undercarriage and the lower body, which carry a substantial share of the load. A useful installation addresses the full footprint of the vehicle — sills, chassis, mudflap areas and the underside, where deposits accumulate and are hardest to reach.

**Water capture and reuse.** A wheel wash that discharges onto the ground at the gate has relocated the problem by a few metres. The station needs collection and treatment — settlement stages, recycling or discharge to a treatment facility — and it needs a routine for the sludge, because that sludge is the material that was being prevented from leaving. A closed arrangement in which the wash water passes through settlement and returns to a clean-water tank turns the station from a consumable into a recycling loop.

**Free drainage.** A vehicle that leaves the gate still shedding water deposits a wet trail on the public road, which is a nuisance in dry weather and a safety hazard when it freezes. A drainage or forced-drying stage after the wash, and heat tracing of the pipework in cold climates, are part of the specification rather than add-ons.

**Wrong-way traffic.** The station only works if vehicles actually pass through it. Physical arrangement, signage and site discipline matter as much as the nozzle layout, and on larger sites a single exit is often not enough.

Wheel wash is not an air quality device. It is a materials containment device, and it is most effective when it is designed together with the road cleaning schedule, the surface hardening programme and the site traffic plan.

## Smart Control: Replacing the Timer with an Event

The conventional installation is a timer and a contactor. The yard sprays for five minutes every half hour, whether or not anything is moving. It is inexpensive, defensible on paper, and it fails in three directions at once: water and power are spent on an empty yard; the material becomes too wet in one season and the pipes freeze in another, so operators disable the system; and when a review asks what happened during a particular shift last month, no record exists.

The alternative is to run the system as a loop rather than a schedule. Six functions, executed continuously: **monitor, identify, grade, trigger, verify, record**.

![Six functions of a closed-loop dust control system: monitor, identify, grade, trigger, verify, record](/images/cement-smart-control-loop.svg)

**Monitor.** Cameras aimed at actual emission points rather than at the skyline. Particulate monitors at hot spots and at the boundary. A compact weather station supplying wind speed, wind direction, temperature and humidity. The wind data is not decoration: it determines whether a spray programme lands on the source or drifts onto a road, a workshop or a neighbouring property, and the BAT framework for bulk materials explicitly relies on combining monitor data with wind direction to work out which sources are driving ambient readings.

**Identify.** Vision models detect plumes — and, critically, separate them from steam and water vapour, the single most common false-alarm source in a cement plant, since clinker cooling, hot material and wet processes all produce white plumes that are not dust. A second class of models recognises the operation itself: truck tipping, loader movement, belt running. Recognising the operation allows suppression to begin before the plume is visible rather than after it has dispersed.

**Grade.** A binary alarm produces either nuisance triggering or missed events. Grading converts a detection into a proportionate response: a short local burst at the lowest level, the full preset for the zone at the next, and escalation to the control room with the image attached at the highest. This is also the mechanism that stops a stuck valve from running a pump for an entire shift.

**Trigger.** Only the affected zone runs, on stored presets for azimuth, pitch, flow, droplet size and duration. The gateway should speak the plant's own protocol — dry contact, Modbus or MQTT — with a proper handshake to the control system, so that operators keep authority over their area rather than losing it to a black box.

**Verify.** The same sensors that raised the alarm confirm that the plume cleared. If it does not clear within the expected window, the system escalates instead of spraying indefinitely.

**Record.** Zone status, alarm lists, equipment availability, paired images, water and power consumption, and the compliance records a reviewer will ask for — exported on a schedule and retained for the period the permit requires.

Two rules separate a system that survives from one that is muted within a month. Thresholds must be set **per zone**, because an alarm level that makes sense at a raw material yard is meaningless inside a crushing station; and a commissioning period on site is a normal part of delivery, not evidence that the product is unfinished. Two to four weeks of tuning per plant should be costed as scope.

The most useful KPI is also the most direct: **visible dust-escape events per week, per zone.** It is a number an operations manager can act on, a number a community liaison officer can report, and — where an opacity or visible-emission limit applies — a number that maps straight onto the regulatory criterion.

## Where Water Is the Wrong Answer

A credible programme states its limits as clearly as its applications, because equipment specified into the wrong scenario is not merely ineffective: it gets switched off, and the conclusion drawn is that dust control does not work.

**Channelled sources belong to capture and filtration, not to spraying.** The kiln, the preheater and the clinker cooler have a duct and a stack, and they already have filters. Spraying water into that gas stream would be pointless at best.

**The finished-cement section is closed to water.** The cement mill, the silos, the packers and the loading heads are served by enclosure, inward negative pressure and bag filtration. Where a loading head needs the dust knocked down right at the spout, the small quantity of mist involved has to evaporate before it lands, be interlocked with the loading action, apply only when a vehicle is actually being loaded, and be agreed with the people who own the product specification — because at that point the water quantity is a process parameter, not an environmental setting.

**The coal system is a protection problem.** A suspended coal dust cloud is itself a hazard. Housekeeping practice matters as much as equipment: blowing accumulated dust off with compressed air is precisely the action that creates the cloud an explosion needs, so wet or vacuum methods are used instead. Ex configurations can be provided to suit the customer's application; the specific protection type and certificate are supplied with the project. Layer control, temperature and carbon monoxide monitoring, and correct electrical protection belong in the same package.

Stating these limits is not a disclaimer. It is the reason the rest of the proposal can be trusted.

## Matching the Tool to the Source

Read across the table, and a simple rule emerges. **Open, coarse and wind- or traffic-driven sources take area suppression. Confined, fine and continuous sources take point suppression. Vehicles leaving the site take wheel wash. All of them take a control layer if the result is to be provable.**

| Character of the source | Dominant mechanism | Matching measure | Precondition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Open area, coarse particles, wind-driven | Inertial collision, gravity | Long-range fog cannon | Reach and overlap designed for the site wind; sweeping planned |
| Confined point, fine particles, continuous | Diffusion and agglomeration | Dry fog system | A containment or enclosure exists |
| Vehicle tyres and undercarriage at the site exit | Adhesion and carry-out | Wheel wash station | Capture, treatment and reuse of wash water; drainage |
| Time-varying, event-driven across many sources | Detection and scheduling | Control layer with per-zone presets | Sensors at the source; operator authority preserved |

The pairing is not a hierarchy. A dry fog system is not a small fog cannon, and a fog cannon is not a large dry fog system. They operate at opposite ends of the material handling chain: dry fog locks a source, a cannon covers an area, wheel wash closes the gate, and control connects them into something that can be measured.

## Choosing Between Quotations Without Writing a Tender

Most sourcing decisions in this sector are still taken on three columns — throw distance, flow rate, price — none of which predicts what the equipment will cost to own. The three questions that actually separate suppliers are a conversation, not a scoring matrix.

**Was it designed as a system?** Ask how the pump, spray ring, nozzles, fan and control were matched to one another, and what the design was optimised for — reach, coverage, water consumption, or year-round availability. A supplier who can explain the trade-offs they chose has usually thought about the duty. A specification that lists components without describing how they were matched describes an assembly, not a design.

**What has it actually done in this industry?** Not a gallery of site photographs, but how long the supplier has been building for cement, mining and bulk material handling, and what those installations look like after several years of abrasive dust and continuous duty. Experience shows up in details: whether a manifold can be drained, whether a nozzle can be changed without dismantling the spray ring, whether a control cabinet sits somewhere it will not flood.

**What happens after commissioning?** Spare parts availability, response arrangements and a documented winter procedure decide the annual availability figure. A settled product platform with a long supply commitment lets a plant build a maintenance routine; a design revised every year does not.

A certain amount of durability information cannot be verified on site regardless of how the questions are framed. Droplet size cannot be measured in a working plant and drifts over time as nozzles wear, scale and filters load. Noise and vibration differences are not audible on a new machine and only accumulate under continuous duty. Throw distance is a design figure measured under defined conditions, and no mandatory test regime applies to it — which is precisely why an industry technical standard, such as JB/T 13562-2018 *Technical specification for air-assisted spray dust suppression devices*, is more useful at tender stage than a brochure number. Asking under what conditions a figure was obtained is more informative than comparing the figure itself.

The cost of getting this wrong is not theoretical. A unit that underperforms in effective reach or spray distribution does not fix itself with a settings change; the remedy is additional units, new foundations and a second round of shutdown coordination. An unreliable unit produces repeated repairs in an area that is already dusty and has poor visibility, which is a safety question before it is anything else. Neither outcome appears in a three-column comparison. What becomes visible, after three years, is which supplier's equipment is still running and which has become a maintenance item.
