A city does not generate dust in one place. It generates it in hundreds of small, moving, irregular places, almost all of them outdoors, none of them fitted with a stack, and many of them somewhere else by next month. Regulatory attention has traditionally gone to the large industrial sources, and that attention has worked: the organised emissions from power plants, cement kilns and steel works are monitored, capped and steadily falling. The dust that remains in urban air is increasingly a problem of surfaces — of material sitting on a road, on bare ground, on rubble or in a loading bay, waiting for a wheel, a gust of wind or a bucket to put it back into the air.

This kind of dust is called fugitive dust, and three properties make it awkward to handle. Its emission strength is uncertain, because nobody can say in advance how much material a given square metre of pavement is holding. Its release is random in time, because it depends on when a vehicle passes, when a load is tipped, or when the wind picks up. And its sources are varied in both location and type. Those three properties together explain why fugitive dust has proved so much harder to close out than an industrial stack — and why, after a decade of dramatic improvement in urban air quality, it is one of the items still visibly on the list.

Understanding it starts with a shift of view. The question worth asking is not only how much dust a city produces, but where that dust goes after it has been produced once.

## Four Sources That Account for Most Urban Dust

Technical guidance on urban dust in China divides the sources into four broad groups: road dust, construction dust, soil dust from exposed ground, and dust from material stockpiles. In a working city, the first two dominate everything else. Regional source inventories for the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region and its surrounding cities put fugitive dust at roughly 1.7 million tonnes of PM10 and 450,000 tonnes of PM2.5 a year, and attribute more than 80 percent of that total to roads and construction taken together.

Four ordinary activities do most of the work. They are worth separating, because they behave differently and they are not all in the same place.

![Urban dust sources grouped into four categories — demolition, construction sites, road and utility works, and material haulage — with the two emission routes, primary and secondary, and road surface dust loadings](/images/urban-dust-where-it-comes-from.svg)

**Demolition.** Buildings are taken down, debris is loaded out, and rubble sits in the open while the clearance proceeds. The dust is thrown up by impact rather than carried by wind, it arrives in short intense episodes, and it contains a mixture of materials — mortar, brick, plaster, concrete fines. When one block is finished, the source stops, until the next one starts.

**Construction sites.** Earthworks, excavation, spoil heaps, bare ground and unpaved internal roads. The material is fine enough to travel and heavy enough to settle nearby, and crucially the bare surfaces keep feeding the source for months. The location is essentially fixed; what varies is the intensity, which rises and falls with the stage of the job.

**Road and utility works.** Cutting, trenching, backfill, and a subgrade left open to traffic for weeks. What makes this category different from a construction site is not the material but the position: the work happens inside the traffic corridor, so dust is raised and dispersed by vehicles passing a few metres away, and the affected stretch moves down the street as the works progress.

**Material haulage.** Spillage from loads, mud carried out on wheels, uncovered truck beds. This category is the most mobile of the four and the least visible, because nothing dramatic happens — a tipper leaves a site and a few kilograms of material drop off it over the next two kilometres. Repeated all day on the same route, the effect accumulates.

One finding from source apportionment is worth pausing on, because it changes how the problem is usually framed. Road surfaces do not generally generate particles; they accumulate them. Studies of road dust loadings have traced much of that accumulated material back to the haulage of construction material and spoil. In other words, a site's dust problem is not confined to the site: the material leaves on wheels and on the underside of trucks, and reappears as road dust a kilometre away, where it will be counted as a traffic problem rather than a construction one.

The numbers behind that loading are more variable than most people expect. Measurements in Chinese cities commonly find road surface dust loadings of 10 grams per square metre or more. On well-managed roads the figure is one to two grams per square metre, or lower. The traffic on those roads is comparable; the difference is what is lying on the surface. That gap is the single clearest argument for treating sweeping as a dust-control measure rather than as street cleaning.

Against that backdrop, it is worth noting how much fugitive dust still contributes. After a decade in which urban PM2.5 concentrations in China fell by more than half, source apportionment in most cities still attributes roughly 15 to 25 percent of PM2.5 concentration to dust sources. A residual share of that size is not an accounting curiosity. It is the part of the problem that the industrial playbook does not reach.

## The Route That Has No Site Fence Around It

A construction site can be fenced, and a road cannot. That asymmetry is the reason urban dust control fails more often than it should.

Dust leaves a working city by two routes. The first is primary dust: material raised directly by an operation — a breaker, a bucket, a tipper, a wheel crossing loose ground. Its signature is that it stops when the operation stops, which makes it relatively easy to associate with a cause. The second is secondary dust: material that has already settled and is lifted again by wind, by tyres, or by sweeping. This second route has no operational signature at all. It needs no construction to continue, it can run for months after a site has been handed over, and it is fed by the first route, so the two are not independent problems but successive stages of one.

Secondary dust is also where the boundary of a site stops being meaningful. A demolition project ends, the fence comes down, the contractor leaves — and the fine material that left the site on wheels is still sitting on the road, still being lifted by traffic every morning. Any control strategy that stops at the gate simply hands the remaining work to somebody else, usually the municipal cleaning budget, usually without the information needed to do it.

There is a second, subtler consequence of the same asymmetry. Because these sources are distributed, intermittent and unattached to any measurement device, most of them have no baseline. An industrial stack has a continuous monitor and a permit number; a tipping apron has nothing. Where measurement is absent, assessment tends to become visual, and Chinese urban construction-dust rules reflect that: sites are expected to be enclosed, exposed material covered, vehicles washed before they leave, internal ground hardened, demolition carried out with water suppression, and spoil transported in sealed vehicles. Every one of those requirements describes something an inspector can see. That is both the strength and the limitation of visual criteria — they are unambiguous and enforceable, but they describe the site, not the material that has already left it.

## Where the Dust Goes: Four Fates

Once airborne, dust leaves the atmosphere by a small number of routes. Atmospheric science describes the sequence simply: emission, transport, deposition. Some of it is carried out of the city and diluted; the rest comes back down, either under gravity as dry deposition or washed out by rain as wet deposition. What happens after that depends entirely on what it lands on, and it is here that the four fates diverge.

![The four fates of urban dust: settled and re-entrained, converted into secondary pollution, inhaled, and deposited into water, soil, plants and material surfaces](/images/urban-dust-four-fates.svg)

## Fate One: Back to the Ground, Then Up Again

This is the largest single fate, and the only one that repeats.

Where dust settles on vegetated ground, it enters the soil and largely stays there; vegetation also reduces the wind speed at the surface that would lift it again. Where it settles on a road, a pavement, a yard or a roof, it joins a reservoir. The next vehicle, the next gust, or the next dry spell lifts part of it back into the air, and the process repeats. The same mass of material can be raised and dropped many times over the course of a season.

This is why a street swept at noon can look grey again by evening without anything new having been built. It is also why the arithmetic of dust control is so counter-intuitive: reducing the amount of material on the surface reduces emissions at every subsequent disturbance, so the benefit compounds. Removing a tonne of dust from a road is not a one-off saving of a tonne of emissions; it is a saving on every pass of every wheel for as long as the surface stays clean.

## Fate Two: Converted Into Secondary Pollution

The second fate is chemical, and it is the reason dust and haze cannot be treated as separate problems.

Sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds are emitted as gases. In the atmosphere they oxidise, and in the presence of ammonia they form sulphate, nitrate and ammonium salts. These secondary particles are fine enough to remain suspended for days, and they account for a large share of total PM2.5 mass in Chinese cities — alongside organic matter. Airborne dust does not merely add mass to that mixture. It also provides a surface, and often an alkaline one, on which some of these conversion reactions proceed faster than they would in the gas phase alone. Mineral dust is not an inert passenger in urban air; it participates.

There is a second mechanism that closes the loop between the two fates. Fine particles emitted by vehicle exhausts and by industrial processes — the very particles that secondary chemistry also produces — settle onto roads, bare ground, squares and roofs, and are then lifted back into the air. When this happens, the mass is re-attributed: particles that originated in a tailpipe but were emitted from a road surface are counted as dust rather than as traffic. This matters for control strategy, because it means the measures that reduce surface loading and the measures that reduce exhaust emissions are acting on the same pool of material at different points in its life.

The practical consequence is a useful simplification. Because dust and secondary pollution interact, suppressing dust reduces the surface available for chemistry and the mass available for re-suspension at the same time, while reducing precursor gases reduces the amount of new particle mass that can form on that surface. Neither measure alone finishes the job.

## Fate Three: Into the Lungs

The third fate is the one that decides how much the other three actually matter.

The World Health Organization sets out the mechanism plainly. Particles with a diameter of 10 microns or less can penetrate deep into the lungs and lodge there, causing irritation and inflammation and damaging the lining of the respiratory tract. Particles of 2.5 microns or less — sixty of them span the width of a human hair — can cross the lung barrier and enter the bloodstream, from which they reach the major organs rather than staying in the chest. Exposure is associated with heart disease, respiratory disease, lung cancer and stroke.

In 2015 the International Agency for Research on Cancer concluded that particulate matter in outdoor air causes lung cancer. In 2021 the WHO tightened its global air quality guideline for annual average PM2.5 to 5 micrograms per cubic metre, on evidence of harm at concentrations lower than previously understood. The groups most at risk are children, older adults, and people already living with heart or lung disease. Children breathe more air per unit of body weight, spend more time outdoors and have less mature immune systems; studies of children in high-PM2.5 communities have found measurably slower lung growth compared with children in low-PM2.5 communities.

One misconception is worth correcting in this context, because it recurs in every discussion of construction and road dust: the assumption that a coarse, mechanically generated dust is therefore not a health problem. Mechanical action is less efficient than combustion at producing ultrafine particles, but it does produce a fine fraction, and it certainly produces particles in the 1 to 2.5 micron band. Ambient PM2.5 samples in cities routinely contain crustal material — the mineral signature of dust. A dust source is not automatically a coarse-particle problem, and the fraction that matters most is the fraction nobody can see.

## Fate Four: Water, Soil, Plants and Materials

The fourth set of fates is slower and quieter, and it outlasts the dust episode that produced it.

Particles deposited onto water bodies affect water quality and clarity. Particles that settle on leaves carry metals and organic compounds that can affect plant growth and yield. Particles that settle on building facades, windows and solar panels cause soiling — a cost that is diffuse, real and rarely attributed to dust. Suspended particles reduce visibility by absorbing and scattering light, and they influence the local heat balance in both directions: dark particles such as black carbon tend to warm, while sulphate and nitrate particles tend to cool. And the finer the fraction, the further it travels — dust does not stay in the city that produced it, which is one reason air quality in neighbouring cities moves together rather than independently.

Deposited dust also accumulates. Unlike an emission from a stack, which disperses and dilutes, deposition concentrates material onto surfaces, where metals and organic compounds can build up over years in soil and in drainage systems. This is the fate that most often escapes the environmental assessment entirely, because it happens after the reporting period, off site, and below the threshold at which anyone notices.

## Five Situations, and What Fits Each

If the source is understood properly, the measure tends to follow from it — which is a different logic from choosing equipment by budget. Most of what a city has to deal with falls into five situations, and each has a natural answer.

![Five urban dust situations — demolition and construction, stockpiles and exposed ground, city roads, trunk roads, and city-wide control — mapped to fog cannons, vehicle-mounted suppression, pole-mounted spray lines, sweeping and monitoring](/images/urban-dust-control-measures.svg)

**Demolition and construction sites.** The working face moves as the job advances, so a device fixed at one position stops covering the source the moment the site moves past it. The configuration that works is a cannon mounted on a tower or roof, covering the area from above, combined with a vehicle-mounted unit that follows the working face.

**Stockpiles and exposed ground.** The source stays put, and the wind decides where the dust actually goes. Coverage should therefore be laid out along the prevailing wind direction rather than aimed at the pile, with surface covering or stabilisation wherever the material allows it.

**City roads, day to day.** By the time anything is visible, the dust has been lying on the surface for hours. Suppression brings it down; mechanical sweeping takes it away. Only the pair is complete, and the second half has to be scheduled as a dust-control task, not as an appearance standard.

**Trunk roads and haul routes.** Long, linear, continuous sources, where material is dropped along the whole length rather than at a point. A vehicle loop cannot keep up here — refilling, scheduling, lane occupation, water consumption, fuel and labour do not scale to tens of kilometres. The measure that does scale is a pole-mounted spray line: units mounted high on street-lighting or dedicated poles at 25 to 50 metre spacing, forming a continuous curtain along the road rather than a passing wet stripe.

**All of the above, at the same time.** No operator can say by intuition which section will need water in the next ten minutes. This is where segment-level measurement belongs — a monitoring point for each stretch of road, measuring PM10, PM2.5 and local weather, driving the start, stop, spray rate and duration of the units on that stretch alone.

Two underlying mechanisms explain why the same city needs more than one kind of device. A fog cannon pressurises water through a nozzle ring and accelerates it with an axial fan; droplets run from tens to a few hundred microns, capture is dominated by inertial collision and gravitational settling, and effective distance runs from tens to a few hundred metres. It brings down a visible plume across an area, and it can do so from a tower, so nobody has to stand in the dust. A dry fog system atomises compressed air and low-pressure water together inside the nozzle, producing droplets in the 1 to 10 micron band; capture is dominated by Brownian diffusion and agglomeration, effective distance is a few metres because the nozzle sits at the source, and the moisture added is negligible. It locks down respirable dust at a point — provided there is an enclosure for it to work in. Used in the open, it simply disperses.

The useful rule is that dry fog works on points and a cannon works on areas. Applied to a city, that means the open, wind-driven, coarse-material situations on the street and at the site take cannons and vehicles, while enclosed transfer points and hooded operations take dry fog. Where an enclosed process is spraying into the open to compensate for missing containment, the equipment is being asked to do the steelwork's job.

## Why Suppression Alone Is Not Enough

A spray system moves dust from the air to the ground. That is a genuine improvement in the short term — it lowers inhaled concentration where people are — but by itself it does not remove the material, and Fate One will put it back. This is the single most common gap between a dust-control installation and a dust-control result: water applied without sweeping, repeated indefinitely.

The failure is easy to observe. The pavement is wetted, the plume disappears, the visible criterion is satisfied for an hour or two. By mid-afternoon the surface is dry again, the traffic is the same, and the loading is roughly what it was — slightly consolidated, perhaps, but available. The dust has not gone anywhere; it has been relocated in the vertical direction and left in place.

Two constraints follow. The first is that sweeping and suppression have to be planned as one sequence, with the water applied before the mechanical pass rather than instead of it. The second is that water volume has to be controlled rather than maximised. Over-application creates its own problems — material too wet to handle, ponding and runoff carrying sediment into drainage, and in winter, ice on a public road. Water is a working parameter, not a measure of effort.

There is also a limit that no amount of spraying addresses. Dust suppression acts on particulate material that has already been emitted. It does nothing about the gaseous precursors of secondary pollution, and nothing about the exhaust particles that will become tomorrow's road dust. A city that sprays diligently and does not touch its vehicle fleet, its combustion sources or its ammonia emissions is treating the symptom it can see while the part it cannot see keeps being replenished.

## From a Timer to a Control Loop

The conventional urban installation is a timer and a contactor: the cannon runs for five minutes every half hour, or the spray line runs on a fixed schedule, regardless of what is happening on the ground. It is cheap, it is easy to defend on paper, and it fails in three directions at once. Water and electricity are spent on sections with no dust and on hours with no activity. The system over-wets in wet weather and is switched off entirely in freezing weather, so it is unavailable exactly when the air is worst. And when an assessment team asks what was done last Tuesday, there is no record of anything.

What replaces it is a control loop: monitor, decide, act, verify, record. Monitoring points measure particulate concentration and local weather along each segment; cameras watch the sources and the road surface. A control layer decides whether dust is being generated, where, and how severely. Only the affected zone runs, on a stored preset. The same sensors that raised the question are then used to check the answer. And the run itself is logged, per zone, per shift.

Two details decide whether such a system survives its first year. The first is that the vision layer must distinguish dust from steam and water vapour — a wet road after rain, a cooling plume or a hot surface in cold weather all produce bright white plumes, and nuisance alarms will erode operator confidence faster than anything else. The second is that thresholds must be set per zone, because an alarm level that makes sense on a haul route is meaningless outside a school. A commissioning period is not a sign that the system is unfinished; it is how the thresholds get fitted to the place.

Done properly, the loop produces the one number that both an operator and an inspector can use: visible dust-escape events per zone, per week. That is a direct translation of the visual criteria that urban construction-dust rules are built on, into a figure that can be trended, compared and audited. A timer cannot produce it. A monitoring and control loop produces it as a by-product.

A standard city street dust-control scheme looks much like this: seventy pole-mounted spray units along trunk roads at 25 to 50 metre spacing, supported by six particulate monitoring points and twenty-four video channels driving automatic start and stop of the affected sections. The engineering content is not in the number of units. It is in the fact that each road section decides for itself, using its own measurement, and that the decision is recorded.

Equipment and control for that programme came from Fenghua Environmental, which is the first drafting unit of the industry standard JB/T 13562-2018, *Technical specification for air-assisted spray dust suppression devices*, and builds air-assisted spraying equipment from 30 metre dry-fog units to 300 metre long-range cannons. The distinction that matters when comparing suppliers is rarely a single superior component; it is whether the pump, the spray ring, the nozzles, the fan and the control layer were designed as one system, and whether the parts and the service will still be there in six years.

## What the Last Decade in China Shows

It is worth noting how much has already been achieved, because the pace of it is easy to forget from inside the problem.

Between 2013 and 2023, the national annual average PM2.5 concentration in China fell from 68 micrograms per cubic metre to 30 — a reduction of more than half. The number of days with heavy pollution fell by more than 80 percent over the same period. In 2023, 85.5 percent of days were rated good or excellent across 339 cities at prefecture level and above. In Beijing, the annual average fell from 89.5 micrograms per cubic metre in 2013 to 32 in 2023. On the ministry's own assessment, China became the country improving its air quality fastest.

The aggregate numbers are matched by results recorded at the level of individual cities, in the three everyday situations this article has been about.

In Beijing, a dust-control campaign run from March to May 2024 reported average road dust load down 21.4 percent year on year, dust load on the roads at construction site exits down 34.1 percent, and citywide PM10 down 12 percent. The largest reduction of the three was at the site exits — precisely the part that leaves on wheels, which is to say that it can be managed.

In Fenghua, a district of Ningbo, demolition was given a rulebook of its own: at least one fog cannon per 5,000 square metres of demolition area, the structure pre-wetted 24 hours before demolition, the cannon covering the whole working face during mechanical demolition, an automatic wash platform at the gate, and spoil carried sealed, not loaded above the sideboards.

Guangzhou solved the same problem on a different surface. On corridors where traffic makes water trucks unworkable, high-level sprays were mounted on existing lamp posts with smart monitoring points, switching on and off according to weather and particulate readings. Local measurements: a reduction of roughly 30 to 40 percent in local PM10 after thirty minutes of operation, and up to 50 percent after an hour.

Over a longer horizon, Shanghai's annual average PM2.5 stood at 26 micrograms per cubic metre in 2025, against 62 in 2013 — a reduction of more than half — with 323 good-air days and no days of heavy pollution, while road dust concentration in the city fell 4.9 percent year on year.

The mechanisms behind that change were largely structural: replacing coal with electricity and gas for household heating across tens of millions of rural homes in the north, completing ultra-low emission retrofits on the great majority of the country's coal-fired generating capacity, extending the same regime to steel and cement, and clearing out tens of thousands of small unregulated industrial plants. These are the changes that make the largest difference, and they are the ones that will be hardest to replicate quickly elsewhere. Central government spending on air pollution prevention and control has run above 30 billion yuan a year, with cumulative investment in recent years exceeding 200 billion yuan, mobilising on the order of a trillion yuan of wider investment.

What the same decade shows about urban dust specifically is more subtle, and more transferable. Two ingredients did most of the work. The first was measurement — putting numbers on sources that had never been counted, so that improvement could be demonstrated rather than asserted. The second was converting policy language into something visible and checkable, in this case the requirement that production facilities show no visible dust escaping and no accumulated dust on site. Once a criterion is visible, it can be inspected, and once it can be inspected it gets managed.

The remaining task in Chinese cities is, to a large degree, the one this article has been describing: distributed, intermittent, unfenced sources, whose dust settles onto surfaces and comes back. That is not a failure of the last decade. It is what the last decade leaves on the desk, and it is the reason pole-mounted spray lines, segmented monitoring and automatic control have moved from the edge of the industry to the middle of it in only a few years.

The broader point is a simple one. Urban dust is not a local quirk, an unfortunate property of construction, or a problem that one country has and others do not. Every city that builds, demolishes, digs and hauls produces it, and the physics is the same everywhere: it rises, it travels, it settles, it comes back, and some of it ends up in the people who breathe it. What differs is only how quickly a city decides to measure it and act on it. A decade of improvement in one place, achieved with visible criteria, enforced measurement and an enormous amount of public and private investment, is reasonable evidence that the same curve can be moved elsewhere.

## Keep the Conversation Going

Every city and every site has its own version of this problem, and the useful starting point is always the same: understand where the dust is coming from, where it is going, and which measure fits which situation — before the equipment list is drawn up.
