A fog cannon is usually bought the way a pump is bought: someone reads the datasheet, finds the throw distance, and compares metres. Eighty metres against sixty. The eighty wins.

Inside a tunnel that logic quietly reverses. The mist that leaves the nozzle at eighty metres is coarser than the mist that leaves at forty, and coarse mist is the fraction that cannot catch the dust that actually damages lungs. Meanwhile the tunnel's own ventilation is already moving at a speed that will carry the fine droplets a hundred metres downstream before they land — or push them back onto the machine if the air is running the other way.

So the question a tunnel project should ask is not *how far will it throw*. It is *where will the mist be when the dust is there*. Those are different questions, and on most projects only the first one gets asked.

This article is about the second one. It covers what tunnel dust is made of, why the industry standard ties throw distance to droplet size, why a 7 m ceiling truncates a plume that was rated on open ground, how airflow overrides the fan, where in the tunnel a cannon earns its keep, and — the part most projects miss — what the machine can do with the fan running and the pump switched off.

## 1. The fraction that matters is the fraction you cannot see

Start with the dust, because everything else follows from it.

Tunnel dust is not one material. Drilling produces rock dust. Blasting produces a mix of rock dust and fume. Shotcrete produces cement dust with a different chemistry and a different pH. Diesel equipment adds particulate. Each has its own size distribution, and size is what decides whether a water droplet can intercept it at all.

The measurements are consistent across decades of Chinese tunnel practice. In the dust survey carried out by the former Ministry of Railways labour health institute, more than 90% of the dust generated in tunnel construction fell into the respirable fraction — particles small enough to reach the alveoli. For rock dust specifically, 64.5% of particles were below 2.0 µm. For blasting fume dust, 87.5% were below 2.0 µm.

More recent work on a long tunnel project put respirable dust at 62% of the total aerosol, with free crystalline silica content between 18% and 35%. A separate field study on a large-cross-section high-speed rail tunnel driven by roadheader found face dust with a median diameter between 8.52 µm and 15.59 µm depending on cutting direction, and a respirable fraction of 22.68% to 41.22%.

Two things follow.

First, the arithmetic of a tunnel is unfavourable in a specific way. On the surface, coarse dust dominates the mass and a water spray that wets the surface solves most of the problem. Underground, the mass is carried by particles you cannot wet by sprinkling, because they do not settle. They stay airborne for the length of the tunnel.

Second, the dust changes as it travels. In the same roadheader study, particles larger than 25 µm dropped out within 25 m of the face, and beyond 100 m more than 80% of what remained was respirable. By the time dust reaches the lining crew, it is almost entirely the fine fraction — and it is also far more dilute.

That produces a genuinely useful map: high concentration near the face, high respirability far from it. Almost every sensible dust control decision in a tunnel comes from respecting that gradient.

## 2. The standard already tells you the trade-off

Here is the part that surprises people who only read marketing sheets.

China's machinery industry standard for air-assisted spray dust suppression equipment, JB/T 13562—2018 *Air-assisted spray dust suppression equipment — Technical requirements*, specifies performance in a single linked table. Horizontal spray throw distance, spray flow rate, droplet diameter and fan outlet velocity are given together, not independently, because they are physically coupled.

| Throw distance (m) | Spray flow (L/min) | Droplet diameter (µm) | Fan outlet velocity (m/s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 200 | 240 | < 250 | 50 |
| 150 | 240 | < 250 | 48 |
| 120 | 240 | < 200 | 46 |
| 100 | 200 | < 180 | 44 |
| 80 | 120 | < 150 | 42 |
| 60 | 120 | < 130 | 38 |
| 50 | 60 | < 120 | 32 |
| 40 | 30 | < 120 | 30 |
| ≤ 30 | 18 | < 120 | 28 |

Read the last column backwards and the design logic becomes obvious. To throw 200 m, the standard permits droplets up to 250 µm. To throw 50 m, droplets must be below 120 µm. Distance is bought with droplet mass. A heavy droplet carries momentum and travels; a light droplet decelerates, gets entrained by the surrounding air, and stays where the air puts it.

The same standard also fixes other requirements worth knowing before a tender: supply pressure 1.5–3.5 MPa, liquid-to-gas ratio 0.2–0.3 L/m³, noise not exceeding 85 dB(A) at 1 m, pitch range −10° to 60°, horizontal rotation up to 320°, remote control functional at 100 m, and a dust suppression efficiency of at least 90% when tested in an enclosure with sand dust after 30 seconds of spraying.

Now hold the droplet column against the dust measurements above. Respirable dust is below roughly 5 µm. At the top of the standard's range, the permitted droplet is 250 µm — fifty times the diameter of the particle it is supposed to catch.

This is not a criticism of the standard; the standard is describing a physical reality, and it is honest enough to publish the coupling. It is a criticism of reading the first column only. A 200 m cannon specified for a tunnel face is a machine optimised for a problem the tunnel does not have.

## 3. The tunnel is seven metres tall. A hundred-metre throw has nowhere to go

Every throw distance on every datasheet is measured under three conditions that a tunnel breaks at the same time: open ground, unrestricted height, still air.

Start with height.

| Tunnel type | Internal profile area | Height above rail | Width |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single-track railway | 27–32 m² | 6.6–7.0 m | 4.9–5.6 m |
| Double-track railway | 58–67 m² | 7.2–8.0 m | 8.8–10.6 m |

Road tunnels are wider but not proportionally taller. Metro running tunnels are smaller than both. Whatever the figure, it is a ceiling — and a spray plume is a cone, not a beam.

The cone is the whole point of the machine. It expands as it travels, which is how one unit covers a wide coal yard at 60 m. In a 7 m heading the cone meets the crown long before it reaches the number printed on the plate. What happens next is not mysterious: droplets coalesce on the lining and run down the wall, and the fraction that stays airborne is redirected along the crown by the tunnel's own airflow. The fan is still running, the pump is still drawing water, and the reach is gone.

Then there is what the space already contains. A ventilation duct of 1.5 m diameter runs along one haunch. Drill jigs, lining trolleys and the invert trestle occupy most of the cross-section in sequence as the heading advances. Work published in *Modern Tunnelling Technology* on a drill-and-blast heading found that large trolleys "significantly block the airflow and induce local recirculation and secondary dust entrainment" — the obstruction is not a nuisance, it reshapes the flow field the mist has to travel through. Long before a plume would reach 30 m, it has been cut by steel.

Air is the third condition. Tunnel ventilation is not still. It runs at 0.4–0.7 m/s for optimum dust removal, with a floor of about 0.25 m/s for drill-and-blast headings and 0.5 m/s for TBM headings. That is gentler than outdoor wind, which makes it easy to dismiss — but it is continuous, directional, and it acts on the mist for as long as the mist exists. Open-ground ratings fall by roughly 30% in a force 3 wind and by more than half at force 4–5. A heading is never still air. It is a controlled cross-flow that never stops.

And then the machine itself. A 60 m class unit is typically around 2.16 × 1.46 × 2.26 m with a 1.3 m fan outlet, and it needs a skid or a tow unit plus a clear lane to work from. In a 5 m wide single-track heading, that lane is the transport route. A footprint that fits a coal yard does not fit a heading without displacing something else.

The practical consequence is not that long-throw machines are bad machines. It is that in a tunnel the paid-for part of the rating is the part that gets discarded. Money spent on a larger fan and a longer barrel buys metres that terminate at the crown. The same budget spent on droplet quality, on a lower mounting height, on faster repositioning and on controls that start and stop on measurement buys results that survive the space.

That is the argument for a short-throw, fine-droplet machine — not as a compromise, but as the correct specification for a 60 m² room with a ceiling.

## 4. Airflow, not fan pressure, decides where the mist lands

The second thing that overrides the datasheet is the tunnel's ventilation.

A tunnel is a duct. Air enters at one end and leaves at the other, and everything suspended in it drifts with that flow. Droplet settling velocity is small compared with the air velocity, so a droplet's horizontal travel is set largely by the air, not by the impulse it received at the nozzle.

Work published through the International Water Mist Association modelled exactly this. In a horizontal tunnel with a 3 m/s longitudinal airflow — a normal ventilation condition — droplets between 90 µm and 170 µm, which covers the great majority of commercial tunnel mist systems, were carried 60 m to 130 m downstream before reaching the floor. At 10 m/s the same droplets landed between 130 m and 750 m away. Even a 300 µm droplet travelled 60 m to 90 m at 10 m/s.

Tunnel construction ventilation is gentler than that, of course. The usual design band for dust removal in drill-and-blast headings sits around 0.4–0.7 m/s, with a minimum of about 0.25 m/s for drill and blast and 0.5 m/s for TBM headings, and US guidance treats 0.3 m/s as the absolute minimum for complete removal of a dust cloud, raising it to 0.5 m/s where rock silica exceeds 25%. But gentle air still moves mist. A study of compressed-air-driven fog cannon atomisation found that at a duct outlet velocity of 15 m/s, droplets were carried out of the roadway within about 12 seconds.

Two practical consequences.

If the cannon fires with the ventilation, the mist does not sit at the face. It travels. The face gets a brief pass, and the dust that was not intercepted in that pass is now downstream, mixed with mist, moving toward people who are not wearing the same protection as the operator. If the cannon fires against the ventilation, the mist decelerates faster, the plume shortens, and part of the spray can be driven back onto the machine and the deck.

Neither is a reason not to use a cannon. Both are reasons to place and aim it with the airflow in mind rather than treating throw distance as reach.

The practical rule that emerges from field work is unglamorous: put the machine where the dust is densest, aim across the flow rather than into it, and accept that the useful plume is shorter in a tunnel than on a yard. That is why the remote control range of 100 m in the standard matters more than it looks — the operator needs to be out of the dust while the machine works in it.

## 5. Three zones, three different jobs

Tunnel dust is not evenly distributed along the axis, and this is the single most useful thing to know when deciding what equipment goes where.

Field measurement on a large-cross-section tunnel under forced ventilation produced a clean three-zone picture:

- **Turbulent zone, 0–30 m from the face.** Concentration fluctuates violently. Peak measured value 1424 mg/m³. Median particle size around 14.6 µm.
- **Settling zone, 30–100 m.** Concentration falls from about 960 mg/m³ to about 288 mg/m³ as coarse particles drop out. Median size falls from 8.38 µm to 5.2 µm.
- **Stable zone, beyond 100 m.** Concentration below 70 mg/m³, size distribution steady, dominated by the respirable fraction.

A separate patent-practice description of a very long tunnel reaches the same conclusion from the equipment side: in the turbulent zone use high-pressure spray or foam; in the settling zone use water curtains or filter screens; in the stable zone use mobile air purifiers; and where a bench method creates an eddy at the upper bench, add a local extraction hood.

This is what a fog cannon is actually for. It is a **first-zone instrument**. It belongs 10–25 m behind the face, moving forward with the heading, working on the 1400 mg/m³ problem before that dust has a chance to become a 5 µm problem spread over 300 m. It is not an air purifier and it is not a substitute for the ventilation circuit.

The engineering literature on cannon layout in tunnels converges on the same numbers. A study of nozzle placement published in *Scientific Reports* in 2026 found that mist does not form a uniform curtain at all — it develops in three stages, with the highest droplet concentration appearing 15–25 m from the face, driven by jet entrainment and return airflow. Devices placed about 10 m from the face intercepted dust earliest and protected the busiest work area best in the first minutes; devices placed 15–25 m back produced a residual mist cloud that persisted longer after the pump stopped and kept capturing dust as it drifted out.

Chinese tunnel practice has arrived at the same layout independently, and often in layers. On the Erlangshan tunnel, dust control ran as four successive lines: water-bag charged blasting at the face, a high-pressure jet water monitor on the drill jig about 60 m back, a mobile water curtain around 80 m, and fixed shower-type curtains every 700 m to handle dust raised by muck trucks and lining work. On the Yan'an East Road tunnel renovation in Shanghai, the same idea was formalised as three-stage control: spray at the work face, electrostatic and water-filter separation along the migration path, and a mist curtain at the portal where the tunnel meets the outside air.

Note what the cannon's job is in each of those schemes. It is the first line. It removes the bulk, quickly, at the point of generation. Everything downstream handles what it missed.

## 6. The fan can run without the water

There is a fact buried in the control panel of this equipment class that almost nobody uses: the fan and the pump are separate motors with separate switches. Product documentation lists fan-on, fan-off, pump-on and pump-off as four independent controls. Nothing forces them to be pressed together.

That separation matters because a heading has more than one air problem, and only one of them is solved by water.

![One machine, three ways to run it](/images/tunnel-three-modes-en.svg)

**Fan only.** The machine becomes a movable, aimable air mover. Its use is not dilution — that is the duct's job — but stirring. Dust and stagnant gas accumulate where the main airflow does not reach: against the crown, behind trolleys, in the invert, in a cavity where the section changes. Chinese practice already recognises this as a category of work: ventilation requirements specifically call for local fans at cavities, near lining trolleys and at section changes to clear accumulated gas. A cannon parked where the flow is dead and aimed along the obstruction does the same thing mechanically, and it can be moved as the trolley moves.

**Fan plus fine mist.** This is cooling and humidifying, and the mechanism is evaporation, not wetting. It is the mode that most often goes unused and is the most valuable on a hot heading — more on that below.

**Fan plus full spray.** This is the familiar mode: knocking the cloud down after the blast, during mucking and during shotcrete. It is a first-zone tool, as Section 5 explains.

The airflow numbers explain both the capability and the limit. Commercial units in this class are typically rated between about 14,400 m³/h on a 45 m frame and about 36,000 m³/h on a 120 m frame, with some manufacturers quoting more than 46,000 m³/h on their largest units. Put that against the demand side. Chinese railway practice requires at least 3 m³/min of fresh air per person, and 3 m³/min per kW where diesel equipment is working. One 200 kW loader therefore needs 600 m³/min, or 36,000 m³/h.

So a large cannon moves roughly the air that a single loader needs. That is not a toy volume — and it is also not a tunnel.

**The boundary has to be stated plainly.** Tunnel construction ventilation is a regulated safety system, not an airflow preference. It requires a dedicated duct and fan, a duct outlet within 15 m of the face, a minimum face velocity of 0.25 m/s, one fan per heading, dual power supply, and fan-to-gas interlocking in gas-bearing ground. A mobile machine with a free jet and no duct satisfies none of that. It must never be offered as a substitute, and a supplier who suggests otherwise is telling you he has not read the specification. Used as a substitute it is worse than useless: a free jet discharged into a heading disturbs the directional flow the duct has established and can push dust back along the crown toward the crew.

Used alongside it, the same fan is useful. The distinction is the whole point.

### The hot heading is where this becomes a commercial argument

Tunnel temperature is not a comfort issue. Railway tunnel construction practice caps the working environment at 28 °C, and the highway code (JTG/T 3660—2020) sets the same 28 °C as the design limit for the working environment. Above it, the cost shows up in three places at once.

Chinese practice grades the problem: 28–32 °C is handled by strengthening ventilation alone; 32–38 °C requires ventilation plus ice or spray; 38–50 °C requires ventilation plus mechanical refrigeration plus insulation; above 50 °C requires combined refrigeration, sealing and heavy insulation. In other words, spray is not a folk remedy in a hot tunnel — it is written into the standard response for the middle band, and it is one of the few measures that works on air temperature rather than on the rock.

The measured effect is real but should be quoted with its scope. At the Sangzhuling tunnel, spraying cold water lowered the ambient temperature by 2–4 °C, and local ice placement lowered it 3–5 °C within 3 m. A study in *Modern Tunnelling Technology* comparing four configurations found that ventilation plus ice plus spray gave the best control: an average cooling rate of 5.37 °C/min, a stable ambient temperature of 26.26 °C, and a face rock temperature brought down to 46.1 °C with the working environment held at 25–27 °C. On the plateau railway project, a combined intelligent ventilation and spray system reported a 6–9 °C reduction from the ventilation stage alone.

The economic case is the part that gets left out of the sales conversation, and it is the strongest part. Labour efficiency falls by 10–15% in the 28–32 °C band, 20–30% in the 32–38 °C band, and 30–50% above 38 °C. Equipment failure rates rise and engine output falls. In cost terms, high-geothermal tunnel cooling measures are budgeted in the range of 8,000–12,000 yuan per metre, and one documented 800 m section recorded 8,321 yuan per metre for cooling measures alone, plus a separate heat-related productivity loss allowance at 25% of labour and plant cost.

Against numbers of that size, a machine that already exists on site and can be switched from dust mode to cooling mode by turning one pump off and aiming the barrel is not a marginal addition. It is the cheapest cooling capacity the project owns.

Which is the real argument for the three-mode design. A cannon in a tunnel is not a dust suppression device that happens to have a fan attached. It is heading environment equipment, and dust suppression is one of three jobs it can do.

## 7. The case for the machine, and its honest limit

The strongest argument for a cannon in a tunnel is a measured one.

At the Jinyunshan tunnel on the Chongqing–Yongchuan project — a three-lane, large-cross-section tunnel through Grade III to V surrounding rock, roughly 2.7 km per tube — the project recorded dust before and after introducing a fog cannon at the face. Mean total dust fell from 103.18 mg/m³ to 5.77 mg/m³. Mean respirable dust fell from 31.13 mg/m³ to 1.72 mg/m³. Measured suppression rate exceeded 90% on both metrics.

That is a real result and it deserves to be quoted. But it should be read carefully, because it says something specific: a cannon working close to the face, on freshly generated dust, before dispersion, removes the overwhelming majority of it.

It does not say a cannon can clean a tunnel.

The physical reason is in the collision probability. Conventional water mist at ordinary pressures captures somewhere between 30% and 50% of PM10 and under 20% of PM2.5 — the figures cited repeatedly in comparisons of wet suppression methods. The reason is not laziness, it is aerodynamics: a 100 µm droplet and a 2 µm particle do not share a streamline, so the small particle follows the air around the droplet instead of hitting it. Only when the droplet is small enough — the same order of magnitude as the particle — does interception become likely, and small droplets are exactly the ones that cannot be thrown far or survive a cross-flow.

So the honest division of labour in a tunnel looks like this:

| Zone | Dominant dust state | What works | What a cannon contributes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–30 m | 1000+ mg/m³, mixed sizes | Source capture, high-pressure spray | Primary tool |
| 30–100 m | Falling concentration, 5–8 µm | Water curtain, wet scrubber, filtration | Helper only |
| > 100 m | < 70 mg/m³, > 80% respirable | Ventilation, HEPA filtration, air purifiers | No useful role |
| Worker breathing zone | Fine fraction, all day | Respiratory protection, enclosure, extraction at source | Partial, never sufficient alone |

Two supporting data points show where the ceiling sits. In the Jiaoding tunnel test, a mobile spray scheme reduced dust and CO concentration by up to 60% in the first 20 minutes, better than a water-bag scheme at 52.62% — good, but not a clean tunnel. In a study of ultrasonic mist coupled with forced ventilation in a 175 m² tunnel section, the best configuration — mist started *before* blasting, ventilation at half power — brought PM10 down by 95.56% and PM2.5 by 76.00%, and cut the time to reach the engineering threshold from 53 minutes to 30 minutes. Note the PM2.5 figure. Even a purpose-built fine-mist system with ultrasonic atomisation tops out around three-quarters on the finest fraction.

A fog cannon is not that system. It is a bulk removal device with an unusually good cost-per-cubic-metre, and it should be specified as one.

## 8. Timing does more work than capacity

If there is one finding that changes how a cannon should be operated, it is this: when you start spraying matters more than what you bought.

The ultrasonic mist study compared three configurations in the same tunnel: ventilation only; mist started 30 minutes after blasting; mist started immediately before blasting with ventilation at half power. Results on PM10 removal efficiency were 44.9%, 77.8% and 95.56% respectively. Time to reach the threshold was 53, 40 and 30 minutes.

Starting before the dust exists sounds counter-intuitive until you consider what the mist is doing. A pre-existing mist field intercepts dust at the moment of generation, when particles are still large, still concentrated, and still moving slowly. Mist started afterwards has to chase an aerosol that has already dispersed into a volume a hundred times larger.

Field work on spray angle tells the same story from a different angle. In the *Scientific Reports* study, a 0° (straight ahead) jet gave the highest dust reduction within the first 10 m, because contact time dominated in the early stage. A 45° jet gave a wider residual mist cloud and the highest overall removal efficiency at 86.7%, because after the pump stopped, the cloud kept working. Both are correct; they answer different moments.

For a tunnel crew this translates into three operating rules that cost nothing to adopt:

1. **Start the cannon before the blast, not after the shout.** Pre-wetting the face and the muck pile also stops previously settled dust from being re-entrained by the shock wave — a secondary source that is routinely ignored.
2. **Keep it running after the visible dust clears.** The residual cloud is still capturing particles while it drifts. Stopping at the moment the face looks clear wastes the last and cheapest part of the work.
3. **Angle across the tunnel, not straight down the axis.** Straight-ahead gives reach in the first ten metres and little afterwards. A wider angle trades distance for a longer-lived cloud, which in a confined space is usually the better trade.

## 9. The water budget is real, but it is not the headline

The reason water volume comes up so often in tunnel dust discussions is that it is the one cost that shows up immediately and visibly.

A cannon is genuinely efficient on this metric. Compared with water monitors and sprinkler trucks, air-assisted spray equipment is commonly reported to use 70–80% less water for the same covered area, because the water is delivered as droplets carried by air rather than dumped as a stream.

But overspray in a tunnel is not free, and the costs are specific:

- **Humidity above about 90%** accelerates corrosion of equipment and steel, degrades electrical insulation, and can affect early-age concrete strength in the lining.
- **Visibility below about 10 m** stops mucking and transport, which is a direct schedule cost and a safety hazard in its own right.
- **Water on the rock mass** softens mudstone and shale along bedding and joint surfaces, which is a ground-control concern, not just a housekeeping one.
- **Water in the mix** on a wet-mix shotcrete face changes the water-cement ratio at the surface and affects the finished lining.
- **Water volume itself.** Shield tunnelling is routinely quoted at 5–8 tonnes of water per metre advanced; over a 10 km metro line that is 50,000 to 80,000 tonnes for dust suppression alone, before any treatment or disposal.

The right response to this is not to spray less. It is to spray *usefully*: aim at the source, use the droplet size the dust requires, stop when the dust is gone, and measure rather than assume. A cannon running at 120 L/min for four minutes at the right place beats one running at 240 L/min for twenty minutes at the wrong place — and uses a tenth of the water.

Which is why the fixed 15-minute ventilation rule after blasting and a dust-triggered spray schedule should not be treated as alternatives. Ventilation clears the gas; the cannon handles the particulate; both should stop when the measurement says so, not when the clock does.

## 10. A selection checklist that reflects tunnel conditions

Most cannon datasheets are written for coal yards, ports and demolition sites. Open ground, unlimited headroom, wind from a known direction, no ceiling to trap the mist. A tunnel is the opposite of all four.

What actually needs checking, in order:

**Droplet size before throw distance.** Ask for the droplet diameter at the intended operating pressure, and check it against the dust you have. If the target is respirable dust, the number that matters is the fine end of the distribution, not the mean. A machine that throws 80 m at 150 µm is a different tool from one that throws 50 m at 100 µm, and the second is usually the right one underground.

**Overall height and footprint.** A single-track heading is 6.6–7.0 m high and less than 6 m wide, and the working lane is the transport lane. Verify the actual working height with the cannon at maximum pitch, not at rest, and verify the width it needs when a muck truck passes.

**Independent fan and pump control, with continuous dry running.** The fan and the pump should be switchable separately, and the fan should be rated for continuous running with the pump off — that is what makes the machine usable for airflow work and for cooling. Check that the control logic cannot start the pump by accident, and that the motor cooling does not depend on water flow.

**Pitch and rotation range.** Standard requires −10° to 60° pitch and up to 320° rotation. In a tunnel the useful pitch range is narrower but the rotation matters, because the machine often has to serve both the face and the muck loading area without being repositioned.

**Mounting and travel.** Skid, trailer, flat wagon, or integrated on the drill jig. On a heading advancing several metres a day, a machine that takes half an hour to move will be left behind and will stop being used. This is a bigger determinant of real-world performance than any nozzle specification.

**Water supply and pressure.** Standard operating band is 1.5–3.5 MPa. Tunnel supply is often a branch off the construction water line with variable pressure and visible solids. A booster pump and a filtration stage are not accessories; they are what keeps the nozzles alive. Nozzle blockage from particulate in the water line is one of the most commonly reported causes of poor performance in underground spraying, and the standard practice fix is filtration plus fewer, larger-orifice nozzles.

**Materials.** Cement dust is alkaline and abrasive; tunnel water is often aggressive. Stainless or coated wetted parts, and wear-resistant nozzle inserts, pay for themselves in a heading where maintenance access is difficult.

**Control and safety.** Remote control at 100 m is in the standard for a reason: the operator should not be standing in the dust the machine is making. Confirm the remote works through the tunnel's RF environment and around the jig, not just on an open pad.

**Power.** 380 V where the supply is reliable; onboard generator where it is not. On deep headings with long cable runs, voltage drop is a real constraint and is worth calculating rather than assuming.

**Noise.** The standard caps the equipment at 85 dB(A) at 1 m. That number was written for equipment on a site, not for a machine operating in a space where the baseline is already high from drilling, ventilation and diesel. In a heading, the cannon's contribution is one more source in an already noisy environment, and the mitigation is placement and scheduling, not just a quieter fan. Where explosion-protected configurations are needed for a specific application — a gas-bearing stratum, a petrochemical-adjacent tunnel — they can be provided to suit the customer's application, with the specific protection type and certificate supplied with the project.

## 11. Commissioning is where most installations are won or lost

A cannon that is correctly sized and badly commissioned performs like an undersized one, and the failure is usually invisible because the machine is clearly running.

Four things to verify on site, in the first week:

**Measure the delivered droplet size, not the design value.** Pump wear, partially blocked nozzles and low supply pressure all push the distribution coarser. A simple check: if the plume stops looking like mist and starts looking like rain within ten metres, the effective droplet size is larger than specified, and the fine-dust capture is gone.

**Map the actual airflow.** Smoke test the heading, note the direction and speed at the cannon's intended position, and note where the return air runs. Then place the machine to fire across the flow. On a long heading the airflow at the face can differ from the airflow at the machine, particularly where a bench or an invert excavation creates an eddy.

**Set the spray position by measurement.** Start at 10 m from the face for the early interception benefit, and test whether the residual cloud after shutdown still covers the working area if the machine is set back to 15–25 m. Both configurations have a defensible basis in the literature and the right one depends on the heading's ventilation and the shift pattern.

**Instrument it.** A dust sensor at the operator's position and one at the closest downstream workstation turn the spray from a habit into a control loop. This is also the only way to demonstrate to an owner or a regulator that the equipment is doing what the specification claimed. Where a project has a monitoring platform, tying the cannon's start and stop to a PM10 threshold rather than to a timer converts water savings into something visible on a bill.

## 12. What the machine cannot do

It is worth being direct about the limits, because a specification that overpromises gets abandoned after the first bad shift.

A fog cannon does not remove gases. Carbon monoxide and nitrogen oxides from blasting and diesel engines are handled by ventilation and, where needed, by catalytic treatment. Mist can absorb a small fraction of soluble fume, but that is a side effect, not a design function.

A fog cannon does not replace ventilation. Dilution requires a minimum air velocity, and the ventilation circuit is the only system that removes gas and carries heat out of the heading. The fan-only mode described in Section 6 belongs beside the ventilation design, not in place of it: it stirs what the duct cannot reach, and its cooling works by evaporation and by moving air past people, which changes the temperature the crew experiences without removing the heat load from the tunnel. The two systems should be designed together — a common recommendation in tunnel practice is to match the cannon's coverage with the ventilation layout rather than installing each in isolation.

A fog cannon does not substitute for respiratory protection at the face, particularly during shotcreting and during the first minutes after a blast. The measured numbers above show why: even good suppression leaves respirable dust at levels that are not a substitute for a mask at the nozzle.

And a fog cannon does not fix a dust problem that originates in method. Wet drilling, water-bag or water-cushion blasting, wet-mix shotcrete instead of dry-mix, sealing conveyor transfer points, and watering the muck pile before loading all reduce the dust that has to be suppressed. In the Jinyunshan case, the improvement attributed to the cannon came alongside a switch from dry to wet shotcrete — the two were reported together and they should be read together. Equipment works best on a problem that has already been reduced.

## 13. What this means for a specification

If a project is writing a specification for tunnel dust suppression right now, the useful changes are small and mostly cost nothing.

Write the target in terms of the dust, not the equipment: respirable dust concentration at the operator position during drilling, blasting, mucking and shotcreting, measured over a shift. Then require the equipment to demonstrate it, on site, with the project's own dust.

Require the droplet size at the operating pressure to be stated, and require it to be verified. This single line removes most of the room for a machine that meets its throw distance by making rain.

Require a mounting and travel solution that keeps up with the heading. Ask how long it takes to reposition, and who does it.

Require a commissioning protocol: airflow map, spray position trial, dust measurement before and after, and a written operating schedule covering pre-blast start, post-blast duration and shutdown criteria.

And keep the ventilation design in the same document. The dust control system in a tunnel is a sequence — suppression at the source, capture along the path, dilution and removal by air — and a cannon is one stage of it. Specified as the whole solution it will disappoint; specified as the first stage it can reduce face dust by an order of magnitude, which is exactly what the Jinyunshan numbers show.
