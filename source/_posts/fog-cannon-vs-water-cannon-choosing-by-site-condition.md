A dust problem on an industrial site is usually described by its size: how many tonnes of material, how many tonnes of dust, how big an area. Those numbers decide how much suppression you need. They do not decide which machine to buy. Two devices that both put water into the air can behave completely differently on the same site, and the reason is not quality, price or brand — it is that one of them makes droplets small enough to catch dust, and the other makes droplets large enough to fall straight past it.

That distinction is worth making early, because it is invisible in both quotation formats. A fog cannon is quoted with a throw distance in metres and an air volume. A high-pressure water cannon — commonly called a water spray monitor — is quoted with a working pressure and a flow rate. Neither format states the droplet spectrum, and it is the one number that decides whether the machine suppresses dust at all.

What follows is a working sequence for choosing between them. It is organised around site condition rather than around machine type, because conditions are what you can actually go and look at.

## The Number That Is Missing From Both Quotations

Droplet size is the first question, and it should be asked before distance, before power, and before price.

Dust is captured by collision, interception and settling. All three mechanisms depend on the ratio between the droplet size and the dust particle size — and when a droplet is much larger than the particle it is meant to catch, the efficiency falls away. The droplet has too much mass for the particle to entrain it, and it falls out of the air on a trajectory that never intersects the plume.

The bands are well established, and the industry reference points are consistent:

- **Below about 20 µm** the droplets stay suspended almost indefinitely. They can combine with dust into a fine aerosol, reduce visibility and even raise measured particulate readings rather than lower them. Equipment in this band belongs to point-source capture and to humidification, not to open-area suppression.
- **Roughly 30–100 µm** is the working range for open-area suppression. These droplets collide well with typical airborne mineral and coal dust, and they still have enough fall velocity to settle to the ground afterwards — which matters as much as the capture itself. Droplets that capture dust but never land leave the concentration where it was, merely redistributed.
- **Above about 150 µm** the droplets are simply too heavy. They fall too fast to spend meaningful time in the dust plume, most of them strike the ground or the stockpile without capturing anything, and the result is a wet yard rather than suppressed dust. This is the band a high-pressure water cannon produces, and it is the single reason a water cannon can look effective on the day of commissioning and disappoint in ordinary operation.

The other two requirements follow from the same logic and are just as often left out: total spray volume must exceed the dust generation rate, and the spray pattern must actually cover the emitting zone. Droplet size alone is not a guarantee — an atomiser spraying the right particles at one tenth of the required volume will also fail.

![Three droplet bands: below 20 um will not settle, 30 to 100 um is the working range, above 150 um falls straight past the dust](/images/fog-cannon-vs-water-cannon-droplet-bands.svg)

This is why a fog cannon's own published specification carries both numbers, and why the mechanical industry standard for air-assisted spraying devices ties throw distance to droplet diameter rather than quoting reach alone. A machine specified for long throw is not thereby specified for fine capture; it is specified for its range.

## Two Machines, One Word in the Trade

First, a naming problem worth settling, because it causes real confusion in enquiries. In English, *water cannon* is not a dust-control term. Its primary meanings are a fire monitor on a fire appliance and a crowd-control water vehicle. A buyer who reads *water cannon* in an offer will reasonably picture something from a fire station. In practice the international dust-control trade does use the phrase loosely, which is exactly why it needs clarifying.

The terms that describe the machine accurately are **water spray monitor** (the term used in fire-protection standards for a fixed or trailer-mounted jet monitor) and **high-pressure water spray monitor**. If a specification calls for a "water cannon", the first thing to establish is whether a jet monitor for dust suppression is actually intended.

The other machine needs no introduction in this trade. An air-assisted fog cannon atomises water into a fine fog and uses a high-volume air stream to carry that fog across open ground. The formal name in the mechanical industry standard is *air-assisted spraying device for dust suppression*, and the product range most commonly seen runs from about 30 to 300 metres of throw, with the published droplet spectrum varying by model.

Two machines, two delivery principles, two droplet spectra. Everything else in this article follows from that.

## The Mechanism Difference Is the Whole Difference

Put the two next to each other and the difference shows up in the water path itself.

![How water travels in a fog cannon compared with a high-pressure water spray monitor](/images/fog-cannon-vs-water-cannon-mechanism.svg)

**A fog cannon builds a cloud and moves it.** Water is metered to a nozzle assembly, sheared into fine droplets by air pressure at the nozzle, and then picked up by a large volume of air from a fan. The fan is the delivery system: it does not remove dust, it transports the droplets that do. Reach is a function of air volume and nozzle geometry, which is why throw distance is a headline number on this machine and why it can cross 100 metres without the water pressure rising to anything unusual.

**A high-pressure water spray monitor pressurises water and projects it.** A pump raises the pressure, a small orifice breaks the stream, and the result is a coherent jet delivered straight at the target — 10 to 30 metres in ordinary industrial duties, with pressures in the region of a hundred bar and above depending on the pump, the line and the orifice chosen. There is no air stream and no fine fog. The droplets are large, typically above 150 µm, and they arrive with a great deal of kinetic energy.

That is the whole problem in one sentence. A coarse droplet falls out of the air faster than the dust plume can interact with it, so the jet wets the surface it strikes and leaves the suspended dust in the air. **The result is a wet yard, not a suppressed plume.**

That gives each machine a natural failure mode, and they are not the same failure. A fog cannon that is correctly configured and aimed into an obstruction delivers its fog to the obstruction — a positioning error with an obvious remedy. A monitor whose droplets are too coarse has no remedy at all except to change the machine, because the problem is in the atomisation, not in the aim. This is why a water cannon can be commissioned successfully, photograph well, and still disappoint in ordinary operation: on the day it is installed the yard is wet, and nobody can tell the difference between a wet yard and a clean one by looking.

## Condition One: Droplet Size Before Distance

If the droplet size question has been answered, the distance question becomes much easier, because most of the wrong answers fall away.

**Wherever the suppression is happening, the droplets have to be in the working band.** Coarse droplets falling fast will wet the ground and miss the plume. Very fine droplets that never settle will keep the concentration in the air rather than reducing it. The published figures for industrial fog systems cluster in the tens-of-microns range, and the industry reference value for open-area suppression sits around 50 to 100 µm — a band that both collides well with airborne mineral and coal dust and retains enough fall velocity to reach the ground afterwards.

**A water cannon is the wrong instrument for this, at any distance.** Its droplet band is fixed by the nozzle and the pressure, and it sits above the working band. It has legitimate uses — washing down surfaces, wetting a haul road, knocking down a short-term nuisance plume at close range — but wetting a surface and suppressing suspended dust are different jobs, and only one of them is what a dust problem needs.

**Where a fog cannon belongs, distance then decides the model.** Within the family, throw distance is the sizing variable: the stand-off distance available on a site is set by the layout — the width of a stockpile, the depth of an open pit, the length of a haul road, the distance between the fence line and the nearest building. Specify the model by the distance from the proposed mount point to the far edge of the emission zone, not to the centre of the pile. Dust does not stay at the centre.

**Inside roughly 20 metres, close does not mean coarse.** This is where the most common mistake is made. Loading points, transfer platforms, crusher discharge lips and conveyor galleries are short-range problems, and the instinct is to install a short-range machine. But the droplet requirement does not relax with distance — if anything the reasoning tightens, because at close range there is no travel time for a coarse droplet to be slowed, dispersed or captured by chance. The correct short-range answer is a fine-atomising system chosen for a fine droplet spectrum: a fog cannon where the air stream has room, a dry fog line where it does not, and enclosure with extraction where the source can be closed at all.

## Condition Two: Whether the Source Is Open to the Sky

Air-assisted suppression and enclosed suppression are not the same activity.

A fog cloud needs room. It needs to be delivered into open air, and it needs a direction to travel. Put one inside a fully enclosed building and it will produce a visible fog layer that settles — technically suppressing, practically useless, and expensive to run. That is why fog cannons belong on stockpiles, in open yards, on haul roads and on open transfer areas, and why they do not belong as the primary measure inside a crusher house or a conveyor gallery.

Inside those spaces the answer is a different instrument for a different reason. A jet monitor's coarse droplets are worse inside than outside: the extra volume of coarse spray in a semi-enclosed space wets the floor, raises humidity and adds a slurry to clean up, without addressing the concentration in the air. The correct instruments are a **dry fog system** where the dust is fine and the material must not be wetted, and enclosure with extraction capture where the emission point can be closed at all. The organising rule is simple and worth stating plainly, because it is the single most misunderstood idea in this sector: **a dry fog line serves a point, a fog cannon serves an area.** They are the two ends of one process chain, not a lesser and a better version of the same product.

## Condition Three: What the Water Is Allowed to Touch

Water is a tool on one side of a process and a defect on the other, and the boundary is set by the material, not by the equipment.

Coarse, open, wind-driven material — aggregate, coal for a stockpile that will be burned or loaded, ore, limestone, sinter feed, open-air earthworks, roads — tolerates wetting. So does material that will subsequently be processed wet. For all of these the material boundary is satisfied, and the choice reduces to droplet spectrum and reach. Note what that does and does not release you from: wetting the material is acceptable, but the water still has to be fine enough to catch the dust rather than merely to soak the pile.

The picture inverts wherever the material is a finished or semi-finished product, or where added water causes a chemical or safety consequence. Cement after the mill is the standard example: ordinary Portland cement is a hydraulic binder, and premature wetting triggers pre-hydration, which shows up as build-up on silo walls, blocked outlet chutes, sluggish air-slide flow, belt slip and strength loss. In such places a fog cannon or a monitor can be technically correct and still be the wrong decision, and the correct answer is capture, enclosure or a dry fog system that does not add moisture to the product.

There is a second boundary of the same kind. In applications involving combustible material, water quantity and equipment configuration are questions to be settled per application: **ex-protection configurations can be provided to suit the customer's application, with the specific protection type and certificate supplied with the project.** Compressed-air blowdown of accumulated dust is never an acceptable substitute in such areas, because it builds the suspended cloud that the water was there to prevent.

## Condition Four: Wind, Shelter and Air Movement

An air-assisted fog cannon is a wind-dependent device, and this is both its strength and its weakness. It works *with* the air: in a light breeze a fog plume travels further than the quoted throw and spreads across a wider area, which is exactly what an open yard needs. Against a strong wind it loses reach, and the plume can shear off the target.

A jet monitor is largely indifferent to wind over its short throw, because its delivery is hydraulic momentum rather than a suspended cloud. That is a real advantage when the question is washing a surface. It is not an advantage for suppression, because a droplet that is unaffected by wind is also a droplet large enough to fall straight through the plume.

The practical consequence is that wind should be recorded rather than assumed. Direction and speed across a normal working day, by season, determine how many azimuth positions a fixed fog cannon needs, and which sheltered zones inside the site need a second unit positioned to reach them. Sites with strong seasonal variation often end up with fog cannons at several azimuths, sized for the far edge of the emission zone rather than for its centre.

## Condition Five: Power, Water and Winter

Every site constraint eventually becomes a number on a datasheet.

**Power.** A fog cannon runs a fan and a pump together, so its connected load is higher than a pump-only system at the same duty. Sites with limited power capacity, or with a supply that cannot support simultaneous operation of several units, need this checked before the layout is fixed — not after.

**Water quality.** Fog systems depend on consistent atomisation, which depends on water with controlled hardness, suspended solids and iron content. Scaling and suspended solids affect the nozzle assembly and the filtration; a supply that varies seasonally will produce a system whose performance varies with it. Where the available water is difficult, multi-stage filtration and automatic back-flush become part of the design rather than optional extras.

**Freezing.** Any water-fed system freezes if left full and idle. The exposure is a winter function of site location, not of machine type.

**Service life.** Design service life, stated separately from warranty and after-sales support: **with disciplined operation, the complete machine remains usable for more than 10 years**, on three conditions — consumables replaced on schedule, water quality within the agreed range, and winter anti-freezing procedures followed. Lifetime is an operating discipline, not a purchase specification.

## Condition Six: Who Is Going to Turn It On

The least technical question on this list decides more outcomes than the other five combined.

A fixed fog cannon is normally configured for automatic or scheduled operation, and that is where its value is greatest: it suppresses the emission before it travels rather than after someone notices. Integrated with on-site particulate and weather monitoring, it can start ahead of a dust-forming event and hold concentration below a limit continuously, instead of relying on a last-minute correction. It also produces a record rather than an opinion.

An attended water cannon is a different kind of instrument. It is fast and it is adaptable, and it puts water exactly where you point it — which makes it the right tool for washing a spillage, wetting a haul road, or knocking down a short-term nuisance plume. But it needs a person with water, a hose and a clear line of sight, and if the person is not there at 03:00 when a conveyor trips and dumps a load, it does nothing.

The difference in kind is what matters. A coarse monitor is often used as if it were suppression, producing a wet surface that nobody questions. A fog system is asked to produce a concentration reading it can defend, and it leaves a record of having done so.

## A Decision Sequence That Survives Contact With a Site Visit

A workable sequence, in order:

1. **Establish the droplet spectrum of each machine under offer**, and discard any option whose droplets sit above the working band for open-area suppression. This is not a comparison between two products — it is a check on whether either product is doing the job being paid for.
2. **List every emission point and classify each as open, partially sheltered, or enclosed.** Enclosed points leave this comparison entirely and go to capture or dry fog.
3. **Measure the stand-off distance available at each open point**, from the realistic mount location to the far edge of the emission zone.
4. **Apply the material boundary.** Confirm for each point whether the material being handled or stored tolerates wetting, and where it does not, remove the water option before comparing machines.
5. **Check spray volume against dust generation**, not against nozzle rating. Volume has to exceed the dust being produced, or the correct droplet size changes nothing.
6. **Record wind direction and speed by season**, and mark which areas a fog plume can reach and which it cannot.
7. **Check power, water quality and freeze exposure** at each candidate location, and decide the operating model: which points need continuous unattended coverage and which need attended response.
8. **Then choose the machine**, and specify it by throw distance and atomisation performance rather than by output power.

![Seven site conditions, each of which can remove a machine from the shortlist](/images/fog-cannon-vs-water-cannon-decision-map.svg)

Seven of those steps are free. They take a site visit and a wind record. Skipping them is the expensive way to buy equipment.

## Where the Honest Answer Is Two Systems

On a well-run site, the two technologies are not competitors at all. A fog system holds the open area — the stockpile, the yard, the road, the far field where the plume is already forming. A dry fog line handles what is close, specific and fine: a transfer point, a chute, a conveyor gallery, a point source where the material must not be wetted.

The water cannon belongs in neither list. It has its place, but the place is not suppression: washing down spillage, wetting a haul road, knocking down a short-term nuisance plume at close range, and the plant-safety work that goes with all three. When one is installed to do those jobs and then counted as dust control, the yard looks clean on the day and the concentration does not move.

That is the distinction worth carrying out of a selection discussion. **Wetting a surface is not the same as reducing a concentration**, and only one of them shows up in a monitoring record.

It is in sizing to measured conditions: how much material, how much dust, what stand-off distance, what water, what power, what wind, and what the operating model is.
