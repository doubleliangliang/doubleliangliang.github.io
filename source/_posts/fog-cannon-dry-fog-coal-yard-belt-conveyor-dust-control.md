Ask a power plant what its emission numbers are and you will get a stack figure, because that is what the continuous monitoring system reports and what the regulator checks. Ask where the dust complaints come from and you will get a different answer: the coal yard, the transfer tower, the road behind the tippler house.

Those two answers describe two different problems. The stack problem was largely solved during the ultra-low emission retrofit programme, which pushed the commonly applied limits for particulate matter, SO2 and NOx at coal-fired units to 10, 35 and 50 mg per cubic metre and backed them with continuous monitoring. The coal handling problem was not solved, because it has no stack, no meter and no single owner. It is a fugitive emission problem, and it is now the part of the file that inspectors, neighbours and occupational health officers actually look at.

This article lays out the practical engineering of that second problem: where the dust comes from, when to use a long-range fog cannon and when to use micron dry fog, what decides whether the installation still works in year three, and how to specify acceptance so that the result can be defended.

## Why Coal Handling Is the Weakest Link in a Power Plant's Emission File

A coal-fired plant moves an enormous amount of material through an open or semi-open system. Every tonne is dropped, dragged, crushed, stacked and reclaimed, and every one of those events releases dust. The reason this stays invisible on paper is structural rather than technical.

**There is no measurement point.** A stack has a CEMS and a permitted limit. A wagon tippler pit has neither. Without a number, there is no baseline, no trend and no alarm.

**The emission is intermittent.** A tippler discharges for seconds. A grab passes overhead for a few seconds more. A gust crosses an exposed pile and the plume is gone. Averaging over a shift hides the worst minute of the day, which is exactly the minute somebody photographs.

**The cost is distributed across departments.** Coal lost as dust is a fuel cost. Dust in a gallery is a maintenance and safety cost. Dust over the fence is a compliance cost. Dust in the breathing zone is an occupational health cost. Because no single budget line owns it, it tends to be under-specified.

**Water is not free.** This is the constraint most retrofit designs discover late. Coal has a moisture specification. Add too much water and you get belt slip, chute build-up, screen blinding, lower calorific value at the weighbridge, and in winter, ice. Any suppression measure that ignores the moisture ceiling will be switched off by the operations team, and a switched-off system has an availability of zero.

## The Four-Stage Source Map

![Four stages of coal handling in a thermal power plant and their fugitive dust sources](/images/coal-handling-dust-source-map.svg)

Coal handling splits cleanly into four stages, and each stage has its own dust behaviour and its own correct measure. Treating all four with one technology is the most common reason a project underperforms.

| Stage | Main source points | Dust behaviour | Primary measure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unloading | Wagon tippler pit, unloading trench, screw unloader, grab ship unloader, intake transfer station | Instantaneous, large volume, wide spread, wind driven | Dry fog at the drop points, enclosure, fog cannon over the working area |
| Conveying | Belt drop points, transfer stations, crushers, roller screens, guide chutes, head and tail pulleys | Continuous, confined, positive-pressure puffing, spreads along the gallery | Sealed guide chute, damping plate, belt-interlocked dry fog, local dedusting |
| Stacking | Stacker-reclaimer drop point, working face, mobile belt ends, haul roads | Wind driven, worse in dry and windy weather, covers the whole yard | On-board dry fog at the drop point, low drop height, road compaction and cleaning |
| Storage | Open pile surface, closed dome, reclaim face, yard roads | Persistent, wind eroded, re-suspended by vehicles, drifts off site | Enclosure first; cannon coverage inside the dome, windbreak net and surface binding outside |

Two rules follow directly from the table. **Enclose before you spray.** Every cubic metre of dusty air you stop from escaping is a cubic metre you do not have to wet. **Match the droplet to the dust, not to the brochure.** The stages differ in droplet requirement by an order of magnitude.

## Fog Cannon or Dry Fog: Two Tools, Not Two Brands

![Droplet spectrum and water budget comparison for fog cannon versus dry fog selection](/images/fog-cannon-vs-dry-fog-selection.svg)

The two technologies are frequently sold against each other. In a coal plant they are complements.

A **dry fog system** produces droplets in the 5 to 10 micron range, produced by a micron dry-fog host unit with atomising nozzles, an air-water separator and a solenoid valve group. Because the droplet is close to the size of the dust it is meant to catch, inertial capture works. Because the droplet is tiny, it evaporates quickly and adds very little water. The price of that is range: a dry fog nozzle only works if it is at the source, inside the enclosure, metres from the impact.

A **long-range fog cannon** produces a much coarser spray, typically in the 50 to 200 micron band, and throws it tens to hundreds of metres with a fan. It knocks down visible plumes over open areas and wets the surface of a pile, which is a different job. It does far less for respirable dust at distance, and it adds far more water per unit of dust captured.

The decision is therefore not "which is better". It is two questions: **how close can a nozzle physically get to the dust**, and **how much water can this coal accept**. Where the answer to the first is "inside the chute", dry fog wins outright. Where the answer is "a hundred metres away across an open pile", only the cannon can reach, and the water has to be metered and budgeted.

## Where Each Technology Belongs

### Unloading: tippler, trench and grab

The wagon tippler is the single most violent dust event in a coal plant. A wagon inverts and discharges several dozen tonnes in seconds, and the resulting pulse of dusty air has to go somewhere. The sequence that works is enclosure first, then dry fog at the discharge, then negative pressure collection at the top of the pit.

Where the enclosure cannot be made complete, a fog cannon covers the working area, and it should be triggered by the tippler signal rather than by a timer. At a ship unloader, a cannon mounted to track the grab gives coverage that follows the material instead of waiting for the plume to form. Pre-wetting the incoming coal on the approach track is also worth specifying; it is cheap and it reduces the dust load of every downstream stage.

### Conveying: the transfer point is the whole game

![Cross section of a belt transfer point with sealed guide chute and dry fog nozzle row](/images/belt-transfer-point-dry-fog-layout.svg)

A belt transfer point generates dust continuously for as long as the belt runs, and it is where a well-designed dry fog system pays for itself. Four elements matter.

**Seal the chute.** A widened and heightened guide chute with rubber sealing strips is the baseline. Without it, the fog is fighting a jet of dusty air rather than a pile of dust.

**Kill the pressure pulse.** Coal falling into a chute pushes air ahead of it, and that air is what carries dust out of every gap. A damping plate inside the chute removes most of it before the fog ever starts.

**Put the nozzles inside, at the impact.** A dry fog row mounted in the chute roof over the drop point, sized to chute width and belt speed, catches dust while it is still concentrated. Nozzles mounted outside the chute mostly wet the outside of the chute.

**Interlock with the belt.** Fog on when the belt runs and material flows, off a short delay after the belt stops. This single rule prevents most of the water-related complaints that get systems disabled: no water on an empty belt, no water during a long stoppage, no icing in January.

### Stacking: the stacker-reclaimer

The stacker-reclaimer moves, so the suppression has to move with it. On-board dry fog at the boom drop point, started with the machine, is the standard answer. The second lever is operational rather than mechanical: reducing drop height and using layered compaction when building a pile reduces emission at no capital cost. Yard roads are a separate problem and are solved by hardening, sweeping and speed limits, not by spraying.

### Storage: dome or open yard

![Coal dome with four tower-mounted fog cannons and an open yard with windbreak net and tower cannon](/images/coal-yard-cannon-layout-closed-and-open.svg)

A closed dome changes the problem from "suppress an open source" to "condition a large enclosed volume". In practice this means fixed tower-mounted cannons inside, arranged so their arcs overlap, plus a dry fog curtain at the vehicle doors that opens on vehicle presence. A reference configuration for a dome around 100 metres in diameter is four cannon units in the 80 metre class. Similar 80 metre integrated tower units have been applied at coastal power plants in Guangdong.

An open yard has no such option. The measures are a windbreak net at least 1.1 times the pile height, cannon coverage designed with overlap, surface binding agent on long-term piles, and covering or compacting idle areas. Where the yard is large, a 200 metre class tower cannon covers ground that several small units would struggle to reach, but it must be part of a layout drawing and not a single heroic purchase.

## Five Engineering Details That Decide the Outcome

**1. Interlock sequence, written down.** The operating rule is simple and worth putting into the control philosophy: start suppression before the conveying equipment, stop the conveying equipment before stopping suppression. Plants that get this backwards either run dry for the first minute of every start or leave water running after every stop.

**2. A moisture ceiling agreed with operations.** Define the acceptable moisture increase before selecting flow rates, and tie spray duty to it. For incoming coal, a total moisture band in the order of 8 to 10 percent is commonly used as a reference in unloading specifications. Whatever number the plant uses, it has to be a number the production team signed, not a number the supplier assumed.

**3. A wind rule.** Above an agreed wind speed, open-air stacking and unloading should stop and the yard spray programme should increase, not the other way round. A threshold around 5 metres per second is a common trigger in domestic operating rules. Spraying into a strong crosswind does not suppress dust; it just moves the water.

**4. Water quality and winter.** Dry fog nozzles are small and they clog. Filtration, a maintained cleaning interval and a water quality specification are not optional extras. In freezing climates, drainage, heat tracing and antifreeze provisions have to be in the original design. A system that is bypassed every winter does not have a winter problem, it has an annual availability problem.

**5. A maintenance rhythm that matches the site.** Nozzle and filter cleaning weekly, seals and curtains inspected on a cycle, dust sensors calibrated monthly, and a named owner for the data. Suppression equipment fails quietly; without a person checking, nobody notices until the inspector does.

## Acceptance: How to Prove It Works

Dust projects lose credibility through bad measurement far more often than through bad equipment. A defensible acceptance method has four parts.

**Fix the targets before commissioning.** Typical programme targets used in domestic power plant fugitive dust specifications are fence line particulate matter at or below 1.0 mg per cubic metre, workplace total dust at or below 4 mg per cubic metre, respirable dust at or below 2.5 mg per cubic metre, full availability of the installed suppression facilities, and no visible dust escape from any generating point. Agree which of these are contractual and how they will be sampled.

**Measure at defined points, in comparable conditions.** Same monitor, same wind window, before and after. A calm afternoon compared with a windy morning proves nothing.

**Use continuous data, not spot checks.** Online particulate monitors at hot spots and at the fence line, logged at one-minute resolution, give a distribution you can argue with. Retain the record for at least 30 days so that a specific shift can be reconstructed.

**Track the inputs as well as the outcome.** Water, electricity, run hours and availability per zone. A system that holds concentration while cutting water consumption is the result both the plant manager and the finance department can sign.

## Seven Common Mistakes

1. **One cannon for the whole problem.** Coal handling has four stages and a dozen source types. Coverage is a layout exercise, not a single machine.
2. **Coarse spray for respirable dust.** The visible plume disappears and the PM10 number barely moves.
3. **Dry fog mounted outside the enclosure.** It wets the steelwork instead of the dust.
4. **No interlock with belt and machine signals.** Timed spraying either misses the event or runs on nothing.
5. **No moisture ceiling.** Operations switches the system off, and nobody can argue with them.
6. **No windbreak, no enclosure, all spray.** Water cannot replace a physical barrier; it can only supplement one.
7. **No maintenance owner and no data retention.** The equipment degrades silently and there is no evidence either way.

## The Purchase Price Is the Smallest Number

Most sourcing decisions for this equipment are still made on three columns: throw, flow, price. None of the three predicts five-year cost. In this industry the gap between a cheap unit and a durable one shows up in three places.

**Underperformance means buying twice.** If effective coverage or droplet spectrum falls short, the plume stays and acceptance does not pass. The remedy is additional units, new foundations, new cable runs and a second shutdown window, usually against a compliance deadline, which is the most expensive schedule there is.

**Unstable quality is paid for in operating hours.** Nozzles clog, pumps lose pressure, slewing drives wear, cabinets fail in a coal dust atmosphere. Every failure removes suppression from that zone, and the requirement is continuous rather than average. Repairs inside a live gallery have to be coordinated around generation, so the real cost is measured in operating hours, not spare parts.

**Poor industrial design shortens service life.** Corrosion protection, bearing and motor protection, winter drainage, and whether a technician can actually reach the parts that fail decide whether a unit lasts three seasons or fifteen years. Early replacement is never priced at the equipment tag.

A more useful comparison sheet asks about the brand and local availability of pump, motor, gearbox, PLC and nozzles; corrosion class and measured duty hours in comparable plants; documented continuous operation records; how many design iterations the maker has shipped in this industry; and spare-part lead time and service response in writing. ## Outlook: From Equipment to Operating Discipline

The direction of travel in coal handling is the same as in every other bulk material industry. Fugitive dust is becoming an operating data problem rather than a procurement problem: interlocked instead of timed, per-zone instead of yard-wide, and evidenced continuously instead of assembled before an audit. Plants that make that shift end up with lower water consumption and a better compliance position than plants that simply bought a bigger machine.

The technology is not the constraint. The constraint is whether the moisture ceiling was agreed, whether the interlock was commissioned, and whether somebody still owns the nozzles in year three.
