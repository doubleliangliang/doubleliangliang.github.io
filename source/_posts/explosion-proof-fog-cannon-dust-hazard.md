A fog cannon has one job: put droplets of the right size into the air, in the right place, at the right time. On an ordinary yard that is a straightforward engineering task. On a site where the material in the air can ignite — coal, grain, flour, starch, aluminium, magnesium, polymer — the same machine becomes a device that has to be justified to the people who own the risk. A single spark from a motor terminal is enough. So the question changes: not "how far does it throw", but "what is the evidence that this machine will not be the ignition source".

This article is about that second question. It treats the explosion-proof fog cannon as what it actually is — a dust suppression machine whose ignition risk has been engineered out — and works through what determines that engineering, where it belongs in a layered protection scheme, and what has to be confirmed before a purchase order is raised. It is written for engineers and HSE specialists who will have to sign the specification, and for procurement teams who need to know which questions to ask a supplier.

## The Question That Arrives Before the Specification

Most enquiries about explosion-proof equipment start the wrong way round. They start with a label. Someone has written "Ex" on a quotation, or heard that a machine is "explosion-proof", and the technical conversation begins from that word.

The word on its own carries very little engineering content. Explosion protection is not a single property that a machine either has or lacks. It is a **configuration**, assembled part by part, each part chosen against a specific hazard: what the material is, whether it burns as a gas or as a dust, what its ignition temperature is, and where on the plant the machine will stand. Two machines sold as "explosion-proof fog cannons" can differ in every one of those respects.

So the useful opening question is not "is it Ex-rated". It is:

- What material, and in what physical form — a bulk solid that produces a dust cloud, or a vapour that forms an explosive gas mixture?
- Where exactly will the machine be installed, and what are the characteristics of that location?
- What protection level does that location require under the standard that applies in the jurisdiction where it will run?

Once those three are answered, the Ex marking follows from them. The marking is the output of the analysis, not the input to the enquiry.

## Reading an Ex Marking Without Guessing

Ex markings look cryptic because they are compressed. A typical marking reads as a sequence: `Ex` followed by the protection type, the equipment group, the gas or dust group, the temperature class, and the equipment protection level. Take a representative industrial marking for a dust application — `Ex tb IIIB T135°C Db` — and each part has a specific job.

**`Ex`** identifies equipment certified to the relevant international and national framework (the IEC 60079 series, adopted in China as GB/T 3836).

**`tb`** is the protection type. For dust applications the family is "t" — equipment protected by enclosure. The letter that follows indicates the level of that protection: `ta` for the highest, `tb` for the standard industrial level, `tc` for the lower level. For gas environments, `d` (flameproof enclosure) is the workhorse type most often seen on motors, and `e` (increased safety), `i` (intrinsic safety) and `p` (pressurised) cover other duties.

**`IIIB`** is the dust group, and this is the part most often overlooked. Group IIIA covers combustible fibres and flyings — cotton, fluff, wood shavings. Group IIIB covers non-conductive dusts — flour, starch, sugar, coal dust, polymer dust. Group IIIC covers **conductive** dusts — aluminium, magnesium, titanium, iron and zinc powders. The progression is by increasing conductivity, because a conductive dust cloud can carry an electrostatic charge and bridge an air gap that would not conduct at all. A machine specified `IIIB` is not a substitute for one specified `IIIC` in a metal-dust plant, whatever else it may have going for it.

**`T135°C`** is the maximum surface temperature. In dust atmospheres the marking states the temperature that the surface may reach, either without a specified dust layer or with a layer of stated thickness. The rule behind it is simple arithmetic: the surface must stay far enough below the dust's ignition temperature that it cannot ignite a cloud. NFPA 652 states the same requirement as a margin — hot surfaces should be at least 112 °F (50 °C) below the minimum ignition temperature of the dust. Where a dust cloud is possible, the cloud ignition temperature is substantially lower than the temperature required to ignite a settled layer, which is why cloud-forming zones are the demanding ones.

**`Db`** is the equipment protection level, equivalent to Zone 21: the level applicable where combustible dust is likely to be present in normal operation. Zone 20 (level `Da`) is reserved for situations where dust is present continuously or for long periods, such as inside a silo or a dust collector; Zone 22 (`Dc`) covers the case where dust is present only abnormally, briefly.

![Anatomy of an Ex marking, showing protection type, dust group, temperature class and protection level](/images/explosion-proof-fog-cannon-marking-anatomy.svg)

The practical value of reading the marking is that it converts a vague claim into a checkable one. "Explosion-proof" cannot be verified. `Ex tb IIIB T135°C Db` can be compared against what the location actually requires — and can be found to be insufficient.

## Dust Is Not One Hazard: Matching the Machine to the Material

Combustible dust is not a single category with a single behaviour. The machine's configuration follows from the material, and materials differ in at least four ways that matter.

**Combustibility and explosibility.** Many organic materials and a number of metals will form an explosive dust cloud when reduced to fine particles. Roughly, particles passing a 40-mesh sieve — about 420 µm — are the ones commonly treated as potentially combustible, though many materials become explosive well below that. The standard test programme for a specific material measures minimum explosible concentration, minimum ignition energy, explosion severity and minimum ignition temperature. **These figures belong to the dust sample, not to the machine.** A supplier cannot supply them; they have to come from a test report on the actual material in the plant.

**The cloud versus the layer.** The same dust can be handled by two different protection strategies depending on whether it is airborne or settled. A settled layer burns slowly and produces heat; a suspended cloud burns fast and produces pressure. A location where dust can only settle calls for containment and housekeeping. A location where a cloud can form calls for ignition control, venting and isolation as well. Fog cannon suppression belongs to the second problem — it exists to reduce the material available to become airborne in the first place.

**Conductive versus non-conductive.** As set out above, metal dusts sit in Group IIIC because they can carry charge. Conductive dust also behaves differently as a residue: a conductive layer on a machine surface is a path for current that a non-conductive layer never provides. Machines installed in metal-dust plants are expected to be bonded and earthed as part of the installation, not treated as an optional refinement.

**Water reactivity.** Some materials react with water. The general assumption behind using water-based suppression does not hold for all of them, and for a small set of applications the choice of suppression medium is a decision that has to be taken from the material data before any equipment is specified. Aluminium, magnesium and calcium carbide are the cases that come up repeatedly, and they are exactly the cases where the dust group also climbs to IIIC.

## Where an Explosion-Proof Fog Cannon Belongs

Not every site needs one, and specifying it where it is not required is money spent on nothing. The machine is needed where three conditions coincide: the material can form a combustible dust cloud, the location is classified for that risk, and suppression has been chosen as one of the controls.

The recurring applications are worth naming, because they recur for the same physical reason rather than by industry fashion:

- **Coal storage and handling.** Enclosed coal sheds, coal towers, conveyor galleries and transfer stations, both at coal-fired power stations and at coal chemical plants. Coal dust is a Group IIIB material and is abundant enough to form a cloud throughout the handling circuit.
- **Coking and coal chemistry.** Around coke ovens, coal towers and belt corridors. The dust is fine, the ignition temperatures are not generous, and the installations are large.
- **Grain, food and powder processing.** Flour mills, starch plants, feed mills, sugar and powdered-ingredient handling. Group IIIB, with high production of airborne dust during milling, screening and bag emptying.
- **Metal powder processing.** Aluminium, magnesium, titanium and zinc. Group IIIC, so the required configuration is stricter, and water reactivity has to be checked against the specific metal.
- **Petrochemical and chemical open areas.** Loading and unloading areas, tank farms, product yards and open-air transfer points. Here the explosive medium is often a vapour rather than a dust, which changes the group from III to II and changes the applicable protection type from enclosure to flameproof or increased safety.

What these sites have in common is not industry but physics: a material that forms a combustible cloud, in a location where that cloud can reach an ignition source. Everything else about them differs.

## Where It Does Not Belong — and What Replaces It

An explosion-proof fog cannon is a specific answer, and there are situations where it is the wrong answer even when the site has a dust hazard.

**Indoors in a building where extraction and capture are available.** A fog cloud needs volume and direction. Inside an enclosed building it forms a visible fog layer that settles — technically suppressing, practically ineffective, and expensive to run. Where the emission point can be enclosed, enclosure with extraction capture is the better measure, and a fog cannon inside the building adds running cost without adding capture.

**Where the dust is captured at source.** For fine dust in a transfer point that can be hooded, capture at the source solves the problem more completely than suppressing the emissions downstream. The fog cannon is the instrument for open, area-scale emission; it is not a substitute for capture.

**Where the water would damage the product.** As discussed in relation to cement, water is a defect on one side of a process and a tool on the other. The material boundary applies regardless of the explosion hazard.

**Where suppression is being asked to do a job it cannot do.** This is the most important exclusion on the list, and it belongs in the first paragraph of any specification that mentions explosion protection. **Dust suppression does not replace explosion venting, isolation, or a dust hazard analysis.** Venting sizing and isolation valves protect against the consequences of an ignition that prevention has failed to stop. Suppression reduces the fuel available to be ignited in normal operation. They are different layers, and a facility with only one of them is incomplete regardless of how good that one layer is.

## Suppression as a Layer: Where It Sits and What It Buys

Layered protection is the standard approach in both the Chinese and North American frameworks, and it is worth being precise about what the suppression layer contributes.

NFPA's combustible dust framework — consolidated in **NFPA 660** (2025), which merges the previous 652, 654, 61, 484, 655 and 664 into a single standard — reduces its philosophy to three verbs: **contain, capture, clean**. That is a useful summary of where an industrial fog system belongs: it belongs to *clean*, with a supporting role in keeping the capture systems working.

The layers below it do the rest:

- **Prevention and ignition control.** Bonding and earthing, control of static discharge, intrinsically safe or protected electrical equipment, avoidance of hot surfaces, control of mechanical sparks. This is the layer that decides whether an ignition happens at all.
- **Containment.** Enclosing process equipment so that dust cannot escape, designing internal surfaces that do not accumulate dust, and locating bulk storage enclosures outside buildings where an explosion hazard exists.
- **Isolation.** Preventing flame and pressure propagation between connected equipment — rotary valves, fast-acting valves, chemical isolation.
- **Venting and suppression of deflagration.** Sizing vents to the deflagration severity of the specific dust, so that a deflagration is released in a controlled direction rather than failing the building.
- **Explosion protection of the collection system.** Venting or suppression on the dust collector itself, spark detection and extinguishing upstream in the ductwork, and appropriately located collectors.
- **Housekeeping and cleaning.** Vacuum cleaning rather than dry sweeping, cleaning schedules that account for accumulation points, and inspection programmes. NFPA 652 treats compressed-air blowdown as acceptable only after the primary methods have been performed and additional precautions are in place — which is a considerably narrower permission than general practice assumes.

Against that stack, a fog cannon does something specific and worth stating plainly: **it reduces the mass of combustible dust that can become airborne in the first place**. Lower airborne concentration means the working volume stays further below the minimum explosible concentration. NFPA guidance commonly uses 25% of the minimum explosible concentration as the operating ceiling for dust concentrations, and that figure is the single clearest statement of what suppression buys: it is the engineering control that keeps normal operation away from the lower explosive limit, rather than a mitigation applied after the limit has been crossed.

## What Actually Makes a Machine Explosion-Proof

The marking is the label; the engineering is underneath it. On a fog cannon, the parts that determine whether the machine can be installed in a classified location are specific, and they are worth reviewing at the specification stage rather than after delivery.

**The motor and drive train.** The fan motor is the largest single electrical item on the machine and the most likely point of ignition. It carries the protection type appropriate to the location — flameproof enclosure for gas applications, enclosure protection for dust — and it is a specified, certified component rather than a generic industrial motor.

**The electrics and the control enclosure.** The control box, terminal boxes, cable entries and any sensors or switches attached to the machine must all be appropriately protected, and the cable entries must use the certified sealing arrangement. A flameproof motor fitted to a non-protected control box does not produce an explosion-proof machine.

**The water side.** Pumps, seals and bearings are mechanical items, but they are part of the machine's failure behaviour. A seal failure sprays water, which is a hazard of a different kind in a classified area, and bearings require lubrication regimes suitable for the environment.

**Surface temperature.** The temperature class in the marking exists because some machines run hot. What matters is that no component can carry the machine's surface above the relevant limit in its worst credible case — which is why the marking states a temperature and why it must be compared against the dust's ignition temperature for the specific material.

**Bonding and earthing.** For conductive dusts, and as general good practice everywhere, the machine body must be bonded and earthed as part of the installation. This is an installation obligation as much as a design feature, and it belongs in the commissioning checklist rather than in the delivery note.

**The controls and interlocks.** Where the machine runs automatically, the control logic has to be considered as part of the safety case: interlocks on water supply and pressure, fault conditions that stop the machine rather than leave it running dry, and remote operation that allows the machine to be started from outside the area.

On services and lifetime, stated separately from warranty and after-sales support: **with disciplined operation, the complete machine remains usable for more than 10 years**, subject to consumables replaced on schedule, water quality within the agreed range, and winter anti-freezing procedures followed.

## Specification Language That Avoids Trouble

Most disputes about explosion-proof equipment are not technical. They are specification disputes — the buyer's requirement and the supplier's scope were never written in the same terms. A few practices avoid most of them.

**Specify by application, not by label.** Write the requirement as the location, the material and the required protection level, and let the supplier state the protection type and the certificate that satisfies it. This is also the correct commercial relationship: **ex-protection configurations can be provided to suit the customer's application, with the specific protection type and certificate supplied with the project.** The marking is then something the supplier is accountable for, rather than something the buyer guessed at.

**Keep two certificate families separate.** The Ex marking and the mining safety approval (MA, in China) cover different applications and are obtained and verified separately. Where a site needs one, the other, or both, that should be stated as a separate line in the specification rather than folded into a general phrase about certification.

**Ask for the material data, not the machine data.** The supplier can specify the machine; only a test report on the plant's actual dust can specify the hazard. The minimum explosible concentration, minimum ignition energy, minimum ignition temperature and explosion severity figures come from the customer's own material testing, and any credible proposal will ask for them.

**State the boundary of the equipment's scope.** A suppression machine reduces airborne dust in normal operation. It is not a dust hazard analysis, it is not vent sizing, and it is not a substitute for a classified-area electrical installation. Writing that boundary into the specification prevents the machine from being credited with protection it does not provide.

**Confirm what is certified and what is not.** A machine may be supplied as an assembly with protected components, and the assembly's own certification scope will differ from the individual components' approvals. Ask which parts carry which approvals, and by whom they were verified.

## A Sequence for Specifying an Explosion-Proof Fog Cannon

In order, and each step removes options from the shortlist:

1. **Identify the material and its physical form.** Bulk solid producing dust, or a material producing vapour. If the dust is combustible, obtain the test data — minimum explosible concentration, minimum ignition energy, minimum ignition temperature, explosion severity.
2. **Determine whether water is compatible with the material.** For a small number of materials, including several metal dusts, this changes the choice of suppression medium rather than the configuration of the machine.
3. **Establish the characteristics of each location where the machine will run**, and the classification that applies to it under the standard in force in that jurisdiction.
4. **Derive the required protection level, dust or gas group and temperature limit from the location and the material** — and write them into the specification as requirements, not as a desired marking.
5. **Confirm which certificate families apply** in that jurisdiction, and whether mining approval is required in addition to Ex certification.
6. **Decide the installation and control scope**: bonding and earthing, remote operation, interlocks on water supply and pressure, fault behaviour, and whether the machine runs on a schedule, on a dust-concentration interlock, or on both.
7. **Size the suppression to the emission**, using the established open-area logic — droplets in the working band, total volume above the dust generation rate, coverage of the whole emitting zone — rather than by throw distance alone. In a classified location, the machine still has to do the suppression job it was bought for.
8. **Then select the machine**, and confirm the marking, the certified components and the documents supplied with it.

![Eight specification steps, from material identification to machine selection](/images/explosion-proof-fog-cannon-specification-sequence.svg)

Six of those eight steps require information the supplier does not have: the material, the location, the classification, and the local certification requirement. That is the practical reason why explosion-proof specification takes longer than ordinary equipment selection, and why a supplier who asks for that information early is usually more useful than one who quotes immediately.
