Cement is the third-largest industrial source of air pollutant emissions in China, behind power generation and steel. That ranking is the reason the *Opinions on Promoting Ultra-Low Emission in the Cement Industry*, issued on 19 January 2024 by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment together with the National Development and Reform Commission, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, the Ministry of Finance and the Ministry of Transport, arrived with a calendar attached rather than an aspiration.

The timetable is a ramp: by the end of 2025, key air-quality regions were to have made obvious progress, with 50 percent of clinker capacity retrofitted and large state-owned groups in those regions essentially complete on both organised and fugitive sources; by the end of 2028, clinker producers in key regions should be substantially finished and the country as a whole is aiming for 80 percent of clinker capacity. The concentration limits are the familiar ones — at the kiln and the waste-heat recovery system, and at a reference oxygen content of 10 percent, hourly averages for particulate matter, sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides are capped at 10, 35 and 50 mg/m3.

None of that is the hard part. By 2024 the cement industry had already spent two decades on kiln-line dedusting; bag filters and electrostatic precipitators at the stack are mature, well-vendored and continuously monitored. The hard part is the sentence in the same document that has no number in it at all: dust-generating points and production facilities must show **no visible dust or fume escaping**, and the plant must be **tidy, with no accumulated dust**. That is a visual criterion. It is assessed by looking, and increasingly by cameras.

## Nine Dust Scenarios, and What Each One Takes

A cement works looks like dozens of separate dust problems. Grouped by the place and by the operations that happen there, it is nine scenarios — and each one maps to a single answer. Grouping them this way is what makes the equipment decision tractable: the question stops being "how many machines do we need" and becomes "which of these nine needs what".

![Cement plant dust sources grouped into nine scenarios, each mapped to a fog cannon, a dry fog system, or enclosure and bag filtration](/images/cement-plant-scenario-map.svg)

| Scenario | What it includes | What it takes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quarry | Drilling, blasting, loading, haul roads | Fog cannon |
| Crushing station | Tipping, crushing, screening, belt transfer | Fog cannon on the open tipping apron; dry fog inside the hoods, screens and transfer points |
| Raw material and additive yards | Stacking, reclaiming, discharge, truck traffic, yard roads | Fog cannon |
| Raw meal preparation | Mill feed and discharge, separator, silo top, filter discharge | Dry fog |
| Kiln line and cooler | Preheater, rotary kiln, clinker cooling, kiln baghouse | An organised source, handled by bag filtration and similar means |
| Clinker storage and handling | Silo discharge, stockpiles, transfer | Fog cannon at the silo discharge and stockpiles; dry fog at enclosed transfer points |
| Cement milling and dispatch | Cement mill, cement silos, packers, loading heads | Not a spray job — enclosure, negative pressure and bag filtration |
| Coal preparation | Coal yard, coal mill, coal silo, coal transport | Fog cannon at the coal yard; protection matched to the application on the coal system |
| Plant and haul roads | Re-entrained dust under repeated vehicle traffic | Fog cannon plus mechanical sweeping |

The distribution is worth reading carefully. **Five scenarios take a fog cannon** — the quarry, the crushing station's open apron, the raw material and additive yards, clinker storage and handling, and the road network. What they have in common is that they are open or semi-open, the material is coarse, and the dust is driven by wind and vehicles rather than sitting in a duct.

**Four scenarios take dry fog** — the enclosed parts of crushing and screening, belt transfer points and bucket elevators, the raw mill system and raw meal silo tops, and enclosed batching and weighing. What they have in common is a confined space and dust that is extremely fine and sticky.

**Three scenarios take neither, and saying so is part of the answer.** The kiln line is an organised source with a stack, and it is already handled by bag filtration. Everything downstream of the cement mill is handled by enclosure and extraction — for a reason explained in the next section. Coal preparation is a protection problem rather than a spray problem.

Before specifying anything, it is worth noting the ordering that applies inside the crushing station. The chute, the receiving pit and the hood come first; the cannon finishes the job at the open apron rather than carrying it. A cannon aimed at a crusher that has not been enclosed is being asked to compensate for missing steelwork, and it will not succeed.

## Cement Sets When You Add Water — Where Water Is Off Limits

This is the property that separates a cement plant from a steel stockyard or a coal terminal, and it is worth stating precisely, because it defines the boundary of everything a spray system can do here.

Ordinary Portland cement is a hydraulic binder: it begins hydrating on contact with water and will set in air or under water. The finished product is therefore specified with a tight moisture ceiling — the cement leaving the mill is typically required to hold below roughly 0.5 percent. Anything above that shows up as caking on silo walls, blocked discharge chutes, poor flow in air slides, slipping belts, and cement that underperforms its declared strength class. The industry language for it is pre-hydration, and it is a quality problem, not a dust problem.

The same logic has already been applied inside the mill itself. Water injection into the mill, or spraying the shell to bring down cement temperature, is an emergency measure — it lowers temperature and raises moisture at the same time, so the accepted process position today is to manage temperature through mill ventilation, grinding media and avoiding over-grinding instead.

The practical consequence is short and absolute: **downstream of the cement mill, a fog cannon is not the answer, it is a liability.** The cement mill, separators, cement silos, dispatch chutes, packers and loading heads are handled by enclosure, inward negative pressure and local extraction. That is not a gap in the equipment range — it is how that section of a cement plant has always been controlled, and it is why those three lines in the table above are stated as limits rather than applications. Where a very small amount of water is used at an immediate loading point, it is a production parameter agreed with the mill and quality teams and interlocked with the loading cycle, not an environmental decision.

For coal there is a second, separate boundary. A suspended coal-dust cloud is itself the hazard, and fine coal in a plant where the housekeeping reflex is to blow accumulated dust off beams with compressed air is a plant that manufactures the cloud the hazard needs. Coal handling needs wet or vacuum housekeeping, control of accumulated layers, monitoring for carbon monoxide and temperature rise in stored coal, and electrical equipment whose protection matches the application: Ex configurations can be provided to suit the customer's application, with the specific protection type and certificate supplied with the project. Explosion protection and mining approvals are separate regimes and should be verified separately.

## Two Mechanisms, Two Jobs

Fog cannons and dry fog systems are often discussed as though one were a more powerful version of the other. They are not. They atomise differently, their droplets travel differently, and they act on dust by different physical routes. That is why they serve opposite ends of the same process chain rather than competing for the same job.

| | Long-range fog cannon | Dry fog system |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Atomisation | Water pressurised through nozzles, then accelerated by an axial fan | Two-fluid: compressed air and low-pressure water atomised together inside the nozzle |
| Droplet size | Tens to a few hundred microns, selected to suit the duty | Roughly 1 to 10 microns |
| Capture mechanism | Inertial collision and gravitational settling of the coarser fraction | Brownian diffusion and agglomeration: fine droplets evaporate in the air stream and cause fine particles to combine and settle |
| Effective distance | Tens of metres up to a few hundred metres | A few metres — the nozzle is placed at the source |
| Moisture added | Substantial, and it has to be budgeted | Fractions of a percent |
| Space it needs | Open: a yard, a road, a stockpile face | A confined or at least enclosed space |
| What it is good at | Bringing down a visible plume across an area, and doing it from a tower so nobody stands in the dust | Locking down respirable dust at a single generating point without wetting the belt or the product |

The practical rule that falls out of the table: **dry fog works on points, a fog cannon works on areas.** A transfer point, an elevator discharge, a silo top or a mill feeding point is a point — it has a defined opening, it is usually already hooded or enclosed, and the dust is fine enough to stay airborne for a long time. A stockpile face, a haul road or a crusher apron is an area — the plume is low and wind-driven, the coarser fraction settles out quickly, and the water can be allowed to land.

The complementary case is worth spelling out, because it explains why a cement plant usually needs both. At a belt transfer point, a dry fog system holds the fine fraction inside the chute and stops the dust becoming airborne at all. But if the material arrives at the head end already hot and heavily dust-laden, or the discharge is not enclosed, the residual plume has to be knocked down in the open — and that is a cannon's job, at a distance, with water that will be tolerated because the material is still upstream of the mill. Two devices, two principles, one process chain.

The water budget closes this section. Every litre added to a stockpile, a belt or a truck is a litre that the next stage of the process has to tolerate. Agreeing the acceptable moisture increase with production and quality **before** flow rates are chosen is what prevents a site from ending up with a system that nobody dares switch on.

## Same Spec Sheet, Different Result

Two fog cannons can both be sold as 120-metre units with a rated droplet size, and behave completely differently in the same quarry. The difference is rarely a single superior component. It is whether the components were designed as one system:

| Element | What the design decision actually is |
| --- | --- |
| Water pump | The pressure–flow curve has to match the total flow of the spray ring, with acceptable loss across the manifold |
| Spray ring and manifold | Nozzle count, spacing, angle and number of rings, coupled to the flow field at the fan outlet |
| Nozzles | Spray angle, flow consistency between units, and a wear- and clog-resistant construction that can be serviced individually |
| Fan | The trade-off between air volume, static pressure and throw, matched to the droplet spectrum |
| Control system | Manual, remote or programmed operation, and whether it can run per zone and interlock with what the plant is doing |
| Structure and protection | Outdoor steelwork, corrosion protection, bearing and motor protection, winter drainage, and whether a technician can reach what fails |

Get the chain wrong and it shows immediately. Pressure falls short, droplets coarsen, and reach and atomisation degrade together. The spray field turns hollow or lopsided, so part of the target is over-sprayed and part is bare, with fan energy wasted on both. One deviating nozzle distorts the whole pattern. The root cause is the configuration, and it will not be solved by replacing a single part. **A configuration that is reasonable and stable matters more than how good any one component is.**

Beyond the components, eight things decide how the machine behaves in year one and in year six — and none of them appear on a data sheet.

![Eight things a fog cannon data sheet does not show: droplet size, noise and vibration, throw distance, energy, automation, durability, design stability, and what a parameter promise is worth](/images/fog-cannon-design-blind-spots.svg)

**Droplet size cannot be measured on site.** There is no way to verify it in a working plant, and it drifts: nozzle wear, scaling, clogging filters and pressure fluctuation all coarsen the spray over months, while the first few weeks look perfectly fine. What matters is the design provision — anti-clog construction, water quality, nozzles that can be changed one at a time, and margin in the pump curve.

**Noise and vibration only show up later.** On a test run the difference is hard to hear. Impeller balance, foundation stiffness and drive layout accumulate over continuous duty, and a noisy installation is usually an energy-hungry one.

**Throw distance is a design figure.** It is quoted in still air, without obstruction, at one particular droplet size. There is no mandatory test standard for reach; what exists is an industry technical standard, and asking under what conditions the figure was measured is more useful than comparing the figure itself. In a working yard, side wind shortens effective reach and pushes the plume off target, so mounting height, overlap between adjacent units and wind-aware presets matter more than the headline number.

**More motor power is not a higher specification.** Reach comes from air volume, static pressure and droplet spectrum together, not in proportion to power. At the same reach, extra power does not become extra throw — it becomes electricity and noise, every year. Sizing the fan to the reach and coverage actually needed, with modest margin, is what keeps the running cost where it should be.

**"Automatic" means different things.** A manual button, a remote control, a program on a timer or a zone, or interlocking with what the plant is actually doing and adjusting on the result. Ask what it does: per-zone start and stop, interlock with belt running and truck presence, and a record of runs and alarms.

**Durability is not about the warranty.** What a plant wants is a machine that never needs repairing, and much of that comes down to whether maintenance can actually be carried out — service access, consumables that change easily, a documented schedule.

**Design stability is the precondition for a long life.** A settled model, a configuration that does not change every year, and parts available for a decade are what let a plant build a maintenance routine. A constantly revised design makes that impossible.

**"Compliant" is not "achieved".** Many parameters cannot be verified on site, and they will not all be checked item by item at delivery. Some promises made at tender stage are simply not met in service — which is why the supplier behind the machine matters more than the comparison table.

And the cost of instability is not theoretical. Every line on the sheet can carry the word "compliant", and the difference appears after a season on site. An unreliable unit means repeated repairs, in an area that is already dusty with poor visibility — a safety question before it is anything else — plus parts to replace, and in the worst case a second purchase. The comparison worth making is not between three columns of numbers, but between how two machines behave after three years and after five.

## The Details That Decide Whether It Works on Site

**Wear, corrosion and heat.** A cement plant is a hostile place for outdoor machinery: abrasive limestone and clinker dust, alkaline chemistry, ambient heat near the cooler and the kiln, and fine dust that settles into every crevice. Protection grades, corrosion protection, nozzle materials and lens-cleaning provision on any camera should be chosen to suit the atmosphere the equipment actually lives in, and the spare-part list should fit the plant's own maintenance rhythm.

**Freeze protection.** Northern plants lose suppression in the first cold snap, and a system that is bypassed in January has an annual availability problem, not a winter problem. Drainage points, heat tracing on exposed pipework, and a defensible winter sequence are best designed in from the start rather than retrofitted.

**Linkage.** A truck tipping at the crusher or a loader working a stockpile produces a plume that peaks within seconds. Linking suppression to what the plant is physically doing — a truck present, a belt running, a loading cycle in progress — is what turns a reactive system into a predictive one, and it reduces water use at the same time.

**Zoning.** Where a target area is large, splitting it into zones served by more than one unit is what makes the coverage real rather than nominal, and it means ordinary maintenance does not leave a hole in it.

## Run It as a Loop, Not a Timer

The conventional installation is a timer and a contactor: the yard sprays for five minutes every half hour regardless of what is happening. It is cheap, it is defensible on paper, and it fails in three directions at once. Water and power are spent on an empty yard. In summer the material gets too wet, so operators shorten the interval; in winter it freezes, so they switch it off. And when an inspector asks what the plant did during last Tuesday's shift, there is no record of anything.

![Closed-loop dust control for a cement plant: monitor, identify, grade, trigger, verify and record, with the two tuning rules specific to cement and what the record should show](/images/cement-dust-control-loop.svg)

A closed-loop system replaces the clock with an event. Cameras and particulate monitors watch the sources; the control layer decides whether dust is being generated, where, and how severely; the suppression equipment in that specific zone runs a preset programme; and the result is verified against the same sensors that raised the alarm. Monitor, identify, grade, trigger, verify, record.

The grading step is what makes it tolerable to live with. A minor event gets a short local burst. A sustained event runs the full preset for the zone. A severe one escalates to the control room with the image attached. Verification is what stops a stuck valve from running a pump all shift, and it is also what produces the evidence trail.

Two cement-specific tuning rules are worth flagging. First, vision models must distinguish dust from **steam and water vapour** — in a plant with a cooler, a waste-heat boiler and wet material, false alarms from white plumes will otherwise erode operator confidence in the system. Second, thresholds must be set per zone, because an alarm level that is appropriate at a raw mill discharge is meaningless at a bagging line. A commissioning period on site is not a sign that the system is unfinished; it is how the thresholds get set to the plant.

## How to Compare Two Quotations Without Turning It Into a Tender

Sourcing in this industry is still largely decided on three columns: throw, flow, price. The first two are usually quoted under conditions that do not exist in a working plant, and the third says nothing about what the equipment will cost to own. None of that is a reason to write a specification that only a handful of suppliers can answer.

A comparison that is both fair and useful asks three questions, in this order.

**Is the machine designed as a system?** Ask how the pump, spray ring, nozzles, fan and control were matched to each other, and what the design was optimised for — reach, coverage, water consumption, or winter availability. A supplier who can explain the trade-offs they chose has usually thought about the duty. A supplier who can only quote numbers has usually not.

**What has it actually done in this industry?** Not a photograph gallery, but how long the supplier has been building for cement, mining and bulk material handling, and what those sites look like after a few years of dust, abrasion and continuous duty. Experience shows up in the details: how a manifold is drained, whether a nozzle can be changed without dismantling the ring, whether a cabinet is positioned where it floods.

**What happens after commissioning?** Spare-part availability, response arrangements and whether the winter procedure is documented are worth understanding before the order, because they decide the annual availability number. This is a conversation rather than a scoring matrix, and it is usually more informative than the answers.

## Outlook

The direction of travel is not in doubt. Cement's organised emissions are largely a solved engineering problem with mature vendors and continuous monitoring. What remains, and what the assessment will actually turn on, is the distributed, intermittent, unmeasured half of the problem: the yards, the crusher apron, the transfer galleries, the clinker handling, the haul roads.

Those sources will not be managed by installing equipment and walking away, and they will not be covered by a single machine applied across the site. They will be managed as an operating data loop — event recognition instead of schedules, per-zone accountability instead of site-wide averages, and evidence generated as a by-product of running the plant rather than assembled the week before an audit.

That is also why it is worth knowing, at the start of a project, exactly which scenarios a spray system can address and which it cannot. A plant that has had that conversation will specify better, and will not find out in year three that the equipment was aimed at a job it was never able to do.
