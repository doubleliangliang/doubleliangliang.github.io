An open coal yard has an obvious problem: the dust leaves. It drifts over the fence line, settles on neighbouring roofs, and shows up in boundary monitoring. So operators enclose it — a steel shed, a circular dome, a battery of concrete silos, or more recently an air-supported structure that covers the whole stockyard in a single membrane. Boundary dust falls. The visible plume disappears. The compliance file looks clean.

Then the people who work inside start complaining, and the gas and dust readings inside the building tell a different story.

This article is about the second half of the problem — the part that an enclosure does not solve, and can in fact make harder. It covers what changes when a coal yard gets a roof, why ventilation alone is not the answer, and what a control strategy inside an enclosed coal store actually has to do.

## The Half of the Problem an Enclosure Solves

It is worth being precise about what enclosure achieves, because the achievement is real and it is not the whole job.

In an open yard, dust is generated at four broad groups of points: the working face of the stockpile where material is dropped, the transfer points along the conveyor route, the surface of the piles themselves under wind, and the haul roads where vehicles move. Once airborne, the fine fraction — particles below roughly 10 micrometres — can stay suspended for hours and travel with the wind. That is the fraction that reaches the boundary, appears in TSP and PM10 monitoring, and generates complaints.

A roof, a dome or a silo removes the wind-driven component almost entirely and blocks the escape path. Boundary concentrations drop sharply, and the plant satisfies the visual criterion that most permits are written around: no visible dust leaving the installation. This is a genuine and measurable improvement, and it is why enclosure programmes have spread across coal-fired power stations, ports and coal preparation plants worldwide.

What the enclosure does not do is reduce the rate at which dust is *generated* at the transfer points and the working face. It does not remove the fine fraction from the air. It simply removes the wind, changes where the air can go, and puts a lid on the volume that has to absorb everything the material handling system produces all day.

Put differently: the enclosure converts an emission problem into an occupational and process-safety problem. Both matter. Only one of them shows up in the boundary monitor.

## Five Ways to Enclose a Coal Yard — and What Each One Changes

Not all enclosures behave the same way. The choice of structure determines how much air volume there is, how easily it can be ventilated, how much material sits per unit of floor area, and how hard it is to remove dust and heat.

![Comparison of five coal yard enclosure types — open stockpile, steel shed, circular dome, silo battery and air-supported structure — showing how each one changes internal ventilation, dust and heat behaviour](/images/enclosed-coal-storage-types.svg)

Two observations follow from that comparison and are easy to get wrong.

The first is that the tighter the structure, the better it contains dust — and the worse it is at letting anything out. A well-sealed structure is excellent at keeping emissions inside the fence and equally good at keeping heat, moisture and gas inside the building.

The second is that a silo is not simply "a small shed". Storing coal in a tall, nearly sealed column gives it a long residence time with a high exposed surface area per tonne. Industry guidance on coal storage is direct about this: long-term storage in enclosed silos and bunkers allows enough air movement to create ideal conditions for accelerated self-heating, which is why silo systems are commonly supplied with carbon monoxide sensors, active airflow prevention and inerting such as nitrogen purging. A silo is a different problem set, not a smaller version of the same one.

## The Dust Did Not Leave. It Accumulated.

Here is the mechanism that catches people out.

Dust generation inside an enclosed coal store continues at essentially the same rate as in the open, because the sources are the same. Material still falls from the stacker or the tripper; conveyors still discharge at transfer towers; front-end loaders and stacker-reclaimers still drive over the pile; vehicles still move through the gates. What has changed is that there is no wind to dilute and no open boundary to carry the fine fraction away.

In the open, the concentration at any point is a balance between generation and dispersion. In an enclosed volume, dispersion is weak and the equilibrium sits at a much higher concentration. Add settle-out and you get the second effect: fine coal deposits on every horizontal surface — ledges, beams, conveyor galleries, the tops of ducts and cable trays, the structure of the roof itself — and is then re-suspended by vehicle movement, vibrating machinery, or a single person sweeping with a broom.

Two reference points are worth carrying in your head. The first is that settled dust is the inventory, not just an untidy floor. A layer of about 0.8 mm — roughly the thickness of a paperclip — covering five per cent of a floor area is the level that guidance treats as a combustible dust hazard, and deposits on overhead steelwork can add the equivalent of a further ten per cent of floor area. The second is that coal dust stays combustible down to particle sizes well below what most people picture; explosive behaviour is most pronounced in the 75 to 150 micrometre band, and material finer than 420 micrometres is the working definition of what matters.

The practical consequence: "there is no dust leaving the building" and "there is no dust in the building" are not the same statement, and the second one is the one the people inside care about.

## Ventilation Is Not the Same Thing as Dust Control

The instinctive response is to ventilate. It is the right instinct and it is not a complete answer, for three reasons.

First, ventilation and containment pull in opposite directions. Every opening that lets air in is also an opening that lets dust out. The design target is not "move a lot of air", it is "let air in without letting dust out" — a much harder problem, and the reason why the conventional arrangement of low-level louvres plus roof vents is so sensitive to wind direction. When the wind runs parallel to a louvred wall rather than across it, the intake collapses and internal conditions deteriorate. Powered extractors and ventilation systems exist precisely because natural arrangements are unreliable, but they reintroduce the problem the enclosure was built to solve unless the extracted air is cleaned before it is released.

Second, ventilation controls concentration, not generation. If dust is generated continuously at the transfer points and the working face, a ventilation system running at a given air change rate will hold the internal concentration at some equilibrium value. Move that air change rate up and the equilibrium falls; but you are still maintaining a steady concentration above whatever the sources produce, and you are paying to move very large volumes of air. Ventilation also does nothing about settle-out: it can move airborne dust, but it will not clean the ledges.

Third, ventilation is not free of operational consequences. Air movement across a coal surface removes moisture, and dry coal generates more dust. The recommended handling range for bituminous coal sits around 8 to 12 per cent moisture — enough to suppress dust formation without creating handling problems or increasing the risk of spontaneous combustion — and aggressive air exchange works directly against holding that band.

The regulatory framing in Europe reflects this. The BAT conclusions for iron and steel production do not simply tell operators to ventilate; they require a diffuse dust action plan inside the environmental management system, built on measurement — PM10 monitoring combined with wind direction and speed to locate the dominant sources — and then a set of controls applied at the source. Ventilation is one tool in that plan. It is not the plan.

## Inside a Sealed Building, the Air Has Nowhere to Go — the Health Side

This is where the enclosure changes the nature of the exposure rather than merely its location.

Respirable dust — the fraction that penetrates to the gas-exchange region of the lung — does not care that it is inside a building. In a coal yard, the material handling system produces fine coal dust and, crucially, the mineral matter that travels with it. Respirable crystalline silica is the component that regulators have moved hardest against, because it is far more damaging per unit mass than coal dust alone.

In the United States, the Mine Safety and Health Administration issued its final rule on respirable crystalline silica in April 2024, taking effect in June 2024, establishing a permissible exposure limit of 50 micrograms per cubic metre over an eight-hour shift and an action level of 25 micrograms per cubic metre for all mines, coal and metal/non-metal alike, with coal operators required to comply by April 2025. The rule moves MSHA onto the same numerical footing OSHA established for general industry in 2016. It also places the responsibility on engineering controls — ventilation, enclosure, water sprays, dust suppression — and explicitly does not accept rotating workers in and out of high-exposure tasks as a compliance strategy.

The reason for the rule is the trend in disease. After decades of decline, black lung disease in the United States has risen again: the prevalence of the advanced form among long-tenured miners in central Appalachia went from under one per cent in the 1990s to about five per cent by 2015, and the most severe form, progressive massive fibrosis, has been documented in miners in their thirties and forties. Silica is understood to be a principal driver. Black lung has no cure; for advanced cases a lung transplant is sometimes the only option, and even then survival beyond five years is not typical.

Set that against the configuration of an enclosed coal store. In an open yard, a worker at a transfer point has unlimited fresh air around them and the wind is usually moving. Inside a sealed or nearly sealed building, the same worker is downstream of a dust source in a volume with limited exchange, often at a low level where the coarse fraction settles and where fresh air is hardest to deliver. Chinese occupational hygiene standards give a useful sense of the target: total dust at the workplace is commonly held to 4 mg/m³ and respirable dust to 2.5 mg/m³. Those are not numbers that a sealed building with continuous material handling reaches by accident.

An enclosure that is not paired with dust control at the source has, in effect, concentrated the exposure into a smaller volume and handed it to the people who work in it.

## The Explosion Side: When Confinement Becomes the Fifth Condition

The health argument is the first one to make, because it is the one that costs lives slowly and invisibly. The explosion argument is the second, and it is the one that changes the building.

Consider the conditions a dust explosion requires. Most people list three: fuel, oxygen and an ignition source. Combustible dust adds two more. The dust must be *dispersed* — suspended in air at a concentration within its explosive range — and the event must be *confined*, so that pressure can build instead of dissipating. Together these five form what is often called the dust pentagon.

Now look at what an enclosed coal store contributes. It provides a continuous supply of fuel in the form of fine coal dust, both suspended and settled. It provides confinement by definition. And it provides dispersion constantly: material dropping at a transfer point, a stacker-reclaimer moving through a pile, a vehicle crossing a floor, or — most efficiently of all — someone cleaning with compressed air. Of the five conditions, two are inherent to the building and one is created by ordinary operations. Only oxygen and ignition are things you might imagine eliminating, and neither is realistically removable.

The consequence is the well-documented two-stage pattern. A primary deflagration, usually small and often inside a piece of equipment — a conveyor enclosure, a chute, a dust collector — is survivable in itself. Its pressure wave then lifts settled dust from every horizontal surface in the building into a cloud. That secondary cloud, far larger than the original and spread through the structure, is what destroys the building and kills people. The 2008 sugar refinery explosion in Georgia followed exactly this path: a primary event confined in an enclosed steel belt conveyor, and secondary deflagrations that killed fourteen workers. The U.S. Chemical Safety Board's review of combustible dust incidents from 1980 to 2005 recorded 281 events, excluding grain, with 119 fatalities and 718 injuries.

Coal is a thoroughly documented member of this family. A coal dust explosion releases energy fast enough to reach instantaneous temperatures of the order of 2300 to 2500 °C. Carbonaceous dusts including coal fall in Class II, Group F for hazardous-location purposes, and coal dust is commonly classified St-1 in terms of explosion severity — a reminder that a low Kst value reduces the engineering difficulty of protection, not the need for it.

The standards landscape has recently simplified and tightened. Since December 2024, NFPA 660 has consolidated six previously separate commodity standards — 61, 484, 652, 654, 655 and 664 — into a single document covering combustible dusts and particulate solids, with a transition period running to mid-2026. The underlying requirement is unchanged and is the one that matters most: a documented Dust Hazard Analysis, performed by a qualified person, following the material through receiving, processing and storage, identifying credible ignition sources and loss scenarios, and tracking recommendations to closure. Bulk storage is explicitly in scope: bins, silos and hoppers present dust clouds during filling, smouldering material, static discharge and explosion propagation; conveyors and bucket elevators combine internal dust clouds with friction, belt slip and bearing failure; dust collectors routinely contain explosible atmospheres and can link several process areas through their ductwork.

Two things should be said plainly, because a dust suppression article that skips them is not being honest.

The first is that suppression does not replace explosion protection. Reducing airborne dust and keeping surfaces clean lowers the probability of the dispersion and fuel conditions being met simultaneously, and it is genuinely valuable. It does not remove the need for venting, isolation and suppression to be designed by someone competent, or for the hazard analysis to be done. NFPA 68 and NFPA 69 remain the reference documents for deflagration venting and for explosion prevention, suppression and isolation respectively.

The second is that inside a coal handling environment, electrical equipment and instrumentation have to be selected for the application. The right way to express this is that Ex configurations can be provided to suit the customer's application; the specific protection type and certificate are supplied with the project. What that means in practice is that the enclosure design, the dust control system and the electrical classification are decided together, not in sequence by three different suppliers.

## The Gas Side: Methane, Carbon Monoxide and Self-Heating

There is a third internal problem that has nothing directly to do with dust generation and everything to do with the fact that coal is a chemically active material in a sealed box.

Coal oxidises at ambient temperature. The reaction is slow but exothermic, and it produces carbon monoxide as a marker. In an open pile, most of that heat escapes to the atmosphere. In an enclosed store, and especially in a silo or a bunker where the material sits for weeks or months, the heat accumulates. Internal temperature rises, the oxidation rate rises with it, and at some point the pile reaches a temperature at which self-heating turns into combustion. This is not a marginal effect: the industry treats spontaneous combustion as one of the primary technical challenges of coal storage, and notes that even without an open fire, heat-affected coal may become unsuitable for its intended use — which makes prevention and early detection both a safety and a commercial requirement.

Alongside heat, the coal releases gas. Methane is the significant one, both because it is flammable and because it is lighter than air, so it migrates to the highest point of the structure and collects there. In a dome or an air-supported structure, the highest point is precisely where fine coal dust also settles and where inspection is hardest. Carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulphide and reduced oxygen are the other indicators that matter.

The geometry of an enclosed store makes this worse in a specific and predictable way. Stacker-reclaimers reach most of a pile but not all of it. Material that cannot be reclaimed stays in place indefinitely. Every enclosed coal yard therefore accumulates dead zones — behind retaining walls, in the corners a machine cannot reach, at the toe of a pile — and material that remains undisturbed for a long period is exactly the material that self-heats. Operators working in this area describe it as the standard reservation about enclosed storage, and it explains why the monitoring package on a modern silo or dome is closer to a process safety system than to a dust control system: distributed fibre-optic temperature sensing along the walls or into the pile, multiple carbon monoxide points (CO being the earliest practical indicator, well ahead of visible smoke), methane and oxygen sensors at high level, and flame or smoke detection, all feeding one platform that escalates from a temperature-only trend to a combined temperature-plus-CO alarm and then to action.

Detection is worth building around for one more reason. Where coal heating is under way, the correct response is to remove and cool the hot material, and that response has to start early, because stockpile fires are difficult to extinguish once established. Early warning is not a luxury; it is the difference between reclaiming the material and writing it off.

## Visibility, Machinery, and the Bill Nobody Prints

Two further consequences of internal dust are less dramatic and more expensive, and they are the ones that show up in the maintenance budget.

The first is visibility. Suspended dust reduces contrast, compresses depth perception and hides the edges of openings and the outline of moving equipment. Inside a coal shed this matters more than outside, because the space contains large machines operating near structures and near people. Deterioration is gradual, so the people working in it adapt to reduced visibility and do not report it — the condition becomes the new normal. The regulatory handle for this in many jurisdictions is the opacity limit, commonly 20 per cent averaged over six minutes as a permit condition in the United States, judged by eye using EPA Method 9. It is worth noting what those rules exempt: uncombined water, meaning steam and water droplets. That exemption is the single most important design constraint for automatic monitoring, because a system that cannot distinguish dust from water vapour will generate false alarms and will be switched off by the people who have to live with it.

The second is equipment. Coal dust is abrasive and, when mixed with lubricant, forms a grinding paste. It settles into grease points, coats heat exchangers and radiators, and finds its way into electrical enclosures and control cabinets. Conductive dust bridging terminals is a recognised failure mode, and sensors contaminated with dust have a particular characteristic: they keep operating and they keep reporting, but what they report is wrong. The cumulative effect is an increase in maintenance frequency, an increase in unplanned stoppages, and a slow drift in the reliability of everything in the building. None of that appears on a dust control quotation, and all of it appears in the annual budget.

There is also the interaction with water that nobody likes to discuss. Sprinkling is the oldest dust control method in existence and it works. It also adds moisture to the coal, and moisture is not neutral: it reduces calorific value, it affects combustion, it promotes caking in filters, it makes belts slip, and it can freeze. The 8 to 12 per cent handling band exists for a reason. Any suppression strategy inside an enclosure has to respect it, which immediately rules out the simplest approach of spraying more water more often.

## The Fix Is a Layered System, Not a Bigger Sprayer

The way to hold all of this together is to stop treating the enclosure as the control measure and start treating it as one layer in a stack. Inside an enclosed coal store, the layers look like this.

**At the source.** Reduce the energy that puts dust into the air. Lower drop heights at the working face and at transfer points, optimise the angle of chutes so that material slides rather than falls, fit damping plates inside loading chutes to dissipate the positive pressure the falling stream creates, and seal conveyor covers and skirt boards so that the dust-laden air has nowhere to escape. Source control is the cheapest dust control there is, because it prevents generation rather than managing the consequences.

**At the transfer point.** Where material falls inside an enclosure — at a chute, a crusher outlet, a tripper, a loading spout — fine water mist, or dry fog, applied inside the enclosed space captures the fine fraction where it is generated. The critical precondition is an enclosure around the point of application: fine fog is a local control, and used in the open it simply evaporates. This is also where the enclosure itself becomes an asset rather than a liability, because it provides the containment that the technique needs.

**In the volume.** Within the building itself, fixed fog cannon or mist systems positioned to cover the working face, the reclaim area and the high-level dust layer, triggered by dust concentration rather than by a timer. In an enclosed space, discharge is against the internal volume and the movement of the machines, not against a wind, which changes both the placement and the duty.

**At the openings.** The gates are the boundary. Fog curtains at vehicle entrances and exits, activated by the vehicle's approach, contain the dust that would otherwise be dragged out by the vehicle itself and by the pressure difference the movement creates. This is the layer that reconciles the conflict between ventilation and containment, because it allows exchange to happen while limiting what leaves.

**In the extract air.** Where mechanical ventilation is used to manage heat, humidity and gas, the extracted air has to be cleaned — bag or cartridge filtration with the capacity matched to the actual duty — and the recovered coal returned to the process. Extraction without filtration simply relocates the emission.

**On the floor and the steelwork.** Cleaning is a control measure, not housekeeping. Vacuum recovery is the correct method because it removes material from the building. Compressed air blowdown is the wrong method because it does exactly what the first stage of a dust explosion needs: it suspends the settled inventory as a cloud. Where the material is carbonaceous and can smoulder, the recovered dust should leave the building promptly rather than accumulate in containers standing indoors.

**In the safety layer.** Venting, isolation and suppression, a documented hazard analysis, selected electrical equipment, and temperature and gas monitoring with defined alarm levels and defined responses.

Underneath all of it, and this is what turns the layers into a system rather than a shopping list: the dust control, the ventilation, the gas monitoring and the material handling have to be interlocked through one control layer, with the sequence rule that suppression starts before the handling equipment does and stops after it stops.

## Dry Fog vs. Water: Why Volume Is the Wrong Lever

There is a persistent assumption that dust control is a question of how much water you can put on the material. Inside an enclosed coal store, that assumption is close to backwards.

The reason is the size of the particles that matter. The fraction that stays suspended, travels, and penetrates the lung is below about 10 micrometres. A conventional spray produces droplets in the hundreds of micrometres and above; these fall fast, wet the surface, and add moisture to the coal while capturing relatively little of the fine airborne fraction. Fine fog — produced by combining compressed air and water to generate droplets in the range of roughly 1 to 10 micrometres — puts droplets into the same size class as the particles they are meant to capture, so that collision and agglomeration do the work and the resulting agglomerates settle under gravity. The humidity added to the material is correspondingly small.

For an enclosed coal store, that difference is decisive in three ways. It determines whether the suppression system can run continuously without pushing the coal outside its handling moisture band. It determines whether the interior stays workable, because a system that fills a shed with visible water mist trades a dust visibility problem for a water visibility problem, and makes the dust-versus-water distinction that the monitoring system depends on even harder. And it determines where each technology belongs: fine fog is a point control for enclosed transfer points, chutes and loading points, while a cannon is a volume control for the open working face and the reclaim area. They are two ends of the same process, not a basic version and an advanced version of the same product.

## Make the Invisible Visible — Monitoring and Interlocks

A control strategy inside an enclosure only works if it is driven by measurement. The package that a modern enclosed coal store needs covers four families of data.

**Dust.** Particulate monitoring across the size fractions that matter, placed at the working areas where people actually are, at the transfer points, and at high level where the fine fraction accumulates. The purpose is not only to prove compliance; it is to start suppression before conditions deteriorate, and to build the evidence base that distinguishes a genuine excursion from a measurement artefact.

**Gas and oxygen.** Methane at high level, carbon monoxide at multiple points in the pile and the free space, oxygen for both combustion and personnel safety. These belong to the process safety layer and need their own alarm logic and their own response, including the possibility of stopping work and leaving the space.

**Temperature.** Distributed sensing into the pile and along the structure, or thermal imaging, to catch the heat that precedes the gas. Temperature-only trends and temperature-plus-CO trends warrant different responses, and the differences should be written down in advance.

**Video and visual emission monitoring.** Cameras serve two purposes: they give an operator in the control room a real-time view of whether dust is leaving or accumulating, and — with recognition applied — they can trigger suppression automatically. That capability is directly relevant in jurisdictions where the permit criterion is written as a visual one: no visible dust escaping from the production facility.

The interlock logic is where a system earns its cost. Dust over threshold starts suppression. Vehicle approach opens a fog curtain and closes it after the vehicle has passed. Conveyor start signals enable suppression upstream; conveyor stop signals disable it downstream. Ventilation responds to gas and to heat load, not to the clock. Everything is logged, with retention long enough to answer a regulator and long enough to see a trend. And the monitoring has to be able to tell dust from water vapour, because a system that cannot will be ignored, and an ignored system is worse than no system at all.

## Three Questions Worth Asking Before You Buy Anything

Dust control inside an enclosed coal yard is a system design problem, and the failure mode is not usually a bad fan or a bad nozzle. It is a set of individually reasonable components that were never designed to work together, in a building whose ventilation, handling pattern and monitoring were decided separately.

That produces a short and practical set of questions, and none of them require a specification sheet.

**Was this designed as one system?** Dust control, ventilation, gas monitoring and interlocks, sized against the actual material flow and the actual building volume — or several quotations for equipment added up at the end?

**Has the supplier worked in this kind of structure before?** A coal silo, a large-span dome, a bar shed and an air-supported structure behave differently, and the differences are learned on site, not in a catalogue.

**What happens after commissioning?** Not the warranty period — the fifteen years after it. Whether the design is stable, whether consumables and spares remain available, and how quickly support arrives when a system that is supposed to run continuously does not.

The enclosure was the right decision. It removed a real and visible problem. What it did not do was remove the dust — it concentrated it, along with the heat and the gas, in a space where people work and where confinement is one of the five conditions a dust explosion needs. Treating the enclosed coal yard as an engineered space with its own air quality, its own temperature and gas profile, and its own control architecture is the second half of the job, and it is the half that determines whether the enclosure is remembered as a compliance project or as a safety upgrade.
