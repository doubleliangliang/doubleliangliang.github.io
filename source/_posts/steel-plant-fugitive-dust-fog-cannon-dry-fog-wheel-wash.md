Steel plants have spent two decades and a great deal of capital on the stack. Desulphurisation and denitrification on the sinter strand, high-efficiency bag houses, electrostatic precipitators and continuous monitoring at every channelled outlet are now standard engineering, delivered by mature vendors, backed by data that can be defended line by line. For a large part of the industry, organised emissions are a solved problem.

The part that is still open is, literally, open. Particulate that leaves a stockyard, a transfer tower, a cast house or a haul road never passes a sampling port. There is no stack, no flow rate, no continuous monitor. European regulators call it **diffuse dust**; in North America the usual term is **fugitive dust**; both describe the same thing — particulate released from a process without passing through a controlled point of discharge.

That is where permits are still breached, where neighbours still file complaints, and where equipment decisions are most often made on the wrong basis.

This article takes one question with four answers. **What actually reduces fugitive dust in a steelworks, and where does each tool belong?** The tools are long-range fog cannons, dry fog systems, wheel wash stations, and the control layer that decides when the other three run. Everything else in the plant — sinter strand, coke oven, converter primary dedusting — is mentioned only where it marks a boundary.

## Why the Visible Plume Is the Last Problem Standing

There is a practical reason fugitive dust outlives the stack problem. A stack is a single, fixed, accessible point. You can measure it, model it, retrofit it, and prove the result to an inspector. A stockyard is none of those things. It is an area, not a point; it changes shape as material is stacked and reclaimed; it depends on the weather; and its emissions are driven by the movement of vehicles that have to keep moving to keep the plant running.

Three structural properties explain why fugitive dust resists the usual approach.

**It is distributed.** An integrated works may contain dozens of genuine emission points spread over several square kilometres — a raw material yard, a dozen transfer towers, a crushing and screening station, a coke and coal handling route, a slag yard, and a road network connecting all of them. No single device sits in front of that.

**It is intermittent.** A truck tips for forty seconds. A loader works a pile for three minutes. A gust crosses an open stockpile and the plume is gone before anyone reaches for a control. Average-based thinking fails here, because the emission is concentrated into short events.

**It is unmeasured.** This is the most consequential of the three. A stack has a monitor; most open sources have nothing attached to them at all. Without a baseline there is no way to prove improvement, and without proof a dust control programme becomes a matter of opinion.

The consequence is a familiar pattern: capital is spent, equipment is installed, and at the next review nobody can say what changed. The fix is not a bigger machine. It is to treat fugitive dust as a measurable operating problem rather than an equipment purchase.

## The Language Your Regulator Actually Uses

One source of confusion in international projects is terminology. Different jurisdictions frame the same obligation in different words, and a proposal written in the vocabulary of one system can read as non-responsive in another.

In the **European Union**, the controlling instrument is the Industrial Emissions Directive (2010/75/EU), supported by the BAT conclusions for iron and steel production (Commission Implementing Decision 2012/135/EU). Those conclusions deal with diffuse dust explicitly. The general requirement is to prevent or reduce diffuse dust from material storage, handling and transport, and they set out an associated *diffuse dust action plan* within the plant's environmental management system — including the use of sufficient PM10 monitors together with wind direction and strength data to identify which sources are actually driving ambient readings. The listed techniques include orienting long stockpiles along the prevailing wind, wind barriers, controlling delivered moisture content, containment on conveyors and in hoppers, dust-suppressing water sprays, rigorous housekeeping with cleaning and damping of roads, dust extraction and bag filtration, and emissions-reduced sweeping cars. For road transport the same document is direct: hard-surfaced roads, cleaning of roads, sheeting of vehicles, restriction of vehicles to designated routes — and the employment of **wheel-cleaning equipment to prevent the carryover of mud and dust onto public roads**.

In the **United States**, the framework runs through the National Emission Standards for Hazardous Air Pollutants for integrated iron and steel manufacturing, together with National Ambient Air Quality Standards for PM10 and PM2.5, state permit conditions, and — for many sites — visible-emission or opacity limits assessed by trained observers using EPA Method 9. Opacity limits make a visual criterion legally enforceable, which has a direct engineering consequence discussed later.

In **emerging markets**, project-financed greenfield capacity frequently has to meet the IFC Performance Standards and the World Bank Group's environmental, health and safety guidelines from day one, because the financing depends on it.

China's steel sector uses a different construction again — a nationwide programme of **ultra-low emission** retrofits, with concentration limits for organised sources and a visible-criterion requirement for fugitive sources, supported by detailed technical guidance on enclosed storage, enclosed conveying, wheel and body washing equipment at yard exits, and a plant-wide monitoring network covering boundary air quality, key dust-generating areas and treatment equipment run status.

The vocabulary differs. The engineering required converges. In every one of these frameworks the same four functions have to be performed: bring down dust across open areas, lock it down at enclosed points, stop it leaving the site on wheels, and prove that all of the above actually happened.

## Grouping the Plant: Eight Scenarios, Not Eighty Sources

The first practical step in any fugitive dust programme is to stop counting sources and start grouping them. A dust inventory that lists every transfer point, hopper lip and loader pass produces a spreadsheet nobody can act on. Group by **the place and the operation that happens there**, and an integrated steelworks collapses into a manageable set of scenarios.

![Typical fugitive dust scenarios in an integrated steel plant and the measures that match each one](/images/steel-plant-dust-control-scenario-map.svg)

| Scenario | What happens there | Matching measure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raw material stockyard | Stacking, reclaiming, hopper discharge, truck traffic, yard roads | Long-range fog cannon; enclosure where the material and layout allow |
| Belt transfer tower and conveyor gallery | Continuous transfer between belts, confined, fine and dry dust | Dry fog at the transfer points, plus enclosure and local extraction |
| Crushing and screening station | Enclosed crusher and screens; open tipping apron in front of them | Dry fog inside the enclosure; fog cannon on the open apron |
| Coal and coke handling | Open coal yard, grinding, bunkers, coke screening | Fog cannon on the open yard; protection-matched configuration in the coal system |
| Sinter plant | Organised emission from the strand; transfer points and screens | Capture and bag filtration first; dry fog only at enclosed transfers |
| Blast furnace cast house | Short, intense, thermally buoyant emission during tapping | Capture hood or air screen first; event-triggered assistance at the perimeter |
| Steelmaking and slag handling | Converter or EAF secondary emissions; slag pits and cooling | Secondary capture; fog cannon at the slag yard; covered cooling practice |
| Haul roads and vehicle exits | Re-suspended fines, and carry-out onto public roads | Fog cannon, mechanical sweeper, and a wheel wash station at the gate |

Four of these scenarios are the subject of this article. The others are listed because knowing what a tool is *not* for is part of using it well, and because they mark the boundaries of water-based dust control. Sinter strands and cast houses are organised-emission problems, addressed by capture and filtration; slag handling has a cooling and covering dimension before it has a spraying one. A proposal that claims a spray system solves the whole plant is not describing a plant that exists.

## Four Tools, Four Different Jobs

The confusion that costs the most money in this field is treating the available equipment as one product family, ranked by size. It is not. There are four functions, and only one of them involves a decision about how much water to put into the air.

![Four distinct roles in steel plant fugitive dust control: area suppression, point suppression, vehicle carry-out control and the control layer that schedules them](/images/steel-plant-four-tools-roles.svg)

**Area suppression** is the long-range fog cannon. It projects a large volume of atomised water across open ground and brings a visible plume down over an area. It is the right tool for stockyards, the open apron at a crushing station, slag yards, and haul roads.

**Point suppression** is the dry fog system. It atomises compressed air and water together at the nozzle, producing droplets in the micron range that cause fine particles to agglomerate and settle without wetting the product or the belt. It operates inside an enclosure, at a fixed source, at distances measured in metres.

**Carry-out control** is the wheel wash station. It has nothing to do with air. Its job is to prevent the dust already deposited on a vehicle and its tyres from being carried onto public roads, where it becomes a deposit that dries, is re-suspended by traffic, and is attributed to the plant.

**The control layer** decides when the other three run, and produces the evidence that they ran. It is the difference between owning equipment and operating a system.

A steelworks usually needs all four. The proportions differ by site, and the sequence in which they are applied matters more than the quantity of any single one.

## Fog Cannons: Bringing Down a Plume Across an Area

A fog cannon pressurises water through a ring of nozzles and accelerates the resulting spray with an axial fan. Depending on nozzle selection and operating pressure, droplets range from tens to a few hundred microns. Capture is dominated by inertial collision and gravitational settling, effective distance runs from tens of metres to a few hundred, and the unit can be mounted on a tower so that no operator has to stand in the dust to run it.

Three characteristics determine whether a cannon performs in a steel plant rather than on a test pad.

**Reach is a still-air figure.** Published throw distances are measured under calm conditions, without obstruction, at one droplet size. In a working yard a crosswind noticeably shortens effective reach and pushes the plume off target. Mounting height, the overlap between adjacent units, and wind-aware presets matter more than the headline number. A layout designed so that any single unit can fail without leaving a gap is more robust than one that depends on a hero machine at the corner of the yard.

**Droplet size must match the dust, and the match is not universal.** Coarse spray above roughly 200 microns knocks down a visible plume over a stockpile effectively, but does very little to respirable particles. Fine mist in the 10 to 50 micron band is the correct regime for PM10 and PM2.5 — and it only works when the nozzle is close to the source, because fine droplets lose momentum and evaporate quickly over a long throw. This is precisely why long-range cannons and dry fog systems are complements rather than substitutes.

![Droplet size versus dust particle size, and the distance over which each suppression method remains effective](/images/steel-plant-droplet-matching-throw-distance.svg)

**Water is a budget item, not a free resource.** Every litre applied to a stockyard either evaporates, drains, or enters the material. In a steel plant the last of those matters: added moisture has to be agreed with the people running the sinter strand and the blast furnace, not decided by the environmental department alone. Specify flow rates after the acceptable moisture increment is agreed, and treat the winter case as a design condition rather than a seasonal inconvenience — a system that has to be bypassed in the coldest month has an availability problem, not a winter problem.

In the stockyard itself there is one more rule worth repeating, because it is the most commonly skipped step: **water brings dust down, but it does not remove it.** Particles that have settled on a haul road are re-suspended by the next vehicle. Without a mechanical sweeper working the same route on a defined schedule, the suppression system is effectively moving dust from the air to the ground and back again. Cannon plus sweeper is one measure, not two.

## Dry Fog: Locking a Point Source Where It Starts

A dry fog system atomises compressed air and low-pressure water together inside the nozzle. The result is a fog with droplets typically in the 1 to 10 micron band. Capture is dominated by Brownian diffusion and agglomeration rather than by gravity, effective distance is a few metres because the nozzle sits directly at the emission point, and the moisture added to the material is measured in fractions of a percent.

Those characteristics fit a specific set of problems. Belt transfer points and loading skirts, where material falls and air is displaced, releasing fines that are too fine to settle quickly. Crusher feed and discharge openings, vibrating screens, chute loading points. Bucket elevators. Bunker and hopper charging points. These locations share three properties: the dust is fine, the source is fixed and confined, and the belt or the product cannot tolerate added water — belt slip and material handling problems both begin with moisture.

There is one non-negotiable precondition. **Dry fog requires containment.** Used in the open, the fog disperses and the dust does not move; the equipment runs, consumes air and water, and achieves nothing measurable. This is why dry fog design always starts with the enclosure, the skirting and the extraction, and only then considers nozzles. When a supplier proposes a dry fog system for an unenclosed transfer or an open apron, the correct next question is what will contain it.

The strategic value of dry fog in a steel plant is that it addresses the sources a cannon cannot serve. A cannon aimed at a transfer tower sprays water into the open air beside the tower, not into the chute. Pairing the two gives complete coverage of the material handling route: dry fog inside the enclosure, cannon on the open yard and the apron.

## Wheel Wash: The Compliance Device at the Gate

Wheel wash is usually the last measure to be considered and the first to be cited in a compliance review, because its absence is visible from outside the fence. Material deposited on a haul road adheres to tyres and undercarriage; a vehicle leaving the site transfers it onto the public road; the deposit dries, traffic re-suspends it, and the resulting dust is recorded as the plant's contribution to ambient particulate levels regardless of how well the stockyard itself is managed.

This is why the European BAT conclusions name wheel-cleaning equipment specifically, alongside hard-surfaced transport roads, road cleaning and sheeting of loads. It is also why national technical guidance in several jurisdictions treats the wheel and body wash as a required installation at yard exits rather than an optional accessory, often specifying that it be sited immediately at the exit and that the wash water be captured or treated rather than allowed to run off.

The engineering content of a wheel wash station is larger than it appears from the name.

**Coverage.** Cleaning the tyres alone leaves the undercarriage and the lower body, which carry a substantial share of the load. A useful installation addresses the full footprint of the vehicle, including the chassis and the areas behind the mudflaps where deposits accumulate.

**Water capture.** A wheel wash that discharges onto the ground at the gate simply relocates the problem a few metres. The station needs a collection and treatment arrangement — settlement, recycling or discharge to a treatment facility — and it needs a maintenance routine for the sludge that is generated, because that sludge is the material that was being prevented from leaving.

**Free drainage.** A vehicle that leaves the gate still shedding water deposits a wet trail on the public road, which is both a nuisance and, in freezing conditions, a safety hazard. A drainage or drying stage after the wash, and heat tracing in cold climates, are part of the specification rather than optional extras.

**Wrong-way traffic.** The station only works if vehicles actually pass through it. Physical arrangement, signage and site discipline matter as much as the nozzle layout, and on larger sites more than one exit may need coverage.

Wheel wash is not an air quality device. It is a materials containment device, and it is most effective when it is designed together with the road cleaning schedule, the surface hardening programme and the site traffic plan.

## Smart Control: Replacing the Timer with an Event

The conventional installation is a timer and a contactor. The yard sprays for five minutes every half hour, whether or not anything is moving. It is inexpensive, defensible on paper, and it fails in three directions at once: water and power are spent on an empty yard; the material becomes too wet in one season and the pipes freeze in another, so operators disable the system; and when a review asks what happened during a particular shift last month, no record exists.

The alternative is to run the system as a loop rather than a schedule. Six functions, executed continuously: **monitor, identify, grade, trigger, verify, record**.

![Closed-loop control for diffuse dust in a steel plant: monitoring, identification, grading, triggering, verification and recording](/images/steel-plant-diffuse-dust-control-loop.svg)

**Monitor.** Cameras aimed at actual emission points rather than at the skyline. Online particulate monitors at hot spots and at the boundary. A compact weather station supplying wind speed, wind direction, temperature and humidity. The wind data is not decoration: it determines whether a spray programme lands on the source or drifts onto a road, a workshop or a neighbouring property, and the EU BAT framework explicitly relies on combining monitor data with wind direction to identify which sources are driving ambient readings.

**Identify.** Vision models detect plumes and — critically — distinguish them from steam and water vapour, which is the single most common false-alarm source in a steel plant, since slag cooling, wet quenching and wet material all produce white plumes that are not dust. A second class of models recognises the operation itself: truck dumping, loader movement, belt running, tapping, slag pouring. Recognising the operation allows suppression to begin before the plume is visible rather than after it has dispersed.

**Grade.** A binary alarm produces either nuisance triggering or missed events. Grading converts a detection into a proportionate response: a short local burst at the lowest level, the full preset for the zone at the next, and an escalation to the control room with the image attached at the highest. This is also the mechanism that prevents a stuck valve from running a pump for an entire shift.

**Trigger.** Only the affected zone runs, with stored presets for azimuth, pitch, flow, droplet size and duration. The gateway should speak the plant's own protocol — dry contact, Modbus or MQTT — with a proper handshake to the distributed control system, so that operators retain authority over their area rather than losing it to a black box.

**Verify.** The same sensors that raised the alarm confirm that the plume cleared. If it does not clear within the expected window, the system escalates instead of spraying indefinitely.

**Record.** Zone status, alarm lists, equipment availability, image pairs, water and power consumption, and compliance records — exported on a schedule that an auditor or a permit reviewer can read, and retained for the period the permit requires.

Two rules make the difference between a system that survives and one that is muted within a month. Thresholds must be set **per zone**, because an alarm level that makes sense at a stockyard is meaningless at a transfer tower; and a commissioning period on site is a normal part of delivery, not evidence that the product is unfinished. Expect two to four weeks of tuning per plant and plan for it.

The most useful KPI is also the most direct: **visible dust-escape events per week, per zone**. It is a number an operations manager can act on, a number a community liaison officer can report, and — where opacity or visible-emission limits apply — a number that maps directly onto the regulatory criterion.

## Where Water Is the Wrong Answer

A credible dust control programme states its limits as clearly as its applications, because equipment specified into the wrong scenario is not merely ineffective: it gets switched off, and the conclusion drawn is that dust control does not work in steel plants.

**Organised sources belong to capture and filtration, not to spraying.** The sinter strand, the coke oven, the converter primary off-gas system and the cast house all have a channelled emission and a thermal driving force. Spraying water into a rising thermal plume mostly wastes water and can be counterproductive. These are capture-hood and bag-filter problems, supported where appropriate by air screens and perimeter assistance. The primary engineering effort belongs upstream of any spray.

**Product-critical streams need a moisture budget before a nozzle.** In a steel plant this applies to sinter feed, coal for injection, and any material whose handling behaviour changes when it gets damp. Dry fog's minimal moisture addition is exactly why it is preferred at enclosed transfer points and bunkers, and why a coarse spray is the wrong choice inside a conveyor gallery.

**Coal and coke systems require protection matched to the application.** A suspended coal dust cloud is itself a hazard. Housekeeping practice matters as much as equipment: blowing accumulated dust off with compressed air is precisely the action that creates the cloud an explosion needs, so wet or vacuum methods are used instead. Ex configurations can be provided to suit the customer's application; the specific protection type and certificate are supplied with the project. Layer control, temperature and carbon monoxide monitoring, and correct electrical protection are part of the same package.

**Slag handling has a cooling dimension.** Dust released during slag cooling and processing is often better addressed by covering, moisture management and enclosed crushing with extraction than by spraying into steam. Where a spray is used, its job is usually containment at the pit, not general suppression.

## Matching the Tool to the Source

Read across the table, and a simple rule emerges. **Open, coarse and wind- or traffic-driven sources take area suppression. Confined, fine and continuous sources take point suppression. Vehicles leaving the site take wheel wash. All of them take a control layer if the result is to be provable.**

| Character of the source | Dominant mechanism | Matching measure | Precondition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Open area, coarse particles, wind-driven | Inertial collision, gravity | Long-range fog cannon | Reach and overlap designed for the site wind; sweeping planned |
| Confined point, fine particles, continuous | Diffusion and agglomeration | Dry fog system | Containment or enclosure exists |
| Vehicle tyres and undercarriage, site exit | Adhesion and carry-out | Wheel wash station | Capture and treatment of wash water; drainage |
| Time-varying, event-driven across many sources | Detection and scheduling | Control layer with per-zone presets | Sensors at the source; operator authority preserved |

The pairing is not a hierarchy. A dry fog system is not a small fog cannon, and a fog cannon is not a large dry fog system. They operate at opposite ends of the material handling chain: dry fog locks a source, a cannon covers an area, wheel wash closes the gate, and control connects them into something that can be measured.

## Choosing Between Quotations Without Writing a Tender

Most sourcing decisions in this industry are still taken on three columns — throw distance, flow rate, price — none of which predicts what the equipment will cost to own. The three soft questions that actually separate suppliers are a conversation rather than a scoring matrix.

**Was it designed as a system?** Ask how the pump, spray ring, nozzles, fan and control were matched to one another, and what the design was optimised for — reach, coverage, water consumption, or year-round availability. A supplier who can explain the trade-offs they chose has usually thought about the duty. A specification that lists components without describing how they were matched describes an assembly, not a design.

**What has it actually done in this industry?** Not a gallery of site photographs, but how long the supplier has been building for steel, mining and bulk material handling, and what those installations look like after several years of dust, abrasion and continuous duty. Experience shows up in details: whether a manifold can be drained, whether a nozzle can be changed without dismantling the ring, whether a control cabinet sits somewhere it will not flood.

**What happens after commissioning?** Spare parts availability, response arrangements and a documented winter procedure decide the annual availability figure. A settled product platform with a long supply commitment lets a plant build a maintenance routine; a design revised every year does not.

A certain amount of durability information is not verifiable on site regardless of how the questions are framed. Droplet size cannot be measured in a working plant and drifts over time as nozzles wear, scale and filters load. Noise and vibration differences are not audible on a new machine and only accumulate under continuous duty. Throw distance is a design figure measured under defined conditions, and there is no mandatory test regime for it — which is exactly why an industry technical standard, such as JB/T 13562-2018 *Technical specification for air-assisted spray dust suppression devices*, is more useful at tender stage than a brochure number. Asking under what conditions a figure was obtained is more informative than comparing the figure itself.

The cost of getting this wrong is not theoretical. A unit that underperforms in effective reach or spray distribution does not fix itself with a settings change; the remedy is additional units, new foundations and a second round of shutdown coordination, usually against a compliance deadline. An unreliable unit produces repeated repairs in an area that is already dusty and has poor visibility, which is a safety question before it is anything else. Neither outcome is visible in a three-column comparison. What is visible, after three years, is which supplier's equipment is still running and which has become a maintenance item.

The catalogue is the least informative document a supplier can send. What matters is whether the design was deliberate and whether the parts, the documentation and the service people will still be there in year six.

## Running It: Layout, Zoning and Tuning

Three implementation decisions determine whether the system performs as designed.

**Zoning before sizing.** A large target area should be split into zones served by multiple units rather than covered by a single machine. Zoning provides redundancy — one unit under maintenance leaves no gap — and it is what allows the control layer to spray only where an event is occurring. It also produces per-zone consumption data, which is the only way to demonstrate efficiency later.

**Overlap by wind, not by nameplate.** Adjacent cannons should be positioned so that their effective coverage overlaps under the site's prevailing wind, not so that their published throw distances just touch. This is a drawing exercise that has to be done with site wind data, and it is the step most often skipped when a layout is copied from a previous project.

**Tuning as a scheduled activity.** Thresholds, presets and zones need adjustment during the first weeks of operation and periodic review afterwards. A system left on its commissioning settings will either over-spray or generate nuisance alarms. Budget the tuning as scope, and keep a record of what was changed and why — that record is also what a subsequent reviewer will ask for.

## What This Looks Like in Practice

The direction of travel in the industry is consistent, and it is visible in how established producers describe their own programmes. European steelmakers publishing dust reduction roadmaps describe the same combination set out above: windbreaks around storage areas, covered slag cooling with a mist curtain, covering and extraction on bunker and conveyor routes, tyre washing at material logistics exits, additional environmental cameras, and extra capture hoods at tapping operations. The individual numbers differ by site. The architecture does not.

That is the useful conclusion for anyone planning a fugitive dust programme now. The question is no longer whether a cannon can throw 120 metres, or whether a dry fog nozzle produces a five-micron droplet. The question is whether the plant can show, for any given shift, what the air did at each of its dust scenarios and what the system did about it — with the source correctly grouped, the right tool applied to it, and a record that survives review.
