An open-pit mine has one dust problem in the way that a house has one fire risk. The phrase covers eight different sources behaving on two entirely different clocks, and the equipment bought to deal with one of them is routinely asked to deal with all of them. That is the origin of most dust programmes that look complete on paper and underperform on site.

The practical starting point is not a machine. It is a source inventory — what is emitted, from where, for how long, in what size fraction, and how far it travels before it becomes somebody else's problem. Once that inventory exists, the sequence of control measures follows from it almost mechanically, and the argument about which device to buy becomes a much smaller argument.

## The Dust Does Not Come From Where Most Budgets Assume

Walk around the perimeter fence of a mine that has a dust programme and you will usually find the same investment pattern. Cannons aimed at stockpiles. A water cart on the main ramp. Some hosing at the crusher. The visible, photogenic sources have been covered, because those are the ones that get photographed, complained about and pointed at during an inspection.

The dominant source is usually not among them, and it is not visible as a plume. It is the road.

This is not an opinion about mining. It is what every published emissions inventory for surface mining has concluded for two decades. Studies of open-pit coal operations place transport on unpaved haul roads at **80 to 96 per cent of PM10 emissions** from the mining operation, with the broad figure for total dust generation from haulage in opencast mines cited at around 70 to 90 per cent. Emission factors for unpaved road haulage have been estimated at roughly **0.18 kg of PM10 and 0.73 kg of total suspended particles for every tonne of coal produced** — a number worth sitting with, because it is expressed per tonne of product, which means it scales with production rather than with the size of the fleet.

![Reported share of particulate emissions from surface mining by source group, with transport on unpaved haul roads at 80 to 96 per cent of PM10](/images/open-pit-dust-pm10-contribution-shares.svg)

None of this means blasting and drilling do not matter. It means they matter for different reasons — health, safety, and the momentary spectacle that drives community complaints — and they are controlled by different measures. Treating the road as a secondary concern while spending the capital budget on cannons is the single most common misallocation in open-pit dust control.

## Eight Sources, and the Two Clocks They Run On

The distinction that determines everything downstream is not how big a source is. It is whether the dust is released in an instant or continuously for ten hours.

**Short-lived events.** Blasting, dumping and loading release a large mass of dust in seconds to minutes. Measured concentrations immediately after detonation have been reported at around 3.45 × 10⁻² kg/m³ — tens of grams per cubic metre, against ambient limits measured in micrograms. The control window is short and it is mostly *before* the event: pre-wetting the bench, water stemming, and immediate suppression of the cloud while it is still cohering near the source. A machine that arrives four minutes after the blast has missed the job.

**Continuous release.** Drilling, haulage, crushing, stockpiles and wind erosion run for the whole shift, every shift. There is no event to respond to. These sources are controlled by whether the right thing happens automatically, at the right moment, when nobody is watching — which is a controls problem before it is an equipment problem. A continuous source served by a manual system is a source that is controlled for the first two weeks after commissioning.

Two more distinctions matter for design. The **size fraction** decides which droplets or filters can touch the dust at all: a particle of a hundred microns settles on its own if you give it distance, while a particle of three microns will still be airborne a kilometre downwind. And the **silica content** decides what the dust does to the workforce, which is the subject of the section after next.

## Why "Just Water It" Stops Working by Mid-Morning

Water works. It simply does not work for long, and the arithmetic of the haul cycle is the reason.

A haul road is a linear source tens of kilometres long. A water cart wets a strip of it. Between the wetting and the next pass of a loaded truck, the following things happen: the water evaporates — faster on a dry, hot, windy day, and at a rate that a 60-tonne truck with hot tyres does not slow down; the truck's tyres pick up the residual moisture in the surface, which is exactly the mechanism that generates dust in the first place; and the fine material that the tyres lifted is now dry and airborne again. Then the cart reaches the end of the circuit and starts the next pass, which may be forty minutes away.

That is the structural weakness: **the watering cycle and the traffic cycle are different lengths, and the traffic cycle is shorter.** Any road whose dust control is a single water cart circulating on a fixed route will exceed its target for part of every hour, and the part tends to be the busiest part.

There are practical consequences beyond effectiveness, and they are the ones operators raise first. Water consumption on a large mine site is a genuine constraint, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions where the same water is competing with process use and with the community. Runoff has to be managed and cannot be discharged. Repeated watering degrades the road surface, producing ruts, potholes and a softer running course that generates more fines the next day. In winter the water freezes, and the freezing case is not a slow degradation but a pressure event that cracks pipes and splits pumps. And water hardness and turbidity from mine sources are the leading cause of nozzle blockage — a dust system that loses its nozzles in the first season is a dust system that has stopped working while still being on the asset register.

None of which is an argument against water. It is an argument against water **alone**, and against watering used as a substitute for the design work.

## The Four Variables That Actually Decide What a Haul Road Emits

If watering is the only lever a site pulls, it is fighting three others that are set elsewhere.

**Vehicle speed.** Emissions from unpaved roads rise with speed as a power law rather than linearly, and the relationship is well established. A speed limit that is enforced — by telemetry, not by signage — is a dust control measure with an unusually good cost-benefit ratio.

**Payload and axle load.** Heavier trucks do more damage per pass to the running surface, crushing material into a finer, more easily suspended fraction, and they lift more of what is already there. The effect compounds: the same road, carrying the same tonnage in larger trucks, is a different road by the end of the season.

**Silt content of the running surface.** This is the reservoir from which every other variable draws. It is recharged by spillage from loaded trucks, by material tracked out of loading areas, by grading that mixes fines into the running course, and by airborne deposition. Road maintenance is therefore dust control, and the two are usually budgeted separately.

**Surface moisture.** The only one of the four that changes within hours — and the one most sites treat as the only one that exists.

![Four variables driving haul road dust emissions, alongside the relationship between PM10 from tyre disturbance and surface moisture, which turns sharply at around two per cent](/images/haul-road-dust-emission-drivers.svg)

The moisture relationship is worth looking at closely, because it explains why watering seems so effective at first and so disappointing later. Measured PM10 from tyre disturbance stayed **below 10 mg/m³ once silt moisture reached roughly 2 per cent**, and rose steeply below that. The curve is not a slope; it is a cliff and a plateau. Watering a dry road produces a dramatic, visible improvement, and then the improvement decays fast as the surface dries through the steep part of the curve. Watering a road that is already near the plateau produces almost nothing except runoff.

Which is the operational argument for doing three things instead of one: reduce the silt load so there is less to suspend, hold the surface near the plateau rather than trying to soak it, and stop treating a road as a seasonal problem. Where water is scarce, the same logic is what makes chemical suppressants, binders and surface stabilisation worth their cost — not because they are better than water at wetting, but because they extend the plateau.

## What the Dust Is Doing While the Decision Is Being Deferred

**It is shortening lives, on a long delay.** Hard-rock mining liberates crystalline silica, and respirable crystalline silica is the fraction that reaches the alveoli. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classified crystalline silica inhaled occupationally, in the form of quartz or cristobalite, as **carcinogenic to humans, Group 1, in 1997** — the same classification category as asbestos and tobacco smoke. The disease it causes, silicosis, is not treatable and does not stop progressing when exposure stops. Chronic silicosis typically appears after 10 to 30 years of low to moderate exposure; accelerated silicosis after 3 to 10 years of higher exposure; and acute silicosis can appear within roughly 8 to 18 months of intense exposure and progress to respiratory failure. In China, reported new pneumoconiosis cases have exceeded **20,000 a year since 2010**, with silicosis and coal workers' pneumoconiosis together accounting for more than 95 per cent of them.

**It is degrading haulage safety.** Dust reduces visibility on ramps and at intersections, and reduced visibility on a mine haul road is not an air quality issue at all — it is a collision risk involving vehicles that weigh hundreds of tonnes. Operators who will not fund dust control for environmental reasons usually fund it as soon as it is framed as a safety measure.

**It is fixing the value of the permit, not the dust.** Ambient and emission limits differ by jurisdiction, but the shape of the requirement is universal: a boundary that must not be exceeded, and a perimeter that must not show visible dust leaving the site. China's ambient air quality standard GB 3095-2012 sets second-class limits at 70 µg/m³ annual and 150 µg/m³ daily for PM10, 35 and 75 µg/m³ for PM2.5, and 200 and 300 µg/m³ for TSP; the integrated emission standard GB 16297-1996 sets a perimeter limit of 1.0 mg/m³ for fugitive particulate matter. The World Health Organization's 2021 global air quality guidelines go considerably further, recommending 15 µg/m³ annual and 45 µg/m³ daily for PM10. The important thing about these numbers is not their strictness but their behaviour: they are measured where the dust arrives, not where it is generated. A mine can control its sources well and still fail at the fence, if it did not first understand the paths in between.

**It is quietly consuming the fleet.** Abrasive dust drawn into engines accelerates cylinder and ring wear; it blocks radiators and air filters, contaminates lubricants, and attacks electrical enclosures and switchgear contacts. On a site with tens of haul trucks, the maintenance line item is larger than the dust control budget that would have prevented part of it.

## Autonomous Haulage Removed the Drivers. It Did Not Remove the Dust.

China's open-pit mines are being converted to remote and autonomous operation faster than almost any other industrial setting in the world, and the numbers moved quickly. Autonomous haul trucks in operation at Chinese mines went from about **88 units in 2020 to more than 4,000 by the end of 2025**. One supplier alone runs more than 2,300 trucks across 26-plus sites, with the largest single-mine deployment above 500 units. Roughly two-thirds of the mines that have adopted autonomous haulage are open-pit coal operations, and autonomous trucks account for close to 88 per cent of the vehicles running at those sites. The performance figures have stopped being demonstration numbers: extended-range and battery-electric autonomous trucks have been reported at **98 to 107 per cent of manual transport efficiency**, with fuel savings of 20 to 30 per cent per unit. At one iron-ore operation, phased conversion of the haul fleet cut driver positions by about 150 — close to 80 per cent of the driving roster — with annual labour savings above 42 million yuan.

The safety argument behind that conversion is worth stating precisely, because it is usually stated loosely. In the conventional model, haulage is where the people are: transport accounts for close to half of a mine's total labour. Every truck carries a driver. Every shovel carries operators. Around them run light vehicles — pickups, survey utes, supervisors, fitters, visitors — and on a mine these are traditionally marked with a **very tall flag on a pole**, because a person beside a light vehicle simply does not register in the field of view of a truck whose bonnet sits above the cab roof. Now put that traffic mix on a ramp where visibility is periodically reduced by the material this article is about. A dust plume is not a nuisance in that context. It is the condition under which a 300-tonne truck and a light vehicle share a road.

Autonomy dissolves that configuration. It removes the drivers from the cabs and the fitters from the benches; it withdraws the light vehicles that existed mainly to carry people to machines; and it replaces eyesight with a sensor stack that does not tire and does not need to see through a windscreen. The 5G and 5G-A links, centimetre-level stopping accuracy and multi-modal perception marketed with these fleets are described in terms of safety and productivity, and they are — but they also describe a system that reads the road through lidar, millimetre-wave radar and cameras, and that holds station in visibility of a few tens of metres in snow, dust or darkness.

**What autonomy does not touch is the source term.** An autonomous truck lifts the same mass of respirable dust off the running surface as a driven one, because the mechanism is tyres and silt, not the driver. Electrification does not change it either: a battery-electric haul truck has no exhaust particulate, which is a genuine and substantial gain, but the dust produced by tyre shear on an unpaved road is mechanical, not combustion-related. What autonomy changes is **who is exposed**. The occupational health problem moves sharply down the list. The environmental problem at the fence does not move at all — which is why a mine can be fully autonomous, fully electric, and still fail its boundary monitoring.

There is a second-order effect that matters more than it first appears. **Removing the drivers also removes the people who used to notice the road.** A driver on a ten-hour haul cycle is a continuous, involuntary road-condition sensor: they feel the surface, see the plume in the mirror, and complain long before anyone measures anything. When the cab is empty, the road is only monitored if somebody is measuring it. Automation therefore raises the requirement for instrumentation rather than lowering it — and it shifts mine management from managing people to managing equipment, which is a data problem before it is anything else.

## Matching the Method to the Source

There is no single device for an open-pit mine, and the reason is that the four available methods are not rival versions of the same thing. They do different jobs, on different dust, with different response times.

**Fog cannons** manufacture a droplet spectrum and transport it tens to hundreds of metres through the air. They are the right instrument for airborne dust over a moving or intermittent source with a working face, a loading point or a freshly blasted bench — the source is mobile or transitory, so the machine must be able to aim and move.

**Dry fog and fine mist** work inside an enclosure or directly at the mouth of a chute, crusher outlet or transfer point, where the dust is concentrated, the volume is small, and the problem is to wet particles before they can escape. Applied in open air at a stockpile, the same device loses most of its advantage to wind and evaporation.

**Water carts, chemical suppressants and binders** treat surfaces. They act on dust that has not yet been lifted, which makes them the only method that scales to a linear source tens of kilometres long and the wrong method for a cloud that already exists.

**Enclosure and extraction** collects rather than suppresses. It suits fixed points with a defined air volume — crushers, screens, conveyor transfers, load-out stations — and it is the only approach that makes the dust a recoverable product rather than a deposited nuisance.

![Grid matching five open-pit dust source types against four control methods, marking each combination as primary, supporting, limited or not applicable](/images/open-pit-dust-control-method-matching.svg)

The grid in the diagram is deliberately not a specification. Its purpose is to make the mismatch visible: a fog cannon aimed at a haul road is doing the job of a water cart badly, and a water cart dispatched towards an airborne blast cloud will arrive after the cloud has gone. Both mistakes are common, and both are the result of choosing equipment before writing the inventory.

## From the Crusher to the Stockyard: the Half of the Mine That Gets Left Out

The haul road deserves the attention it gets, because that is where most of the mass is. But material does not stop at the crusher, and the second half of its journey is where dust control is simultaneously easier to do properly and, in most projects, less complete.

Follow the material. It is loaded at the face, hauled up the ramp, tipped at a crusher or a run-of-mine stockpile, reclaimed onto a conveyor, transferred two, three or five times, stacked in a yard, and finally loaded out onto road trucks. That is five to seven additional dust sources connected in series, and each one generates dust by a different mechanism.

**Crushers, screens and transfer points** produce the most concentrated dust on the site, and they are the easiest to control — because the equipment is fixed, the air volume is knowable, and the dust is inside a box that can be sealed. The mechanism is specific: material falling down a chute accelerates to five to eight metres per second and drags an induced air stream with it, and it is that induced air, not the wind, that carries the fines out of the transfer. The order of attack matters. A curved chute that slows the fall to two to three metres per second reduces the energy available; a sealed skirt with a double seal on the belt line keeps the displaced air inside; dry fog inside the enclosure wets the particles before they can escape. Vendor measurements for a properly designed transfer-point system put concentrations around the transfer **below 5 mg/m³**, and dry fog and water-air spray have been reported at **70 to 85 per cent reduction in respirable dust**, some of them at roughly a third of the water consumption of conventional water-only spray.

**Conveyor lines** are a linear source over kilometres, exposed to cross wind, and are dealt with by enclosure, double-sealed skirt boards, and belt scrapers that stop the return strand from carrying material back along the line.

**Stockyards and load-out** are the hardest half, because the surface is open and the material is loose: long slopes, exposed faces, wind erosion, self-heating on coal and sulphide material, and a plume at every tipping and loading point. Surface sealing, progressive rehabilitation, and suppression aimed at the fall of material are the practical measures — and they are the ones most often deferred, because they are the least visible.

None of that is new equipment. **What is new is the requirement that all of it be connected** — and here the autonomous haulage described earlier sets the standard.

A mine that has just spent several years wiring its trucks, shovels and dispatch system to a control centre has already built the network and the operating habits. What it usually has not done is extend the same discipline to the dust side of the plant. The result is a site that can tell you, to the metre, where every truck is, and cannot tell you whether the dry fog at transfer point 3 has been running since Tuesday. The material that automation has made possible is a chain in which four things are visible to the same control room, at the same time.

**Production.** Conveyor start and stop, belt load, crusher current, stacker position, truck arrival. These are not only process data; they are the trigger signals. A suppression system that starts because a conveyor started is a system that also works on the night shift.

**Pollution.** Particulate monitors at the crusher, the transfer points, the stockyard and the boundary, plus wind speed and direction at the pit rim, reporting continuously — PM2.5, PM10, TSP, and the wind field that decides where they go.

**Dust control.** The state of every suppression device: running or stopped, water pressure, flow, nozzle status, fault alarms. A device that cannot report its own state is a device that will be discovered to have been broken for three weeks.

**The record.** Data retained, so that the sequence — threshold exceeded, suppression started, concentration fell, suppression stopped — can be shown to a regulator or a client, and so that the control logic can be tuned against measured performance rather than against a design assumption.

This loop already exists in Chinese mines, largely underground: ground-level centralised dust control platforms that collect from monitoring points around the clock, raise an audible and visual alarm on exceedance, and remotely start, stop and adjust every suppression device on the section, running unattended; AI video analytics on pan-tilt cameras that read visibility in the dust itself and trigger spray on that basis; infrared and belt interlocks that open a water curtain when the belt turns and close it when a vehicle passes. The open-pit chain — crusher, transfer, belt, yard — is where the gap is largest, and it is the part of the mine that most specifications still treat as a set of unrelated fittings.

For anyone specifying equipment, this reframes the question. A dust suppression system bought as an unconnected appliance — a pump, a timer and a manual switch — will be operated like one, and will be found to have been idle for most of the year. The version that survives is specified as an **instrumented terminal of the control room**: it accepts a start signal from the process, it accepts a threshold from the monitor, it reports its own state, and it can be scheduled and interrogated remotely. The equipment is not harder to build that way. It is only harder to specify that way — and that is a decision taken at the beginning of a project, not at the end.

## The Sequence That Holds Up on Site

**First, measure rather than estimate.** A source inventory built on site walks, dust readings over time, and wind data will contradict the intuition of everyone in the room, including the people who were right about the last project. It needs to establish which sources dominate by mass, which dominate by size fraction, and when each of them actually occurs.

**Second, before designing anything, understand the pit.** An open-pit mine is a vessel with its own internal airflow. Benches, high walls and pit depth change wind speed and wind direction inside the excavation, and they create recirculation that can hold a plume in the pit rather than releasing it. Shallow pits ventilate differently from deep ones, and dust tends to accumulate on the downwind side. Designs that ignore geometry end up placing equipment where the air moves dust away from it.

**Third, match method to source** using the logic in the previous section, and accept that a site will need two or three of the four methods working together.

**Fourth, automate the trigger logic.** Continuous sources and mobile sources cannot be served by a schedule. Start signals from conveyors and crushers, vehicle detection on approach, particulate readings over threshold, wind speed and direction at the pit rim — these are what make a system operate in month six rather than in week one. The interlock logic is where the design earns its cost, and it should be written down before it is programmed. Then connect it to the room the fleet already reports to: on a mine that has moved to autonomous haulage, a suppression device that cannot be seen from the control centre is a device nobody owns.

**Fifth, verify with the same instrument you used at the start.** A dust programme that cannot show its own effect on the measurement that triggered it will not survive its second budget review.

## Details That Get Missed

A short list, drawn from the failure modes that recur across mine sites rather than from a catalogue.

**Drilling.** Wet drilling — water injected through the rod to the hole bottom, so the cuttings form a slurry rather than a dust plume — is the standard answer, and where a drill carries its own collector, the two are complementary rather than alternatives. On a drill rig, the dust problem is at the collar, a metre or two from the operator.

**Blasting.** Pre-wetting and water stemming reduce what can be released; immediate post-blast suppression reduces what travels. Both are needed, and neither substitutes for keeping people out of the cloud and away from the gases that come with it.

**Loading points and shovels.** The suppressing device has to travel with the machine or be aimed at it. A cannon on a fixed tower can cover a bench; it cannot follow a shovel along the face.

**Dump points.** Drop height and tip geometry determine the plume more than the wind does. Suppression at the tip, aimed at the fall of material, is more effective than suppression applied after the material has settled.

**Crusher and conveyor transfers.** Enclose first, suppress inside the enclosure, and keep the water out of any stream that cannot accept moisture. Downstream of a crusher in a dry processing line, water is a product quality problem, not a dust solution.

**Stockpiles.** Long slopes, exposed surfaces and self-heating risk on coal and sulphide material. Surface sealing and progressive rehabilitation reduce the area that needs treating at all.

**Water quality and nozzles.** Mine water is often hard, turbid and aggressive. Nozzle selection, filtration and a cleaning routine decide whether the system still works in year three.

**Winter.** Freezing is a pressure event, not a slow process, and its risk window opens well above zero degrees.

**Power and access on benches.** A method that cannot be reached by a maintenance vehicle, or that depends on a temporary cable run, will not be maintained. Access is part of the design.

## Questions Worth Asking Before You Buy

None of these require a specification sheet, and most suppliers can answer all seven without hesitating.

**Which source is this machine for?** A single answer — not "general dust control on the site".

**What is the control window?** Instantaneous, intermittent or continuous. If the answer is continuous, the next question is what starts and stops the machine.

**What happens in the four minutes after a blast?**

**Which size fraction is the target, and what droplet size reaches it?**

**Who maintains it, from where, and with what access?**

**What does it consume per operating hour — water, power, consumables and labour — and what does that cost over ten years?**

**Does it report its own state to your control room, and over what protocol?** If the answer is a phone call and a clipboard, the mine will eventually be fully automated on the haul side and manual on the dust side. Those two halves should reach the same platform.

The last question is the one that reframes the discussion. Purchase price is a fraction of the ten-year cost, and the fractions that dominate it are water, power, maintenance and downtime. A cheaper machine that cannot hold its droplet spectrum, or that needs a person to press a button every time the wind changes, is not cheaper.

## What Actually Changes the Number at the Fence

An open-pit mine dust programme succeeds or fails on a sequence that has little to do with equipment brands. The site establishes what is really being emitted and when. It designs the pit's own airflow into the solution and stops pretending the atmosphere outside the fence is the only atmosphere that matters. It puts the largest share of the effort where the largest share of the mass is, which is the road, and it stops treating the road as a spraying problem when it is also a speed, load and silt problem. It automates the sources that never take a break. And it measures the result against the same standard that triggered the investment.

Everything else — the number of cannons, the throw distance on the brochure, the size of the water cart — is downstream of those decisions. Most sites that struggle with open-pit dust have made reasonable equipment choices on top of a source inventory nobody wrote down. That is a fixable problem, and the fix costs nothing but a week of measurement.
